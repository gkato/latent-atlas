Behind acronyms like SwiGLU, RMSNorm, rms_norm_eps and output_gate_type: sigmoid there is a simple piece: a neuron that multiplies numbers by weights, adds everything up and decides how much of the result to let through. Put millions of them in layers, connect the layers with shortcuts and normalize the numbers along the way, and you have the skeleton of any LLM, from GLM-5.3-Flash to DeepSeek-V4-Flash. In this chapter you will build that piece from scratch, watch the plane fold on screen, train a network live in the browser and understand why the recipes copy every ε and every activation function from the checkpoint so carefully.

The neuron: weigh, sum, decide

Imagine a party bouncer with a clipboard. For each guest he looks at a few features (\(x_1\): on the list? \(x_2\): showed up in flip-flops?), gives each one a weight (being on the list counts a lot, in favor; flip-flops count a little, against), adds everything up, subtracts his own bad mood and decides. That bouncer is an artificial neuron. The entire math fits on one line:

\[ z = \sum_{i=1}^{n} w_i\,x_i + b = \mathbf w^\top \mathbf x + b, \qquad a = f(z) \]
  • \(\mathbf x \in \mathbb R^n\): the inputs (the features the neuron looks at)
  • \(\mathbf w \in \mathbb R^n\): the weights, learned during training. The sign says whether the input counts in favor (+) or against (−), and the magnitude says how much
  • \(b\): the bias, a threshold that shifts the decision
  • \(z\): the pre-activation, the raw “score”
  • \(f\): the activation function, which turns the score into a response (step, sigmoid, ReLU…)
  • \(a\): the output, or activation, of the neuron

Work one out by hand. With \(\mathbf x = (2, -1)\), \(\mathbf w = (0.5,\ 1.5)\) and \(b = 0.2\): \(z = 0.5\cdot 2 + 1.5\cdot(-1) + 0.2 = -0.3\). If \(f\) is ReLU, the output is \(\max(0, -0.3) = 0\): the neuron stays silent. If \(f\) is the sigmoid, the output is \(1/(1+e^{0.3}) \approx 0.4256\), a “maybe, leaning toward no”.

What a neuron sees: a line

The set of points where \(\mathbf w^\top \mathbf x + b = 0\) is a line in the plane (in \(n\) dimensions, a hyperplane). The vector \(\mathbf w\) is perpendicular to that line and points toward the side where \(z\) grows. The signed distance from a point to the line is \(z/\lVert\mathbf w\rVert\); in other words, \(z\) is that distance multiplied by \(\lVert\mathbf w\rVert\) (positive on the side \(\mathbf w\) points to). A neuron answers two questions: which side of the line the point is on, and how far away.

In the figure below, each point in the plane is a guest. The background shows the neuron's response \(a = f(z)\) at each position, and the diamond is a guest wandering around the plane. On the left you see the same neuron drawn as a graph: the inputs flow through the weights to the summation node and then through the activation.

Notice two things. First, changing \(b\) only slides the line, without rotating it. What rotates it is the ratio between \(w_1\) and \(w_2\). Second, with the default controls you can get all 16 points right, because the two clouds are separated by a line. If the pink points sat in two opposite corners (the XOR pattern), no line would do it.

Deep dive · a bit of history

Rosenblatt's perceptron (1958) was exactly this neuron with a step as its activation. In 1969, Minsky and Papert showed that a single neuron cannot solve XOR, and the field cooled off for years. The way out, known ever since, is to stack neurons in layers and train them all together. What was missing was an efficient way to train them, which only caught on in the 1980s with backpropagation (section 6).

In an LLM, a neuron's input is the entire vector that represents a token. In GLM-5.3-Flash and DeepSeek-V4-Flash that vector has 4,096 numbers (hidden_size); in Qwen3.8-Flash-Next, 2,560. A single neuron in the intermediate layer of a GLM expert (one of the small FFNs in an MoE layer, chapter 11) therefore has 4,096 weights, and the expert has 2,048 of those neurons. Nobody programs them one by one: they come packed in a matrix.

The linear layer: a matrix that bends space

Put \(m\) neurons side by side, all looking at the same input \(\mathbf x\). Each one has its own weight vector. Stack those vectors as the rows of a matrix and you have a linear layer:

\[ \mathbf y = W\mathbf x + \mathbf b, \qquad W \in \mathbb R^{m\times n},\quad y_i = \sum_{j=1}^{n} W_{ij}\,x_j + b_i \]
  • \(n\): input width (in_features); \(m\): output width (out_features), that is, the number of neurons
  • row \(i\) of \(W\): the weights of neuron \(i\); column \(j\) of \(W\): where input \(j\) “pushes” the output
  • \(\mathbf b \in \mathbb R^m\): one bias per neuron (many LLMs don't use it)
  • cost: \(m\cdot n\) multiplications and additions per token, or \(2mn\) FLOPs

Checkpoints store \(W\) in this [out, in] convention, the same one PyTorch's nn.Linear uses. When the GLM inventory says that an expert's gate_proj has shape [2048, 4096], it is saying: 2,048 neurons, each with 4,096 weights, 8.4 million numbers.

The geometry: what a matrix does to the plane

A \(2\times 2\) matrix transforms the plane in a very specific way: the origin stays put, lines stay lines, and parallel lines stay parallel and evenly spaced. The trick to reading any matrix is to look at its columns: the first column is where the vector \(\hat{\mathbf e}_1 = (1,0)\) goes, the second is where \(\hat{\mathbf e}_2 = (0,1)\) goes. Everything else follows by linearity. The bias \(\mathbf b\) shifts everything as a block (the transformation becomes affine).

Three observations that will come back many times along the trail:

  1. The determinant measures what is lost. With \(\det W = 0\) the matrix has rank 1: two dimensions go in, one comes out, and the information in the other is lost for good. Low-rank matrices are a deliberate way to compress, and they will reappear in MLA (chapter 12) and in the low-rank gates of GLM and Qwen.
  2. Reflecting flips the letter. The mirrored “F” shows \(\det W < 0\): the orientation of the plane was turned inside out.
  3. ReLU folds. After the matrix, ReLU zeroes the negative coordinates. Everything that was outside the first quadrant is squashed against the axes. It is the first operation in the figure that is not linear.

Why stacking linear layers doesn't help

Apply two linear layers in sequence: \(W_2(W_1\mathbf x + \mathbf b_1) + \mathbf b_2 = (W_2W_1)\,\mathbf x + (W_2\mathbf b_1 + \mathbf b_2)\). The result is another affine transformation, with matrix \(W_2W_1\). A hundred linear layers in a row are equivalent to a single one. The network would be stuck with transformations that keep lines straight, and none of them separates XOR. That is why every neural network interleaves its matrices with nonlinear functions.

Why this matters if you serve models

Almost all of an LLM's parameters live in linear layers. Serving the model is, to a large extent, reading those matrices from memory and multiplying them by vectors. Quantizing is storing \(W\) with fewer bits (Part IV). Tensor parallelism is splitting \(W\) by rows or columns across GPUs (chapter 38). Every optimization in the recipes starts from understanding that “layer” means “matrix”.

A bookkeeping detail: a linear layer has \(m\cdot n\) weights plus \(m\) biases. The decoders of the three models skip the bias in the attention projections (attention_bias: false in GLM and Qwen), which saves parameters and simplifies quantization. The vision towers (ViT, the Transformer that reads the image, chapter 17) still use bias: the MLPs of Qwen's ViT, the SwiGLU MLP of GLM's ViT and DeepSeek's Aligner have bias.

Activation functions: the zoo of folds

The activation function is what gives the network the power to fold space. A good activation has to be cheap, have a derivative that is easy to compute (training depends on it, section 6) and not saturate too much: if the curve goes flat, the derivative goes to zero and learning stops. Each family strikes this balance in its own way, and the three recipes use almost all of them, each in a different role.

The six you need to know

Sigmoid \(\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}\)
Squashes any number into \((0, 1)\). The derivative \(\sigma(1-\sigma)\) never exceeds 0.25, so dozens of sigmoids in series multiply the gradient by small numbers until it vanishes. It fell out of use as a hidden-layer activation, but became the gate function par excellence: a number between 0 and 1 is exactly “how much to let through”.
tanh \(\tanh(z) = 2\sigma(2z) - 1\)
A sigmoid stretched to \((-1, 1)\) and centered at zero. It reigned in recurrent networks (LSTM). Today it shows up as a component of other functions: in the fast GELU approximation and in the logit softcap \(c\cdot\tanh(z/c)\).
ReLU \(\max(0, z)\)
The cheapest one: a single comparison. Derivative 1 for \(z>0\) and 0 for \(z<0\), with no saturation on the positive side. It leaves many neurons at exactly zero (sparse activations). The risk is the dead neuron: if \(z\) stays negative for every input, the gradient is zero forever.
Softplus \(\ln(1+e^{z})\)
A smoothed ReLU: always positive, with derivative \(\sigma(z)\). It is the choice when you need a strictly positive number that comes out of an unconstrained computation, such as a decay rate.
GELU \(z\,\Phi(z)\)
\(\Phi\) is the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal. GELU multiplies \(z\) by the probability that a standard normal falls below \(z\), a smooth “probabilistic ReLU” with a small negative dip (minimum ≈ −0.17). Popularized by BERT and GPT-2. There is a tanh approximation, \(\tfrac{z}{2}\big(1+\tanh(\sqrt{2/\pi}\,(z + 0.044715\,z^3))\big)\), which PyTorch calls gelu_pytorch_tanh.
SiLU / Swish \(z\,\sigma(z)\)
The input multiplied by its own sigmoid: the neuron “authorizes itself” to pass. Smooth, non-monotonic (minimum ≈ −0.278 at \(z\approx -1.278\)) and unbounded above. It is the activation the three LLMs declare in config.json (hidden_act: silu), because it is the “gate” half of SwiGLU (section 5).

Pitfall · the activation is part of the checkpoint

The weights were trained together with a specific activation. Swapping exact GELU for GELU-tanh changes each output by up to ~5×10⁻⁴ (the figure computes it). That looks small, but the error repeats in every neuron of every layer and accumulates. This is the kind of deviation that breaks nothing visibly and only shows up when you measure the divergence between distributions (KLD, chapter 25). When porting a model, copy hidden_act and the exact variants.

MLP: stacking folds until any function fits

An MLP (multilayer perceptron) alternates matrices and activations. The version with a single hidden layer is already powerful:

\[ \mathbf y = W_2\, f(W_1\mathbf x + \mathbf b_1) + \mathbf b_2 \]
  • \(W_1 \in \mathbb R^{h\times n}\): the hidden layer, with \(h\) neurons (the width)
  • \(f\): the activation, applied elementwise
  • \(W_2 \in \mathbb R^{m\times h}\): the output layer, which combines the responses of the \(h\) neurons

The universal approximation theorem (Cybenko, 1989, for sigmoids; Hornik, 1991; Leshno and colleagues, 1993, for any non-polynomial activation) guarantees that such an MLP, with enough neurons, approximates any continuous function on a bounded domain to whatever precision you want. The intuition is constructive and fits in a figure. Each ReLU neuron \(\mathrm{ReLU}(x - k_i)\) is a hinge: zero up to \(k_i\) and a ramp after that. By adding hinges with chosen slopes, you can draw any polyline:

\[ g(x) = f(k_0) + \sum_{i=0}^{N-1} c_i\,\mathrm{ReLU}(x - k_i), \qquad c_i = s_i - s_{i-1} \]
  • \(k_i\): the fold points (here, evenly spaced)
  • \(s_i\): the desired slope on segment \(i\) (with \(s_{-1} = 0\)); \(c_i\) is how much the slope changes at fold \(i\), the output weight of neuron \(i\)

With sigmoids the reasoning is similar: a very steep sigmoid is a step, and a sum of steps draws a staircase that follows the curve. Notice that, for the same \(N\), hinges win, because they interpolate with straight segments instead of plateaus.

Deep dive · what the theorem doesn't say

The theorem guarantees that the weights exist. It doesn't say how to find them or how many neurons are needed, and for complicated functions the required width can grow exponentially with the dimension. In practice, depth is more economical: each layer folds the result of the previous folds, and the number of linear regions of a ReLU network can grow exponentially with the number of layers (Montúfar and colleagues, 2014). That is why LLMs have dozens of layers, not one gigantic hidden layer.

Every Transformer FFN is an MLP. A useful reading (Geva and colleagues, 2021) is to treat it as a key-value memory: each row of \(W_1\) is a “key” that detects a pattern in the token's vector, the activation decides whether the pattern is present, and the corresponding column of \(W_2\) is the “value” the neuron writes back. In the recipes there are classic MLPs, with two matrices and bias, in the vision towers: the Aligner of DeepSeek-V4-Flash-Vision-Exp is \(9216 \to 4096 \to 4096\) with GELU, and the MLP of each block of Qwen's ViT is \(1152 \to 4304 \to 1152\) with GELU-tanh. In the decoder, the FFNs use a gated variant, SwiGLU, which is the subject of the next section.

Multiplicative gates: how much to let through

So far neurons only add signals. A gate (gating) introduces a multiplication between signals: one branch computes the content \(\mathbf u\), another computes an opening \(\mathbf g\) between 0 and 1, and the output is the elementwise product.

\[ \mathbf y = \sigma(W_g\mathbf x) \odot (W_u\mathbf x) \]
  • \(W_u\mathbf x\): the content, “what I want to say”
  • \(\sigma(W_g\mathbf x) \in (0,1)^m\): how open each faucet is, computed from the data itself
  • \(\odot\): elementwise (Hadamard) product. Coordinate \(i\) of the output is \(g_i\cdot u_i\)
Analogy · the mixing desk

Picture a mixing desk with one fader per channel. The \(\mathbf u\) branch is the music on each channel; the \(\mathbf g\) branch is the position of the faders. The difference from an ordinary desk is that here the music itself moves the faders: each token chooses, for itself, which channels go up and which go down.

Why multiply, instead of just adding? There are four reasons, and all of them show up in the recipes:

  1. Data-dependent decisions. The same layer lets different things through for different tokens, without needing different weights.
  2. Magnitude under control. With \(\sigma\in(0,1)\) the output is never larger than the content. A gate never amplifies, and that helps numerical stability.
  3. Second-order interactions. The product of two linear functions of \(\mathbf x\) is quadratic in \(\mathbf x\). A gated layer represents, with few parameters, relations of the “A and B” kind.
  4. Paths for the gradient. When the gate is open, the signal (and the gradient) goes through almost intact. That is the idea behind the LSTM (1997) and highway networks (2015), and it is a close relative of the residual connection (section 9).

The GLU family, and why SwiGLU won

Dauphin and colleagues (2017) called \(\sigma(W_g\mathbf x)\odot W_u\mathbf x\) a GLU (gated linear unit). Shazeer (2020) tried replacing the gate's sigmoid with other activations inside the Transformer FFN: with GELU it became GeGLU, with SiLU it became SwiGLU, and both improved quality over the ReLU FFN. The FFN now has three matrices: gate_proj (\(W_g\)), up_proj (\(W_u\)) and down_proj, which goes back to the model width:

\[ \mathrm{FFN}_{\text{SwiGLU}}(\mathbf x) = W_{\text{down}}\big(\mathrm{SiLU}(W_{\text{gate}}\mathbf x)\odot W_{\text{up}}\mathbf x\big) \]
  • \(\mathrm{SiLU}(z) = z\,\sigma(z)\): the gate branch. Since it isn't bounded to \((0,1)\), SwiGLU is not a pure faucet but a smooth multiplicative interaction: \(z_g\,\sigma(z_g)\,z_u\)
  • this is the SwiGLU of every FFN, expert and shared expert in the three models

Since the product \(z_g\cdot z_u\) has no ceiling, GLM and DeepSeek also limit both branches before multiplying (swiglu_limit: 10.0). This clamp and SwiGLU's parameter count are covered in detail in chapter 7.

Why this matters for inference

In Qwen's FP8-dense checkpoint, the 591 tensors converted to FP8 had a relative RMSE between 0.0240 and 0.0268 (median 0.0262). The worst case was the block_inject_weight tensors of the Gated Residual write, only slightly above the median, and their impact on GPU was not measured. In other words, these numbers do not show that gates are especially fragile. Even so, the recipe keeps the router, the shared_expert_gate, GDN's in_proj_a/b projections and the norms in BF16. They are small tensors, so quantizing them would save almost nothing, and their output multiplies or selects entire paths. Interpretation (this is the recipe's practice, not a measured result): quantize the big matrices and protect the small gates.

The computational graph and the chain rule

Training means adjusting the weights to decrease a loss \(L\) (chapter 1). For that we need the gradient: how much \(L\) changes when each weight changes a tiny bit. With billions of weights, computing that weight by weight would be impossible. What makes training feasible is looking at the network as a computational graph: a chain of simple operations (multiply, add, activate), in which each node knows how to compute its own local derivative.

The chain rule says how to put the local derivatives together: if \(L\) depends on \(a\), which depends on \(z\), which depends on \(w\), then

\[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial w} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial a}\cdot\frac{\partial a}{\partial z}\cdot\frac{\partial z}{\partial w} \]
  • each factor is a local derivative, computed by a single node of the graph
  • when a variable feeds several paths, the contributions from each path are added

The figure walks through a sigmoid neuron with squared loss, \(L = \tfrac12(\sigma(wx+b) - y)^2\), with \(x = 1.5\), \(w = 0.8\), \(b = -0.5\) and target \(y = 1\). On the way in (forward), values flow from left to right. On the way back (backward), gradients flow from the loss to the weights, and each node multiplies what it receives by its local derivative.

With the default values: \(z = 0.7\), \(a = \sigma(0.7) \approx 0.6682\) and \(L \approx 0.0550\). Going back: \(\partial L/\partial a = a - y \approx -0.3318\); the sigmoid contributes \(a(1-a) \approx 0.2217\), so \(\partial L/\partial z \approx -0.0736\); and since \(z = wx + b\), \(\partial L/\partial w = x\cdot\partial L/\partial z \approx -0.1104\) and \(\partial L/\partial b \approx -0.0736\). A negative gradient means “increase \(w\)”. With step size \(\eta = 1\), \(w\) goes to 0.910, \(b\) to −0.426, and the loss drops to ≈ 0.0395.

Backpropagation in matrices

In a real network, each node is a whole layer and the local derivatives are matrices, the Jacobians. For a layer \(\mathbf z = W\mathbf a_{\text{prev}} + \mathbf b\), \(\mathbf a = f(\mathbf z)\), backpropagation does three computations:

\[ \boldsymbol\delta = f'(\mathbf z)\odot\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf a}, \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial W} = \boldsymbol\delta\,\mathbf a_{\text{prev}}^{\top}, \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial \mathbf a_{\text{prev}}} = W^{\top}\boldsymbol\delta \]
  • \(\boldsymbol\delta\): the gradient at the pre-activation, the layer's “error signal”
  • \(\boldsymbol\delta\,\mathbf a_{\text{prev}}^{\top}\): an outer product; each weight \(W_{ij}\) gets “error of neuron \(i\)” × “input \(j\)”
  • \(W^\top\boldsymbol\delta\): the error sent back to the previous layer, through the transpose of the same matrix

Watch the three computations happen in a small layer, with 3 neurons and 4 inputs (\(W\) is \(3\times4\)). The first one is elementwise. The second, the outer product, turns a column of 3 numbers and a row of 4 into a \(3\times4\) matrix, the same size as \(W\), because each weight needs its own gradient. The third rotates \(W\) (the transpose swaps rows and columns) and multiplies it by \(\boldsymbol\delta\).

With a batch of tokens, the columns \(\boldsymbol\delta\) and \(\mathbf a_{\text{prev}}\) become matrices (one column per token), and computations (2) and (3) become ordinary matrix multiplications. The backward pass therefore costs about twice the forward: two multiplications the size of \(W\) for each one in the forward.

Backprop is a case of reverse-mode automatic differentiation: a single backward pass, which costs on the order of two forward passes, delivers the gradient with respect to all parameters at once. The naive alternative, the finite difference, would need at least one extra forward pass per parameter. For the 321,323,031,390 parameters of GLM-5.3-Flash, that would be 321 billion passes for a single training step.

Why this matters, if the recipes only do inference

Three reasons. (1) Memory: the backward pass needs every layer's activations stored since the forward pass; that is why training costs much more memory than inference. At inference time, engines like vLLM run only the forward pass, with autograd turned off, and each layer discards its input as soon as it produces its output. What persists between tokens is the KV cache or the recurrent state (chapter 10). (2) Quantization tools use information derived from training, such as approximations of the loss Hessian (chapter 22), and quantization-aware training (QAT) teaches the model to tolerate the error: that is the case with DeepSeek-V4's FP8 KV (chapter 23). (3) Everything the gradient shaped (activation, ε, gates, scales) has to be reproduced faithfully at inference, or else the model being served is a different model.

Depth, width and the parameter count

The width of a network is the number of neurons per layer, and the depth is the number of layers. An MLP with widths \(n_0 \to n_1 \to \dots \to n_L\) has

\[ P = \sum_{\ell=1}^{L} \big(n_{\ell-1}\,n_\ell + n_\ell\big) \]
  • \(n_{\ell-1}n_\ell\): the weights of layer \(\ell\)'s matrix
  • \(n_\ell\): the biases (add zero if the layer has no bias)

Do the math for DeepSeek's Aligner, a \(9216 \to 4096 \to 4096\) MLP with bias: \(9216\cdot4096 + 4096 + 4096\cdot4096 + 4096 = 54{,}534{,}144\) parameters, the “≈ 54.5 M” in the inventory. The MLP of one block of Qwen's ViT (\(1152\to4304\to1152\), with bias) comes to 9,921,872, and its 27 blocks add up to 267,890,544 in MLP alone. In SwiGLU without bias there are three matrices, \(3\,d\,d_{ff}\): a GLM or DeepSeek expert (\(4096\to2048\)) has 25,165,824 parameters, a Qwen expert (\(2560\to640\)) has 4,915,200, and the dense FFN in GLM's layers 0–2 (\(4096\to12288\)) has 150,994,944.

With tanh the boundary is smooth; with ReLU, polygonal, made of straight pieces, as in section 4. Increasing depth increases the possible folds, but it also makes training more delicate. What depth and width cost at inference

Width

More neurons per layer means bigger matrices. On a GPU that work is parallel and efficient, but during decode every weight has to be read from memory at every step. The three models are narrow for their size (hidden_size 4096, 2560 and 4096) and hide their capacity in the experts: Qwen has a width of only 2,560 for 125 B parameters.

Depth

Layers are sequential: layer \(\ell+1\) waits for layer \(\ell\) to finish, so depth goes straight into each token's latency (45 layers in GLM, 48 in Qwen, 43 in DeepSeek). With tensor parallelism, each layer also pays for communication between GPUs, so depth also multiplies network latency (chapter 38).

Normalization: LayerNorm and RMSNorm

In a deep network, the scale of the numbers changes from layer to layer and from token to token. A layer that receives inputs with magnitude 0.01 on one token and 100 on the next has to serve both at the same time, and training becomes unstable. Normalization solves this by rescaling each token's vector to a standard magnitude before handing it to the next layer. The two versions that matter are these:

\[ \mathrm{LayerNorm}(\mathbf x) = \boldsymbol\gamma\odot\frac{\mathbf x - \mu}{\sqrt{\sigma^2 + \varepsilon}} + \boldsymbol\beta \qquad\qquad \mathrm{RMSNorm}(\mathbf x) = \boldsymbol\gamma\odot\frac{\mathbf x}{\sqrt{\tfrac1d\sum_i x_i^2 + \varepsilon}} \]
  • \(\mathbf x\in\mathbb R^d\): the vector of one token (normalization is done along the features, token by token)
  • \(\mu = \tfrac1d\sum_i x_i\), \(\sigma^2 = \tfrac1d\sum_i (x_i-\mu)^2\): mean and variance of the vector
  • \(\boldsymbol\gamma\) (gain) and \(\boldsymbol\beta\) (bias): learned vectors of size \(d\), which give the network back the freedom to choose the scale of each feature
  • \(\varepsilon\): the epsilon, a small number that avoids dividing by zero (in the config: rms_norm_eps)

LayerNorm (Ba, Kiros and Hinton, 2016) centers and rescales. RMSNorm (Zhang and Sennrich, 2019) skips the centering and the bias and divides only by the root mean square. It is cheaper (one reduction instead of two) and, in practice, works just as well. The decoders of the three models use RMSNorm; the vision towers vary (Qwen's ViT uses LayerNorm with bias; GLM's and DeepSeek's use RMSNorm).

The central property is scale invariance: for any \(\alpha > 0\), \(\mathrm{RMSNorm}(\alpha\mathbf x) = \mathrm{RMSNorm}(\mathbf x)\), as long as \(\varepsilon\) is negligible next to \(\mathrm{RMS}(\mathbf x)^2\). It doesn't matter whether the previous layer delivered a huge vector or a tiny one: the next layer always receives something with RMS ≈ 1 (times \(\boldsymbol\gamma\)).

ε is not a detail

ε is added to the mean of the squares, so it takes over when \(\mathrm{RMS}(\mathbf x) \lesssim \sqrt\varepsilon\). With \(\varepsilon = 10^{-5}\) that happens below RMS ≈ 0.0032; with \(10^{-6}\), below 0.001; with \(10^{-20}\), only below \(10^{-10}\). In that region the output no longer goes to RMS 1 and stays at \(\approx \mathbf x/\sqrt\varepsilon\), proportional to the input: the norm stops amplifying tiny noise all the way up to the standard scale. With an input RMS of \(10^{-4}\) and \(\varepsilon = 10^{-5}\), the output has RMS ≈ 0.0316 instead of 1 (still larger than the input, just far from 1). An ε different from the one used in training changes the output precisely on the tokens with small vectors, and does so systematically.

Fix in vLLMvision: rms_norm_eps 1e-5 → 1e-6

GLM-5.3-Flash's text stack uses rms_norm_eps: 1e-5. The checkpoint declares the same value for the vision tower, but the tower was trained with 1e-6. The vLLM code used by the recipe forces self.rms_norm_eps = 1e-6 for vision, with a comment saying that serving with 1e-5 “makes RMSNorm drift and produces repetitive/degraded image descriptions”. A single wrong number in the config ruins an entire modality without raising a single error.

Configrms_norm_eps: 1e-20 (Vision-Exp) · 1e-06 (0731)

The Vision-Exp checkpoint, the one in the current lane, ships ε = 1e-20 in every RMSNorm of the text trunk and in the mHC rsqrt; 0731 ships 1e-6. The vision tower doesn't follow this field: the ViT's RMSNorm (patches/vision_exp/vision.py) uses the default eps=1e-6. With 1e-20, ε only prevents an exact division by zero. Interpretation: the norm is left with practically no floor. The repository doesn't measure the impact of this difference. Note that 1e-20 exists in FP32 and in BF16, but becomes zero in FP16. Interpretation: that is a good reason for the norm to compute in FP32.

Implementation: FP32 inside and the “1 + w” gain

The snippet below (simplified from files/ple_layer_patched.py in the Qwen recipe) is the grouped RMSNorm of the N-gram embedding. It packs three implementation decisions you will find in every modern model:

class Qwen3_8FlashNextPLEGroupedNorm(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, hidden_size, eps, group_size, dtype):
        super().__init__()
        self.eps, self.group_size = eps, group_size
        self.weight = nn.Parameter(torch.zeros(hidden_size, dtype=dtype))  # w starts at ZERO

    def forward(self, hidden_states):
        input_dtype = hidden_states.dtype
        hidden_states = hidden_states.float()                              # (1) compute in FP32
        grouped = hidden_states.unflatten(-1, (-1, self.group_size))       # (2) 10240 → 4 × 2560
        variance = grouped.square().mean(dim=-1, keepdim=True)
        normalized = (grouped * torch.rsqrt(variance + self.eps)).flatten(-2)
        return (normalized * (1.0 + self.weight.float())).to(input_dtype)  # (3) gain = 1 + w
  1. FP32 inside. The sum of 2,560 squares in BF16 (relative rounding error ≈ 3.9×10⁻³ per operation) would lose precision, and an ε like 1e-20 would become zero in FP16. The norm upcasts to FP32, computes, and casts back to the original dtype. Compare with the RMSNorm of DeepSeek's ViT (patches/vision_exp/vision.py), which does the same x.float() … .to(dtype).
  2. Groups. With group_size = 2560, the vector of 10,240 numbers (the 4 branches of the Gated Residual) is normalized branch by branch, the grouped RMSNorm. Interpretation: if there were a single norm, a large-scale branch would dictate the divisor for the other three. Normalizing per group lets each branch keep its own scale.
  3. Zero-centered gain. The weight is initialized at 0 and applied as \(1 + w\), the zero-centered gain. In DeepSeek, the gain is the weight itself, initialized at 1 (torch.ones). Mathematically the two are equivalent, but here is an interpretation: with weight decay pulling \(w\) toward 0, what gets preserved is a gain of 1 (identity), not a gain of 0 (which would erase the signal).
Pitfall · “w” or “1 + w”?

A loader that applies Qwen's weight as a direct gain, forgetting the “1 +”, multiplies the output of every norm by numbers close to zero, and the model generates garbage. The opposite (adding 1 to a gain that is already the weight itself, as in DeepSeek) doubles the scale. When porting a model, read the norm's forward in the reference code: the tensor name (*.weight) doesn't tell you which convention applies.

Where the norms live in the three models

GLM-5.3-Flash

RMSNorm before each sublayer (input_layernorm, post_attention_layernorm) and at the end (model.norm); inside MLA, q_a_layernorm [1536] and kv_a_layernorm [512] normalize the latents; the indexer's k_norm is a LayerNorm with bias [128], ε 1e-6; KDA ends with RMSNorm × sigmoid gate; KDA's q and k go through L2 normalization. Drafters: the MTP (layer 45) normalizes the next token's embedding and the target's state with enorm and hnorm [4096] before eh_proj; DFlash2 normalizes the target's features with RMSNorm after fc, has k_norm (QK-Norm) and uses rms_norm_eps 1e-5.

Qwen3.8-Flash-Next

There is no explicit pre-norm or final norm: the Gated Residual read normalizes each branch (grouped, 1 + w) and the hyper_connection_mixer replaces model.norm. Per-head QK-Norm (q_norm, k_norm [256]) in attention; gated RMSNorm [128] at the GDN output; rms_norm_eps = 1e-6. Drafter: the MTP normalizes its two inputs before joining them, pre_fc_norm_embedding [2560] (the next token's embedding) and pre_fc_norm_hidden [10240] (the 4 branches of the target's state).

DeepSeek-V4-Flash

RMSNorm before attention and before the FFN (attn_norm, ffn_norm), on the query latent (q_norm [1024]) and on the KV (kv_norm [512]); each query head is additionally divided by its own RMS, with no weight. The report says it doesn't need QK-Clip precisely because it normalizes queries and KV. Drafter: DSpark repeats the target's family of norms (q_norm, kv_norm, attn_norm, ffn_norm, plus main_norm at the input and a final norm) and also divides Q by each head's RMS, with no weight.

In the recipe · norms don't get quantized

A gain vector has only \(d\) numbers, and it multiplies everything that passes through it. Quantizing it would save almost nothing and risk a lot. Qwen's FP8-dense converter keeps the norms in BF16, and so does the GLM recipe. In vLLM, the norm is usually fused with the residual add (fused_add_rms_norm): one kernel reads the residual once, adds, normalizes and writes (chapter 28).

Deep dive · GatedNorm

Norm and gate can be combined: \(\mathrm{GatedNorm}(\mathbf u) = \mathrm{RMSNorm}(\mathbf u)\odot\sigma(W_2\,\mathrm{SiLU}(W_1\,\mathrm{RMSNorm}(\mathbf u)))\). That is exactly the Gated Residual read in Qwen. According to the tech report, GatedNorm alone cuts loss spikes from 32.0 to 3.2 per 10,000 steps, in a stress run at 4× the optimal LR, and removes activation outliers. Chapter 15 develops this further.

Residual connections: the gradient's express lane

Go back to the chain rule. In a “plain” network of \(L\) layers, \(\mathbf h_{\ell+1} = F_\ell(\mathbf h_\ell)\), the gradient that reaches the first layer is a product of \(L\) Jacobians:

\[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf h_0} = J_0^\top J_1^\top\cdots J_{L-1}^\top\,\frac{\partial L}{\partial\mathbf h_L}, \qquad J_\ell = \frac{\partial F_\ell}{\partial\mathbf h_\ell} \]
  • if each \(J_\ell\) shrinks vectors by a factor of 0.9, after 64 layers what's left is \(0.9^{64}\approx 0.001\): the vanishing gradient
  • if each one stretches them by 1.1, what's left is \(1.1^{64}\approx 445\): the exploding gradient

It's a game of telephone: each layer repeats the message slightly distorted, and after many layers the start of the line receives nothing useful. In 2015, He and colleagues observed that deeper plain networks trained worse than shallower ones (a 56-layer network had a higher training error than a 20-layer one). The solution was the residual connection: instead of replacing the vector, each layer adds a correction to it.

\[ \mathbf h_{\ell+1} = \mathbf h_\ell + F_\ell(\mathbf h_\ell) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{\partial\mathbf h_{\ell+1}}{\partial\mathbf h_\ell} = I + \frac{\partial F_\ell}{\partial\mathbf h_\ell} \]
  • \(I\): the identity, a path through which the gradient passes without being multiplied by anything
  • unrolling: \(\mathbf h_L = \mathbf h_0 + \sum_\ell F_\ell(\mathbf h_\ell)\). The output is the input plus the sum of the contributions of every layer

Notice what the simulation shows. It starts with ReLU, gain 1 and 32 layers. In the plain network, the gradient that reaches the first layer is about 5×10⁻⁷ of what left the loss: it died along the way. Raise the gain and it starts to explode. There is a critical gain (around 1.5 in this simulation; in mean-field theory, \(\sqrt2\approx1.41\), the He initialization) at which the norms are nearly conserved, but it is a knife's edge: at 64 layers, a gain of 1.4 leaves the gradient at 2×10⁻³ and a gain of 1.6 multiplies it by 12. With a residual and no normalization, the identity path saves the gradient, but each layer adds its output with no brakes, and activations and gradient grow about 10⁵ times over 32 layers. With residual + pre-norm, \(\mathbf h_{\ell+1} = \mathbf h_\ell + F_\ell(\mathrm{Norm}(\mathbf h_\ell))\), the gradient reaches the input at the same order of magnitude (≈ 1.3×) and the activations grow slowly, from 1 to about 13 over 32 layers: polynomial growth, not exponential. Each sublayer reads a normalized copy and writes a sum. That is the design of every modern LLM, and the vector that travels through the stack has a name: the residual stream. Chapter 7 treats it as a bus.

DeepSeek's ViT (patches/vision_exp/vision.py) shows the pattern in two lines:

def forward(self, x, cos, sin):
    x = x + self.attn(self.norm1(x), cos, sin)   # reads normalized, ADDS into the residual
    return x + self.mlp(self.norm2(x))           # same for the MLP (SwiGLU)
In the recipes · the residual got wide

The three models widen the residual stream to 4 streams. GLM and DeepSeek use mHC (hc_mult: 4, 4 × 4096 = 16,384 numbers per token), with the mixing matrix between streams projected, through 20 Sinkhorn iterations, onto the set of doubly stochastic matrices, whose spectral norm is ≤ 1. That is this section's concern (not letting the signal explode across depth) turned into an architectural constraint. Qwen uses the Gated Residual (4 × 2560 = 10,240). In the tech report's experiment (a 25B-A3B model, 560 B tokens), the loss dropped from 1.617 (classic pre-norm) to 1.596 (static mHC), 1.594 (dynamic mHC) and 1.590 (GR). Details in chapter 15.

Why this matters to recipe writers

The residual stream is where you “listen” to the model. Speculative drafters read hidden states from the target's layers (GLM has to contract the 4 mHC streams down to 4096 with hc_contract before handing them to DFlash2), and abliteration edits what the layers write into it (chapter 19). And a wide residual costs bandwidth: in Qwen served by vLLM, the Gated Residual weights stay in BF16, replicated on both GPUs, 1.19 GiB read per step, about 13% of the traffic.

In the recipes: activations, gates and norms in the config

Now you can read an LLM like a subway map: every residual read, every norm, every gate and every activation has a place and a reason to be there. Two things matter when reading the map. First, the stack is made of repeating layers, but not every layer is the same: the token mixer (the piece that mixes information across positions) alternates between two or three types, and each layer has one of them, never all. Second, some pieces sit outside the layer: the input, the output, the vision tower and the drafters. Checklist for your new model

When a new model comes out (chapter 46 does this with MiMo-V2.6-Flash), read the config and the reference code with this list in hand:

  1. Activation. Look for hidden_act (text and vision). gelu and gelu_pytorch_tanh are different functions. MiMo declares silu, and vLLM rejects any other activation in its MoE.
  2. ε and the field name. The name varies: rms_norm_eps (GLM, Qwen, DeepSeek), layernorm_epsilon (MiMo, 1e-6), norm_eps (DeepSeek's inference config). Also check whether some sub-tower uses a different value, like GLM's vision tower.
  3. Norm type and convention. LayerNorm or RMSNorm? Does it have a bias? Is the gain \(w\) or \(1 + w\)? Is it grouped? Does it have a weight or not (DeepSeek's per-head query norm has none)?
  4. Gates. List every \(\sigma(\cdot)\) and every “2·σ”. Check that the engine implements them in the same form, and keep them in high precision when quantizing.
  5. Measure. Even with everything checked, compare the served model's distribution with the reference (KLD). Deviations in ε, activation and gain convention show up there before they show up in a benchmark.
