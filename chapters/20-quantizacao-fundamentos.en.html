In BF16, GLM-5.3-Flash weighs 642.6 GB. Two DGX Sparks leave about 243 GiB visible to the GPU, combined. No scheduling trick makes 642.6 GB fit into ≈ 261 GB (the same 243 GiB, in the same unit). What solves it is changing how much each number costs. Quantizing means trading 16-bit values for 8- or 4-bit codes accompanied by a scale, accepting a small, measured error in exchange for 2 to 4 times fewer bytes stored and read. In this chapter you will build the quantization ruler by hand, see where the error is born (rounding and clipping), discover why a single outlier ruins an entire matrix, tell quantizing weights apart from quantizing activations, understand calibration, GPTQ and the Hessian, the difference between PTQ and QAT and, at the end, read the precision mix of the three recipes as engineering decisions, one by one.

Where the bytes are

Every recipe starts with two back-of-the-envelope calculations, which you already saw in the journey map. The first question is whether the model fits: the weights, plus the KV cache, the activations and the system, have to fit in the nodes' memory. The second is whether it is fast. In the chapter on prefill, decode and the KV cache we saw that each decode step generates one token per sequence and, to do so, has to read from memory every weight active in that step. With few rows per GEMM, the GPU spends most of its time waiting for bytes. This regime is bandwidth-bound, and in it the time of a step is, to a first approximation, bytes read divided by bandwidth.

\[ \text{Memory} \approx \sum_{c} N_c \cdot \frac{b_c}{8} \qquad\qquad t_{\text{step}} \gtrsim \frac{B_{\text{step}}}{\text{BW}} = \frac{\sum_{c} N^{\text{read}}_c \cdot b_c / 8}{\text{BW}} \]
  • \(c\): a component of the model (experts, attention, embeddings, lm_head…), each with its own format
  • \(N_c\): the component's parameters; \(N^{\text{read}}_c\): how many of them the step actually reads (in MoE, only the chosen experts)
  • \(b_c\): effective bits per parameter (16 in BF16, ~8 in FP8, ~4 to 4.5 in the 4-bit formats)
  • \(B_{\text{step}}\): bytes read per step; \(\text{BW}\): memory bandwidth (GB10: ~273 GB/s theoretical, 230–250 GB/s effective in the reports)

The recipes' numbers show the size of the problem. GLM-5.3-Flash has 321.3 B parameters, and 97% of them live in the routed experts. In BF16 that would be 642.6 GB; the official FP8 checkpoint is 328.3 GB, and neither fits in two nodes. The 4-bit-per-weight EXL3 checkpoint is 175.6 GB (163.6 GiB), and vLLM reports Model loading took 79.65 GiB per rank at TP=2, or 82.94 GiB with the BF16 copy of KDA that the recipe's default turns on (we come back to it in the section on weights and activations). In Qwen3.8-Flash-Next, the NVFP4 experts take up ≈ 63 GiB: 56.25 GiB of 4-bit codes plus 7.03 GiB of E4M3 scales (the FP8 variant with 4 exponent bits and 3 mantissa bits), exactly 0.5 extra bits per weight. Even so, a decode step with batch 1 and MTP=3 reads only ~2.1 GiB of them per GPU, out of a total of ≈ 9.2 GiB, of which ~6.9 GiB are dense projections in BF16. At 235–245 GB/s that gives 40–42 ms per step; with ≈3.2 tokens per step (2.2 accepted + the bonus token from verification), the streaming-only ceiling would be ~76–80 tok/s. The measured figure with MTP=3 was 52.1 tok/s (2026-09-05), with real steps of ~60 ms: ~1/3 of the step is fixed cost outside the weight reads. The recipe report cites 55.8 tok/s, an earlier measurement with MTP off and different weights. In DeepSeek-V4-Flash, each rank reads ≈ 16.5–17 GB per step, and the recipe report estimates that single-sequence decode is ~80% weight reads.

Notice two things. First, what takes up space is not what takes up bandwidth. The experts are almost the entire checkpoint, but each token visits only a few of them. That is why, in Qwen, the largest slice read per step is the BF16 lm_head (26%, because the MTP drafter rereads it three times), and not the experts. Second, every byte saved turns into speed almost linearly as long as the step is bandwidth-bound. Cutting 3.45 GiB of dense weights in Qwen would take the step from ≈ 9.2 to ≈ 5.7 GiB, an analytical projection of ~1.5× at batch 1 that the repository has not yet measured on GPU. GLM already reaps this gain in the served state. The checkpoint's BF16 dense weights add up to ~8 GB read per rank every step (inventory estimate), and the default GLM53_DENSE_FP8=all re-quantizes them to FP8 at load time. Converting only KDA and the dense MLP cuts ~2.6 GB ≈ 11 ms per step, the same order as the ~11 ms measured with dense,kda. Flip the switch in the figure to see the difference.

In the recipe: four bits for the dense weights too

The GLM recipe now has a second version, with the same checkpoint and a different engine: TensorFold v0.6.0 with 53 patches, in place of vLLM (chapters An exact engine and Swapping the engine). It takes the same math one step further. The BF16 weights that are not routed experts (attention, shared experts, dense layers and head) add up to ~9.7 GiB per Spark, according to the recipe, and every decode step reads them in full (the vLLM version's inventory, which slices the components differently, estimates ~8 GB read per rank). Where the vLLM version stops at FP8, the TensorFold version offers three levels. With DENSE=fp8, half the bytes of BF16, decode rises ~33%. The default, DENSE=q4, goes down to 4 bits and yields, over FP8, prose 38.9 → 44.4 tok/s (+14%) and code 44.2 → 48.7 tok/s (+10%), with DFlash2 and one boot each. In the matrices that become 4-bit, the bytes drop ~45% (from 8.25 to 4.5 effective bits per weight, our own math with the formula from the granularity section), but the step doesn't shrink in the same proportion. It also reads experts, KV and the drafter, waits for the communication between the two Sparks and pays fixed costs. It's the formula at the top of the section at work: only the dense weights' \(N_c\, b_c\) term shrinks.

Why this matters

Quantizing solves two different things, and they don't always go together. Capacity depends on the bytes stored, which here are dominated by the experts. Decode speed depends on the bytes read per step: active experts, dense weights, lm_head, KV. Prefill is another story. With thousands of rows per GEMM it becomes compute-bound, and then what counts is whether the format runs natively on a tensor core (the GPU unit that multiplies whole matrix blocks per instruction, in low-precision formats), the subject of the section on weights and activations.

The uniform ruler: scale, zero-point and rounding

With \(b\) bits there is room for \(2^b\) distinct codes (the chapter How the computer stores numbers shows how BF16 and FP16 spend their 16). Uniform quantization is a ruler with \(2^b\) evenly spaced ticks. With \(b=4\) bits there are 16 ticks, numbered from 0 to 15 (or from −8 to 7). Quantizing means deciding where to lay the ruler over the real numbers and, for each number, writing down the index of the nearest tick. Two quantities define the ruler. The scale \(s\) is the distance between neighboring ticks. The zero-point \(z\) is the index of the tick that lands exactly on the real zero.

\[ q = \mathrm{clamp}\!\Big(\mathrm{round}\big(\tfrac{x}{s}\big) + z,\; q_{\min},\; q_{\max}\Big) \qquad\qquad \hat x = s\,(q - z) \]
  • \(x\): the real number (weight or activation); \(\hat x\): the value the kernel uses after dequantizing
  • \(q\): the stored integer code, between \(q_{\min}\) and \(q_{\max}\) (0 and 15 for unsigned 4 bits; −8 and 7 signed)
  • \(\mathrm{round}\): round-to-nearest (RTN); \(\mathrm{clamp}\): pins the code to the range, and this is where clipping is born
  • Asymmetric: \(s = \frac{\max - \min}{2^b - 1}\), \(z = \mathrm{round}(-\min/s)\). Symmetric: \(z = 0\), \(s = \mathrm{amax}/q_{\max}\) with \(q_{\max} = 2^{b-1}-1\) and \(\mathrm{amax} = \max|x|\)

In the figure below, step through an example worked by hand: four numbers, \(x = [-0.8,\ -0.1,\ 0.3,\ 1.2]\), quantized to 4 bits. Watch the dots slide to the nearest tick and, in the last step, compare with the symmetric version.

Notice what the example teaches. The largest error, 0.033, stayed below the theoretical limit \(s/2 = 0.067\), and the extremes came out exact because the ruler was stretched all the way to them. The symmetric version is simpler for the kernel, which only multiplies by the scale and doesn't need to subtract \(z\). In exchange, it can waste up to half the ruler when the data is skewed (in the extreme case, with every value of the same sign). Here the values go from −0.8 to 1.2, so only codes −8, −7 and −6 fall outside the data, 3 of the 16, and the tick grows from 0.133 to 0.171.

Deep dive: why zero has to be exact

The zero-point is an integer precisely so that the real zero lands on top of a tick. Many zeros are structural: padding, ReLU outputs, masked positions, the RoPE block of zeros that GLM appends to the KV. If zero became ±0.03, the error would be systematic and would add up in every multiplication. LLM weights are roughly symmetric around zero, which is why the vLLM versions of the three recipes use symmetric scales. Floating-point formats (FP8, FP4) are also symmetric by construction, since they have a sign bit. The exception is DENSE=q4 in the TensorFold version of GLM , an affine ruler: each group stores a scale and an offset (the bias, which is the group's minimum after clipping) in BF16, and the offset is not an integer. The real zero falls between two ticks and inherits up to half a tick of error. In dense projections that matters little, because they have no structural zeros (interpretation).

One caveat before moving on. In the vLLM versions, none of the three recipes stores weights in uniform INT4 or INT8. They use FP8, FP4 and the EXL3 trellis format. The exception is the TensorFold version of GLM , which at load time converts the dense weights to 4-bit integers on an asymmetric variant of this ruler, with a floating-point bias in place of the zero-point and the range chosen by search (the granularity section shows how). The integer ruler is still the foundation of all of them. In FP8, the recipe picks \(s = \mathrm{amax}/448\) (448 is the largest E4M3 value), divides, rounds to the format's grid and multiplies back. That is exactly the code of GLM's per-channel FP8 (|W|.amax(dim=1)/448) and of Qwen's FP8-dense converter (scale = (amax/448).clamp_min(1e-12)). The only difference is that the FP8 grid doesn't have equal steps: it is dense near zero and more spread out far from it. The next chapter opens up this grid bit by bit.

Round or clip: measuring the error

Quantization error comes from two sources, and they pull in opposite directions. Rounding error hits every value inside the ruler. It is at most \(s/2\) and, if the values land scattered between the ticks, it behaves like uniform noise with variance \(s^2/12\). Clipping error hits only the values that fall outside: clipping turns everything above the limit \(c\) into \(c\). If you stretch the ruler to the largest absolute value (\(c = \mathrm{amax}\)), nothing is clipped, but a single giant value makes the ticks wide for everyone else. If you shrink the ruler, the common values gain resolution and the extremes get flattened.

\[ \mathrm{MSE}(c) \approx \underbrace{\frac{s(c)^2}{12}\,P(|x|\le c)}_{\text{rounding}} + \underbrace{\mathbb E\big[(|x|-c)^2\,;\ |x|>c\big]}_{\text{clipping}}, \qquad s(c) = \frac{c}{q_{\max}} \] \[ \mathrm{SQNR} = 10\log_{10}\frac{\mathbb E[x^2]}{\mathrm{MSE}}\ \text{dB} \qquad\qquad \text{rel RMSE} = \frac{\lVert \hat x - x\rVert_2}{\lVert x\rVert_2} = 10^{-\mathrm{SQNR}/20} \]
  • \(c\): clipping limit (the ruler goes from \(-c\) to \(c\)); \(P(|x|\le c)\): fraction of the values that stay inside
  • MSE: mean squared error between \(\hat x\) and \(x\)
  • SQNR: signal-to-quantization-noise ratio. Each extra bit halves the tick and yields ≈ 6.02 dB
  • rel RMSE: fraction of the signal that turned into noise. If the error is uncorrelated with \(x\), the cosine similarity is \(\cos(\hat x, x) \approx 1/\sqrt{1+\text{rel RMSE}^2} \approx 1 - \text{rel RMSE}^2/2\). With rel RMSE 0.026 that gives ≈ 0.9997, which explains the cosine ≥ 0.9996 of Qwen's FP8-dense, a little further on

The figure below uses synthetic weights shaped like an LLM matrix: a narrow Gaussian plus about 1% of values ten times larger. Play with the clipping limit and the bits. The histogram shows where the ticks fall, and the curve shows the MSE for every possible limit. The red dots are clipped weights.

This “U” explains a line in the model card of Qwen's NVFP4 checkpoint: the experts' scales are MSE-calibrated (“MSE-calibrated weight scales”). Instead of using amax, the optimizer picks, block by block, the scale that minimizes the squared error, and sometimes that means clipping. With INT8 the tick is already fine, as the figure shows at 8 bits. In FP8 the reason is different: the grid is logarithmic, the relative error of each value stays almost constant (≤ 6.25%) across the whole range, and shrinking the ruler barely improves the small values. That is why the simple rule \(s = \mathrm{amax}/448\) is enough, and it is what GLM and Qwen do in FP8 without any calibration.

Weight metrics show up in the recipes as conversion checks. Qwen's FP8-dense checkpoint was checked tensor by tensor: across the 591 converted tensors, rel RMSE stayed between 0.0240 and 0.0268 (median 0.0262), equivalent to an SQNR of about 31.6 dB, with cosine ≥ 0.9996 in the samples. The verifier flags HIGH ERROR if any tensor exceeds 0.06. In GLM, the BF16 copy of the KDA FP8 matrix differs from the Marlin path by a rel RMSE of 0.002526.

Pitfall

A small weight error doesn't guarantee a good model. What matters is how much the output changes: the error after multiplying by the activations and, in the end, the next-token distribution (KLD, the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the next-token distributions of the quantized model and of the reference; perplexity; tasks). In GLM, per-channel FP8 with a modest weight error still left text KL 25–30% above the stock-versus-stock pairs, even though the panel came out NOT-WORSE on every item. The chapter Measuring the damage builds these instruments. Here, treat rel RMSE and SQNR as a sanity check of the conversion.

Granularity: who shares the same ruler

So far one scale served an entire vector. In a weight matrix \(W \in \mathbb R^{N\times K}\) (N output rows, K input channels), you decide how many numbers share the same scale. That is the granularity, and it is the main defense against outliers. LLMs have a few channels with magnitudes 10 to 100 times larger than the rest, and with one scale for the whole tensor a single one of these values stretches the ruler for everyone else. The small values end up in a handful of ticks near zero, or at zero itself.

per tensor
one scale for everything. The cheapest and the most fragile. In the recipes: Qwen's FP8 KV, the FP8 PLE table (a single global weight_scale), the global FP32 scale of NVFP4.
per channel
one scale per output row of the weight (K values per scale). In the recipes: GLM's dense FP8 (BF16 scale per row) and Qwen's FP8-dense (FP32 scale per row, weight_scale [out]).
per token
the equivalent for activations: one scale per row of \(X\), computed on the fly. That is the case for the activations of Qwen's FP8-dense.
per group / 1D block
one scale every \(g\) contiguous values along K: 16 in NVFP4, 32 in MXFP4 and MXFP8, 64 in DeepSeek's KV, 128 in the scales of GLM's fp8_ds_mla (4 FP32 scales for 512 dims). In the TensorFold version of GLM : 64 in the q4 dense weights (BF16 scale and bias per group) and 128 in DENSE=fp8 (FP32 scale \(\mathrm{amax}/448\) per row and block of 128 columns).
2D block
one scale per 128×128 tile of the weight. It shows up in DeepSeek's non-expert linears and in Qwen's MTP experts.

Fine granularity costs bytes, because every scale is stored too. The math for effective bits per weight is straightforward:

\[ b_{\text{eff}} = b + \frac{b_s + b_z}{g} \]
  • \(b\): code bits; \(b_s\): bits of each scale (16 in BF16, 32 in FP32, 8 in E4M3 or in E8M0, a byte that stores only an exponent, so the scale is a power of 2); \(b_z\): zero-point bits (0 if symmetric)
  • \(g\): how many weights share the scale (K per channel; 16, 32, 64… per group; 16,384 in a 128×128 block)
  • Examples from the recipes: per-channel FP8 with K = 4096 and a BF16 scale → 8.004; with an FP32 scale → 8.008; FP8 in 128×128 blocks → ≈ 8.002; NVFP4 → 4 + 8/16 = 4.5; MXFP4 → 4 + 8/32 = 4.25; EXL3 K=4 on a [4096 × 2048] matrix → 4.0117 (there is no per-group scale, only the suh/svh vectors); in the TensorFold version of GLM, the affine q4 (BF16 scale and bias every 64, so \(b_z = 16\)) → 4 + 32/64 = 4.5 and FP8 with an FP32 scale per block of 128 → 8 + 32/128 = 8.25

In the figure below, an 8 × 32 weight matrix is quantized to symmetric INT4 with four granularities. Turn on the outlier and change the granularity. The bottom map shows the error of each weight. Hover over (or tap) a cell to see its group and its scale.

The outlier doesn't disappear with fine granularity. It stops being its neighbors' problem. That is why the 4-bit formats use small blocks (16 or 32): with only 15 representable values in FP4, a scale that is too large turns a good part of the block into zero. FP8, on the other hand, has enough dynamic range to live with a per-channel or large-block scale. The other defense against outliers is to rotate the matrix before quantizing, spreading the large value across every coordinate. That is EXL3's Hadamard rotation, in the chapter Rotations, trellises and EXL3.

Searching for the clip, group by group

Three ideas from this chapter combine into a quantization that needs no data: the asymmetric ruler, the error “U” and the small group. The previous section showed that, with few bits, the best clipping limit sits below amax. Finding it doesn't require calibration. You just try a few clips and measure the error of each one, group by group. That is what DENSE=q4 in the TensorFold version of GLM does at load time, for every group of 64 consecutive weights along K:

\[ a_i = 1 - 0.45\,\tfrac{i}{15}, \qquad b = a_i \min g, \qquad s = \frac{a_i\,(\max g - \min g)}{15} \] \[ q_k = \mathrm{clamp}\Big(\mathrm{round}\big(\tfrac{g_k - b}{s}\big),\ 0,\ 15\Big), \qquad i^\star = \arg\min_i \sum_{k=1}^{64} \big(s\,q_k + b - g_k\big)^2 \]
  • \(g\): the 64 weights of a group (one output row, 64 consecutive input columns); \(i = 0,\dots,15\): the 16 clips tried, from 1.0 down to 0.55 of the group's minimum and maximum
  • \(b\) and \(s\) are rounded to BF16 before the error is measured, so the search evaluates exactly the ruler that will be stored. \(b\) plays the role of the zero-point, but in floating point: it is an affine ruler
  • Cost: 4 bits of code plus 16 + 16 bits of scale and bias every 64 weights, or 4.5 effective bits

In the figure, each row of the table is a group of synthetic weights and each column is one of the 16 clips. The cursor sweeps the columns the way the search does, and the green dot marks the best clip seen so far in each group. On the right, the highlighted group appears on the 16-tick ruler of the clip under test, with the clipped weights in red, and below it sits that group's “U”, now with only 16 points. Start with the q4 group of 64 and then grow the group up to 4096, the length of a whole row with K = 4096, which amounts to one scale per channel.

The figure debunks a common intuition. With groups of 64, the bottom of the “U” sits right next to 1.0: in these samples the search picks clips between 0.88 and 1.00 and removes only 3–9% of the MSE. In a small group, clipping the single large weight costs more error than it gives back to the other 63, and the MSE prefers to protect it. With 4096 weights per group the ruler has to cover many more extreme values, and the optimal clip drops to 0.61–0.76, removing over a third of the MSE. Even so, the rel RMSE with search stays near 20%, against 10–13% for the group of 64 with no search at all. Granularity and clipping attack the same problem, and granularity wins, at the price of 0.5 bit of scale and bias per weight. The grid that goes down to 0.55 works as a safety net for groups with heavier tails (interpretation: the repository doesn't publish which clips GLM's real weights choose).

Notice also what this search is and what it isn't. It's cheap: 16 tries per group, done once, at load time, without any input data. But it minimizes the weight error, \(\lVert \hat w - w\rVert^2\), without weighting each channel by the activations it receives. In the language of the calibration section, further on, this is the \(\mathbf H = I\) case. GPTQ and AWQ use real activations to spend precision where the output is most sensitive, and the recipe itself notes in the v1.3.2 CHANGELOG that a calibrated 4-bit, at q4's speed, is in the works. The “In the recipes” section shows how much the search delivered and where it failed.

Scales and tensor parallel: what gets cut together

Granularity has a consequence that only shows up when the model is split across GPUs. In tensor parallel each rank stores a slice of each matrix, for example half of the output rows. The scales have to be cut along with the weights they describe. Per-channel scales are a vector with one entry per row, so they are cut exactly like the weight: rows 0–319 go to rank 0, 320–639 to rank 1. 128×128 block scales only cut cleanly if the boundary between ranks falls on a block boundary. That is the column-parallel cut (the one for the input projections, like gate/up_proj). The other cut, row-parallel (down_proj, o_proj), splits K, the input channels: each rank sums half of \(\sum_k\) and an all-reduce combines the partial sums. There the per-output-channel scale is not cut but replicated, because each rank needs the full scale of every row to scale its partial sum. The block scale, in turn, requires K per rank to be a multiple of 128.

This was one of the three reasons, recorded in the Qwen recipe report, for the FP8-dense checkpoint to use a per-channel scale instead of 128×128 blocks. First, it doesn't need calibration data. Second, the rank-320 projections of the HyperConnections and the TP-sliced shared expert (320 rows) are not divisible by 128. Third, the ParallelLMHead loader in vLLM slices block scales the wrong way: it cuts by the number of vocabulary rows, not by the number of blocks, while per-channel scales “are cut like the weight”. The lesson holds for any new recipe: before choosing a block, check that the per-rank shape is still divisible by it.

Weights, activations and the kernel that dequantizes

A linear layer is a GEMM \(Y = XW\), with \(X \in \mathbb R^{M\times K}\) (the activations of \(M\) tokens) and \(W \in \mathbb R^{K\times N}\) (the weights), as in the chapter on tensors and linear algebra. Note the change of convention: here \(W\) is K×N, the transpose of the previous section. Output channel \(n\), which there was a row, is now a column, and the per-channel scale is now called \(s^w_n\). It is the same scale, just written on the other side. In decode, \(M\) is small: one row per sequence, plus the speculation's verification rows. In prefill, \(M\) reaches the thousands. You can quantize only \(W\) or both operands, and the choice changes what the kernel does.

🧳

Weight-only (W4A16, W8A16)

Only the weight is stored in 4 or 8 bits. The kernel reads the codes, dequantizes in registers (“on the fly”, never writing the BF16 matrix to memory) and does the multiplication on a 16-bit tensor core, with MMA instructions (matrix-multiply-accumulate: each one multiplies two small matrix tiles and adds the result into the accumulator). The activation is left untouched, so there is no activation error. The gain is in bandwidth, not FLOPs. This is the decode regime: GLM's EXL3 experts (FP16 MMA), GLM's dense FP8 via Marlin, DeepSeek's MXFP4 experts in b12x.

⚡

Weights + activations (W8A8, W4A8, W4A4)

The activation is quantized too, and the multiplication runs on a low-precision tensor core. On GB10 the FP8 rate is 2× the BF16 rate. The gain is in FLOPs, which is what matters in prefill. The cost is a new error, the activation's QDQ. W4A8 is the middle ground: it reads the bytes of a 4-bit weight and multiplies against an FP8 activation. In the recipes: Qwen's NVFP4 experts (W4A4, CUTLASS SM120), the optional FP8-dense checkpoint (dynamic W8A8, CUTLASS) and, only as a proposal, the MXFP4 × MXFP8 W4A8 in the DeepSeek report.

The scales enter the computation in a way that decides which kernel is possible. With a per-token scale on the activation (\(s^x_m\)) and a per-channel scale on the weight (\(s^w_n\)), both come out of the sum, and the tensor core multiplies only integers or minifloats. The scales are applied once at the end, in the kernel's epilogue, the final stage that scales and converts the accumulator while it is still in registers, before writing \(Y\):

\[ y_{mn} = \sum_{k} x_{mk} w_{kn} \approx \sum_k \big(s^x_m q^x_{mk}\big)\big(s^w_n q^w_{kn}\big) = s^x_m\, s^w_n \sum_{k} q^x_{mk}\, q^w_{kn} \]
  • \(q^x, q^w\): activation and weight codes; \(s^x_m\): scale of token \(m\); \(s^w_n\): scale of output channel \(n\)
  • The sum over \(k\) runs entirely on the low-precision tensor core (accumulating in FP32); the product of the scales comes in at the epilogue
  • With per-group scales along \(K\), the factor stays inside the sum, \(\sum_{g} s_{m,g}\, s_{g,n} \sum_{k\in g}\cdots\), and the kernel needs a block-scaled MMA (Blackwell FP4/MX) or has to dequantize first

An affine ruler adds a term, and it has a shortcut too. In the q4 of GLM's TensorFold version , each weight of group \(g\) (64 consecutive values along K) is \(\hat w_{kn} = s_{gn}\,q_{kn} + b_{gn}\). The product with one activation row splits into two sums:

\[ \sum_{k \in g} x_{mk}\,\big(s_{gn}\,q_{kn} + b_{gn}\big) \;=\; s_{gn} \sum_{k \in g} x_{mk}\,q_{kn} \;+\; b_{gn}\,\underbrace{\sum_{k \in g} x_{mk}}_{\sigma_{mg}} \]
  • \(s_{gn}, b_{gn}\): the group's scale and bias (BF16) for output column \(n\); \(q_{kn} \in \{0,\dots,15\}\): the 4-bit code
  • \(\sigma_{mg}\): the sum of token \(m\)'s activation over group \(g\). It doesn't depend on \(n\), so it is computed once per row and group and serves every output column. That is why TensorFold's Q4 kernels receive, besides \(X\), the per-group sums over 64 (qmm.group_sums), and the bias costs one multiplication per group, not per weight
  • The activation stays in BF16: it's a W4A16, weight-only, like the dense FP8 of both versions of the recipe

GLM measured the price of activation error. For the prefill (large M) of KDA's in_proj projection, PR #182 tested a W8A8 path: activation quantized per row to FP8 × FP8 weight via _scaled_mm. It was fast, about 12.57 ms against Marlin's 27.21 ms at M = 7168, but it reproducibly shifted probabilities across the whole model. The attribution was clean: the activation QDQ alone explains the ~0.0266 per-layer error against Marlin, and the backend contributes only ~0.002–0.003. In the A/B/C study (15 texts, 33,005 positions), W8A8 gave 7.99× the KL of stock noise at M = 65 and 23 coordinated token flips, and one reference token dropped from probability 0.778 to 0.0051. The recipe gave up on quantizing the activation. Instead it keeps a BF16 copy of the same logical FP8 matrix (e4m3 × BF16 scale, rel RMSE 0.002526 against Marlin) and uses BF16 cuBLAS above M = 512. That gives 7.36 ms at M = 7168 and TTFT (time to first token, dominated by prefill) −11.4% at 16k, at the cost of +3.29 GiB per rank. This is GLM53_KDA_BF16_LARGE_M=1, on by default in .env.example together with GLM53_DENSE_FP8=all. Those gigabytes came from somewhere: the KV reservation dropped from 14 to 11 GiB (--kv-cache-memory-bytes 11811160064), from 1,572,073 to 1,233,779 tokens. In unified memory, every format is a trade-off between weight bytes, KV bytes and error.

Static or dynamic: when the activation scale is decided

The weight is known in advance, so its scale can be computed once and written into the checkpoint. The activation changes with every token. There are two ways to scale it:

  • Static: the scale is fixed offline, by measuring amax (or the MSE optimum) on a calibration set, and written into the checkpoint (for example, input_scale). At runtime it costs nothing. The risk is that a token outside the calibration distribution blows past the ruler and saturates.
  • Dynamic: before each GEMM, the kernel computes the amax of each token (or of each block of 128 values) and derives the scale on the spot. It needs no calibration and doesn't saturate. The cost is one reduction and one quantization step per GEMM, which can be fused into the previous kernel (into the norm, for example) or run in a kernel of its own, like act_quant in the DeepSeek reference code.

In the figure, each token is a person with 16 activations (the bars), and the frame is its FP8 ruler. With a static scale everyone goes through the same frame, calibrated for the typical token. With a dynamic scale each one gets a made-to-measure frame. Crank up the atypical token and see what the fixed frame does to it. Then switch the atypical value from one token to one channel (the same atypical index in every token) and the format from FP8 to INT8: the color of each bar then shows the error of that value.

Whenever Qwen and DeepSeek quantize activations to FP8, the scale is dynamic. GLM doesn't quantize activations in the served path. Qwen's FP8-dense declares FP8_PER_CHANNEL_PER_TOKEN: symmetric FP8 weight per output channel (\(s = \mathrm{amax}_{\text{row}}/448\)) and activation quantized per token at runtime. That is why the converter says it doesn't need calibration data (“without calibration data”). The MTP experts in the nvidia checkpoint use FP8 in 128×128 blocks with dynamic activation in groups of 128, and DeepSeek calls act_quant(x, 128) before every FP8/FP4 GEMM, one scale per block of 128 along K, computed on the spot. Qwen's NVFP4 experts mix both regimes: a static global input_scale (calibrated, dynamic: false) and activation block scales computed at runtime (scaled_fp4_quant).

Parameteractivation_scheme="dynamic"

What it is. The field of vLLM's Fp8Config that says how the activation of an FP8 GEMM is scaled: "static" reads a calibrated scale from the checkpoint, "dynamic" computes amax at runtime. Value in the recipe. The patch files/patch_modelopt_fp8_block_moe.py builds the MTP experts as Fp8MoEMethod(Fp8Config(is_checkpoint_fp8_serialized=True, activation_scheme="dynamic", weight_block_size=[g, g])), with \(g\) read from the checkpoint's group_size (128). The FP8-dense path uses the same idea per token. Why. The checkpoint carries no activation scales for these modules, and the dynamic regime needs no calibration set. The patch reads the block instead of guessing it, because a wrong block shape doesn't fail: it silently applies misaligned scales. With the right block, MTP=3 took decode from 24.5 to 52.1 tok/s, with 72.8% acceptance. A wrong block would show up as nearly random acceptance.

Atypical channels: why the activation is harder than the weight

The “one channel” mode of the figure above shows the classic reason, described in the literature (LLM.int8(), SmoothQuant) and not measured in the recipes. In large LLMs, a few activation channels, always the same indices \(k\), are 10 to 100 times larger than the rest in practically every token. A per-token scale isolates an atypical token, but not an atypical channel: the outlier sits inside every row of \(X\) and stretches the ruler of every token. In INT8, the other 15 values of each token fall into a few ticks near zero, and that happens with a static or a dynamic scale. The obvious way out would be a per-channel activation scale, \(s^x_k\). The catch is that it lives on the index the tensor core sums over:

\[ y_{mn} \approx \sum_k \big(s^x_k\, q^x_{mk}\big)\big(s^w_n\, q^w_{kn}\big) = s^w_n \sum_k s^x_k\; q^x_{mk}\, q^w_{kn} \] \[ X W = \big(X\,\mathrm{diag}(\alpha)^{-1}\big)\big(\mathrm{diag}(\alpha)\,W\big) \]
  • \(s^x_k\) varies with \(k\) and doesn't come out of the sum: the low-precision GEMM would have to rescale every term before accumulating, and would lose its advantage
  • Below, SmoothQuant's way out: dividing the activation by \(\alpha_k\) and multiplying row \(k\) of the weight (K×N) by the same factor leaves \(XW\) intact and “migrates” the atypical channel into the weight, which is quantized carefully with a per-output-channel scale. It is the same transformation AWQ uses in the next section
  • The other defense is rotating: a Hadamard rotation spreads the atypical channel across every coordinate before quantizing (chapter Rotations, trellises and EXL3)

Three practical consequences follow. First, weight-only suffers none of this: the activation stays in BF16, which is why decode, being bandwidth-bound, prefers W4A16 and W8A16. Second, the weight is known in advance and can be quantized carefully (GPTQ, MSE-chosen scales), while the activation needs a cheap rule, like amax, applied in every GEMM of every step, and the error is new at every layer. Third, FP8 cushions the problem. With a logarithmic grid, a value 100 times smaller than amax still falls in the normal range of E4M3, with the same relative error (≤ 6.25%). Switch the format in the figure and watch the common-value error stay at ~2–3%. That is why dynamic per-token FP8 activation is viable in Qwen. What remains is the intrinsic error of the 3 mantissa bits, on the order of 2.6% per value. One plausible reading (interpretation; the repository attributes the error only to the QDQ, without breaking it down) is that this is the term behind the ~0.0266 per layer of GLM's W8A8: the same size as the 0.024–0.027 rel RMSE that FP8 produces on Qwen's weights. The difference is that W8A8 adds this noise on top of what the FP8 weight already had: the reference Marlin path already carries the weight error, and the QDQ adds a second error, new at every token and every layer.

Calibration: the error that matters is the output's

RTN rounds each weight on its own, minimizing \(\lVert \hat W - W\rVert^2\). The layer, however, doesn't deliver weights: it delivers \(XW\). If an input coordinate has huge activations, getting the corresponding weight wrong is expensive. If two inputs always move together, opposite errors on the two weights cancel out in the output. The right measure is the output error, and it calls for a matrix that describes the inputs.

\[ \mathbb E\,\big\lVert x^\top(\hat w - w)\big\rVert^2 = (\hat w - w)^\top \mathbf H\,(\hat w - w), \qquad \mathbf H = \mathbb E\big[x\,x^\top\big] \approx \tfrac{1}{n} X^\top X \]
  • \(w \in \mathbb R^K\): the K weights of one output neuron, which is a row of \(W\) in the N×K convention of the granularity section and a column in the K×N convention of the GEMM; \(\hat w\): its quantized version
  • \(x\): one input vector of the layer; \(X \in \mathbb R^{n\times K}\): \(n\) real inputs collected in a calibration set
  • \(\mathbf H\): the proxy Hessian. Its diagonal says which input channels “weigh” more, and the off-diagonal terms say which ones move together. With \(\mathbf H = I\), the output error goes back to being the weight error

GPTQ uses \(\mathbf H\) in a greedy and clever way. It quantizes one input column at a time and, at each step, pushes the error it just made onto the columns not yet quantized, in the direction that \(\mathbf H^{-1}\) indicates. The following columns make up for what the previous one got wrong. The update, inherited from Optimal Brain Surgeon, is:

\[ e_j = \frac{w_j - Q(w_j)}{[\mathbf H^{-1}]_{jj}}, \qquad w_{k} \leftarrow w_{k} - e_j\,[\mathbf H^{-1}]_{jk}\quad (k > j) \]
  • \(j\): the column quantized in this step; \(Q(\cdot)\): rounding to the ruler
  • \([\mathbf H^{-1}]_{jk}\): how much column \(k\) can compensate for the error of column \(j\). After each step, row and column \(j\) leave \(\mathbf H^{-1}\) (Schur complement)
  • In practice there is damping (\(\mathbf H + \lambda I\), here 1% of the mean diagonal) and a Cholesky decomposition instead of the explicit inverse

Notice what the figure shows: the more correlated the inputs, the more GPTQ gains, because there are more directions in which an error can be compensated. AWQ attacks the same problem from another side. It observes that a few input channels have large activations (the “salient” ones, the same atypical channels from the previous section) and protects them with a per-channel factor \(\alpha_k\): \(W' = \mathrm{diag}(\alpha)\,W\) and \(X' = X\,\mathrm{diag}(\alpha)^{-1}\). Here \(W\) is in the K×N convention of the GEMM, so \(\mathrm{diag}(\alpha)\) scales the rows \(k\), which are the input channels. It is the same math as SmoothQuant with a different goal: there the factor flattens the activation so it can be quantized, here it enlarges the important weights so that rounding hurts them less. The product \(X'W'\) is identical to \(XW\) before quantization, but the salient weights become larger relative to their scale and lose less relative precision. The factors \(\alpha\) are chosen by search on a calibration set. In the figure, the amber bars are AWQ: with channels 3 and 8 salient it cuts the output error, and without them it has no one to protect. Both methods belong to the PTQ family and minimize output error, not weight error.

A calibration set is a small sample of real text that goes through the model only to measure activations: hundreds of sequences, not a training run. It needs to resemble real usage, because scales and Hessians calibrated on news articles can go wrong on code. Examples from the recipes: the NVFP4 scales of the nvidia/Qwen3.8-Flash-Next-NVFP4 checkpoint were MSE-calibrated on cnn_dailymail and Nemotron-Post-Training-Dataset-v2, with no fine-tuning. The exllamav3 converter, which the GLM recipe uses for the drafter, by default estimates the Hessian during conversion from calibration text (-cr 250 sequences × -cc 2048 tokens, according to the external documentation), except for architectures flagged as uncalibrated_quantize, like DFlash2 itself. The EXL3/QTIP family minimizes this output error with a GPTQ variant (LDLQ) inside the trellis, the subject of the chapter Rotations, trellises and EXL3.

Not every quantization needs calibration. At 8 bits, per-channel RTN works (in INT8 because the tick is fine, in FP8 because the relative error is almost constant), and that is what GLM does when re-quantizing the dense weights to FP8 at load time, and what Qwen does in FP8-dense, which also uses dynamic activation. The interesting case is quantizing to 6 bits without data, which is what the GLM recipe does with the DFlash2 drafter in the H3 profile.

PTQ or QAT: when the model trained with the error

Everything we have seen so far is post-training quantization (PTQ): the model was trained in high precision, and quantization comes in afterwards as a perturbation that one tries to minimize (RTN, GPTQ, EXL3, ModelOpt's calibrated scales). In quantization-aware training (QAT), the training forward already contains quantize-dequantize (QDQ) nodes: the tensor goes through \(\hat x = s\cdot Q(x/s)\) and continues in high precision, as if it had been stored in the low format. The model learns to live with that specific rounding.

\[ \text{forward: } \hat x = s\cdot \mathrm{clamp}\big(\mathrm{round}(x/s), -q_{\max}, q_{\max}\big) \qquad \text{backward (STE): } \frac{\partial \hat x}{\partial x} \approx \mathbb 1\big[\,|x| \le s\,q_{\max}\big] \]
  • Rounding has zero derivative almost everywhere, so the gradient would not get through
  • The straight-through estimator (STE) pretends that the round function is the identity in the backward pass (derivative 1 inside the ruler, 0 where there was clipping)
  • With FP8, \(q_{\max} = 448\) and the “round” is rounding to the E4M3 grid

DeepSeek-V4-Flash is the cleanest example among the recipes. The published reference forward applies act_quant(..., inplace=True) to the KV after the norm and RoPE, only on the NoPE dimensions: FP8 E4M3 in groups of 64 with a power-of-2 scale rounded up. The 64 RoPE dimensions stay in BF16. The indexer is also simulated in FP4 (blocks of 32, after a Hadamard rotation). Practical consequence: storing the KV in FP8 with a UE8M0 scale every 64 (a byte that stores only the exponent, so the scale is a power of 2; these are the 584 B per KV entry, per layer, of fp8_ds_mla/nvfp4_ds_mla) is not an extra approximation imposed by the recipe. It is the regime the model trained in.

Compare with Qwen's FP8 KV, which is PTQ. The model did not train with it, the scale is per tensor, and the checkpoint carries no calibrated KV scales, so the default of 1.0 applies (the effective value is inferred). The patch warns that, in the reference implementation, a long-reasoning benchmark dropped from 6/6 to 2/6 with FP8 KV, because in sparse attention quantizing the keys changes which blocks the indexer picks. In the recipe, the reasoning-plus-needles check scored 12/12, the same as BF16, and the chapter Quantizing the KV cache details this trade-off.

Overlay kernel_dspark_quant_dequant_nope_kernel

What it is. A Triton kernel (dspark_kernels.py:88-118, wrapper dspark_quant_dequant_nope at :635-674) that simulates in the DSpark drafter the same QDQ used in training. For each KV row and each group of 64 NoPE dims, it computes \(\mathrm{amax} \ge 10^{-4}\) and \(s = 2^{\lceil \log_2(\mathrm{amax}/448)\rceil}\), clamps \(x/s\) to ±448, converts to float8e4nv, multiplies back by \(s\) and writes in place. The result is still in high precision, but with FP8 values. The RoPE slice is not touched, the wrapper requires nope_dim % 64 == 0, and outside CUDA or with a non-contiguous tensor it falls back to a PyTorch reference. Value in the recipe. It only runs with VLLM_DSPARK_REFERENCE_KV_QUANT_DEQUANT=1 (default 0, Stage-C overlay). Why. The drafter's internal KV is computed by the overlay, and to compare this forward with the reference bit for bit it has to go through the same QAT rounding. It is a numerical parity instrument (chapter Measuring the damage), not a memory optimization.

Analogy

PTQ is asking a pianist, on the day of the concert, to play an out-of-tune piano. QAT is letting them rehearse on that piano for months. In DeepSeek, the FP8 KV is the rehearsal piano, and in Qwen the FP8 KV is the concert-day piano. The recipes treat the two cases differently: the first is the native format, and the second is a trade-off (1.70× more tokens per GiB) that has to be validated at long context.

Between PTQ and QAT: QAD, rehearsing with a teacher in the room

A middle ground that vendors have started to publish is quantization-aware distillation (QAD). The student is the model itself with QDQ nodes in the forward pass, as in QAT, but the training target isn't the next token of the text: it's the output distribution of the original BF16 model, the teacher. The loss is usually a divergence (KL) between the two distributions (general definition; the recipe's README doesn't detail the training). The example comes from a sibling recipe of Mia's, Qwen3.8 Flash Next on a single Spark, on TensorFold: its default checkpoint, nvidia/Qwen3.8-Flash-Next-FP4-Local, had its NVFP4 and MXFP8 weights trained by NVIDIA against the BF16 model as teacher. It carries the routed experts in NVFP4 (group 16, with compressed scales), the attention and shared-expert linears in MXFP8, and the MTP and vision tower quantized too. Per the model card (not measured in the recipe), it scores 91.5% on GPQA Diamond and 88.7% on ToolEvalBench, against 92.2% and 89.4% for BF16. The difference from the PTQ of the previous sections: instead of minimizing each weight's error, training directly minimizes the difference in the output, which is what this whole chapter says matters. The cost falls on whoever trains (GPUs, data and the teacher running alongside); for whoever serves, it's a checkpoint like any other. Beyond the Spark: NVFP4 runs natively on Blackwell's FP4 tensor cores (B200, GB200, GB300 and the GB10 itself), and QAD checkpoints come out of the same Model Optimizer whose checkpoints TensorRT-LLM, vLLM and SGLang serve in production. On an H200 (Hopper), with no FP4 tensor cores, FP8 remains the natural point (our interpretation).

Cutting experts instead of bits: a structural trade-off

Another way to shrink an MoE changes the function being computed, not just the numbers. The azampatti/Qwen3.8-Flash-Next-125B-A5B-INT4-AutoRound checkpoint starts from Intel's AutoRound int4 (experts in GPTQ int4, group 128) and makes a top-5 cut: each token goes through 5 routed experts instead of 10, with a “healed” shared expert (the author's word) to compensate, and the active parameters drop from 6.0 B to 4.8 B. In the two-Spark recipe on TensorFold (QUANT=int4ar), prose decode rises from 67.4 to 89.3 tok/s with one request and from 231.2 to 284.9 with 8 (sparkDash). The price, according to the model card, on its authors' own harness: about 10% lower general capability (46.6–47.6 against the original's 51.8), with tool use unchanged (91 against 91–92). Fewer experts per token means fewer distinct experts read per step (chapter 11) on any bandwidth-bound hardware and, on a cluster with expert parallelism, less all-to-all traffic (our interpretation). But the model is no longer the same one, and a KL against BF16 or an English-only benchmark doesn't tell the whole story: measure on your own tasks, as step 6 of the list at the end of the chapter asks.

In the recipes: mixed precision per component

Putting it all together, each component gets a format according to two questions: how many bytes it represents (stored and read per step) and how sensitive it is. The routed experts are almost all of the bytes, and each one is used by a small fraction of the tokens, so they get ~4 bits. The components that make discrete decisions or accumulate state are small and fragile, and they stay in BF16 or FP32:

  • Routers and gates: a small error in the logit swaps the expert chosen by top-k, and the computation changes discretely. GLM computes the router logits in FP32 (moe_router_dtype float32).
  • Indexers: they choose which KV blocks will be read (chapter Sparse attention). A selection error is not corrected later.
  • Recurrence gates and convolutions (in_proj_a/b, GDN's conv1d; chapter Linear attention): the error enters the recurrent state and accumulates with every token.
  • Residual stream mixes (Qwen's HyperConnections, GLM's and DeepSeek's mHC; chapter Hyper-connections): they don't route anything, but they combine the several copies of the residual in every layer. Qwen puts them in FP8 only in the FP8-dense variant, and DeepSeek computes them in FP32.
  • MLA's kv_b_proj: in decode, weight absorption reads this matrix directly (chapter MLA). Exl3Config refuses to quantize it, and the mla group of the dense FP8 leaves it out.
  • Norms, embeddings and MTP/drafters (PLE and MTP are covered in the chapter N-gram Memory (PLE) and MTP): tiny, multiplicative or tied to acceptance. GLM's own official FP8 leaves embeddings, lm_head, all of KDA, the indexer, kv_b_proj, norms, routers and vision in BF16.

In the figure below, the tower is one pass through the model, from bottom to top, with each piece colored by its format. Hover over (or tap) a piece to read why it is at that precision. The “variant” switch turns on the option each recipe offers on top of the checkpoint. For GLM it starts on, because that is how the recipe is served by default.

The benchmark that didn't see it: issue #18

With q4, GSM8K and HumanEval, both in English, didn't budge. A user measured something else and reported it with numbers: on short coding prompts in French, the reply often loses the end of turn and runs to max_tokens, in 12 of 48 replies, against 1 of 48 with bf16 or fp8. The recipe documented the case in v1.3.2 and recommends DENSE=fp8 for prompts that aren't in English. The README speaks of ~10% less decode speed; in the measurements above, FP8 trails q4 by 9–12% (our own math). Three lessons from this chapter meet here. Emitting the end token is a discrete decision, like choosing an expert: it only takes the right logit losing by a little for the whole behavior to change (interpretation; the issue measures the symptom, not the cause). A search that minimizes the weight error doesn't know which inputs matter, and a calibration only helps if the data looks like real use, language included. And the test has to measure what can break, here the fraction of replies that never finish. These are steps 5 and 6 of the list at the end of this section, and the chapter Measuring the damage builds this kind of instrument.

Why. Each item falls into one of the sensitive categories: routing (gates), selection (indexer), recurrence gates and convolution (they feed the state), MTP (acceptance), norms. They are negligible bytes next to the dense weights and too risky to round. The resolution test checks 30/30 prefixes (for example, in_proj_ba → excluded and lm_head → FP8).

Qwen's N-gram table (PLE) shows another pattern: dequantization on lookup. An embedding table doesn't go into a GEMM: it is looked up by rows. With 51.2 B parameters (47.68 GiB in FP8), it makes no sense to dequantize the whole table. It is enough to dequantize the few rows the step looked up.

PLE patch_dequantize_embeddings

What it is. The function in ple_layer_patched.py (:1011-1064) that converts only the looked-up rows of the PLE to BF16. Value. It has two branches. In the FP8 branch, it does emb.to(bf16) * weight_scale, with a single global scale per tensor. In the NVFP4 branch, which exists both on-device and in offload, it splits the nibbles, passes each one through the E2M1 table (the FP4 variant with 2 exponent bits and 1 mantissa bit: 0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 6) with bit 3 as the sign, and multiplies by the two scale levels, the E4M3 block scale every 16 and the global weight_scale_2. Why. Only the rows used in the step become BF16. The table stays packed in memory: 47.7 GiB in FP8 (≈ 23.8 GiB per GPU with TP=2) or 26.8 GiB in total in the packed NVFP4 version (90 bytes per row). In BF16 it would be ≈ 95.4 GiB (≈ 102 GB). Without the dispatch patch, the stock resolver would build the PLE in BF16 and loading would break.

DeepSeek: the vendor's format, almost untouched

DeepSeek-V4-Flash arrives quantized by the vendor itself, and the recipe serves it that way. The 256 routed experts per layer are in MXFP4 (fp4_e8m0_k32, 4.25 bits), and b12x runs them as W4A16 (dequantizes to BF16 and uses BF16 MMA) because its tensor-core FP4 path only accepts a modelopt_nvfp4 source. In decode this costs nothing, because the step is bandwidth-bound. In prefill it does cost: the alternative analyzed in the recipe report is a W4A8, MXFP4 × MXFP8 straight on the tensor core (mma … kind::mxf8f6f4 accepts E2M1 × E4M3 with E8M0 block scales, and “the checkpoint's format is the hardware-native one”), or else re-quantizing the experts to NVFP4. The estimate is prefill +30–60% on prompts of up to 32K, and none of it is implemented (fable5-1-report.md:295-300). The remaining linears are in FP8 with 128×128 blocks and UE8M0 scales, with activations quantized dynamically per block of 128. The KV is FP8 with a UE8M0 scale every 64, which the model learned in QAT, and the RoPE part stays in BF16. lm_head and embeddings are BF16, and mHC and the router scores run in FP32.

How to decide the formats of your recipe

  1. Draw up the byte budget. Add up weights per rank, desired KV, recurrent state and system headroom. Then estimate the bytes read per decode step by component. The biggest bandwidth consumer is the first candidate to lose bits, and it is not always the biggest memory consumer.
  2. Read what the checkpoint already ships with. quantization_config, hf_quant_config.json and the safetensors headers tell you each module's format and the names of the scales. That is how Qwen found out that the official FP8 doesn't quantize the dense weights.
  3. Split by sensitivity. Experts at 4 bits; dense weights in BF16, weight-only FP8 or FP8 W8A8; routers, gates, norms, indexers, kv_b_proj, embeddings and MTP in high precision until a measurement proves otherwise.
  4. Choose the granularity and the activation regime. Per channel when the per-rank shapes are not multiples of the block. Dynamic per-token or per-block scale when there is no calibration. Weight-only when the activation error outweighs the FLOP gain.
  5. Calibrate when going below 8 bits. Use a set that resembles real usage and a method that minimizes output error (GPTQ/AWQ/LDLQ). A synthetic Hessian is only acceptable where error is cheap, as in a drafter.
  6. Verify at two levels. First the weight error (rel RMSE, cosine, a threshold that fails the conversion). Then the output error against the reference (KLD, tasks, drafter acceptance), with negative and positive controls.

Up to here the ruler was almost always integer and uniform. The formats the recipes actually store, FP8 E4M3, FP4 E2M1, MXFP4 and NVFP4, trade the equal steps for a logarithmic ruler and stack scales on two levels. The next chapter opens up these formats bit by bit and shows how the hardware consumes each scale.
