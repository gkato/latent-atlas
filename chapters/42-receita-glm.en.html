Imagine you've just been handed two DGX Sparks, a QSFP cable between them and a 164 GiB checkpoint. In earlier chapters each piece was studied on its own: MoE, MLA, linear attention, EXL3, paged KV, DFlash2, tensor parallel. Now we put the whole thing together. We'll rebuild from scratch, in the order an engineer would, the recipe that serves GLM-5.3-Flash in EXL3 with vLLM, and justify every line of start.sh, of .env and of the vllm serve command. The goal isn't to memorize values. It's to see the chain of constraints that forces each value, so you can redo the reasoning with another model. And, since 2026-10-01, with another engine too: the same recipe now runs on TensorFold . Boxes with that badge show what changes at each step, and the “Update” section, before Step 8, redraws the decision map with the engine swapped. This chapter describes the vLLM recipe at snapshot 94ae731 (2026-09-28); since 2026-10-04 its README opens with “Use the TensorFold recipe” (cafea0f), and where the recipe moved after that, the text says so.

Step 0 · The target and the leftover math

Every recipe starts with a simple question: does it fit? Recapping The three models: GLM-5.3-Flash is a hybrid MoE with ~320 B parameters, 18 B of them active per token according to the model card (~19.5 B by the repository's estimate). Of its 45 layers, 34 are KDA, a linear attention with a delta rule (linear attention), and 11 are MLA without RoPE (NoPE), with sparse attention guided by an indexer (MLA, sparse attention). There's also an MTP block. From layer 3 onward, every MLP is an MoE with 288 routed experts, top-8, plus 1 shared (MoE), plus a vision tower and hyper-connections.

Now, the byte math (quantization). In BF16 it's ~642.6 GB; in the official FP8, 328.3 GB. Neither fits in 2 × 128 GB. The chosen checkpoint quantizes only the routed experts to 4-bit EXL3 (EXL3) and keeps everything else in BF16: 175.6 GB (163.6 GiB) in 120 shards (the snapshot's TR3 checkpoint; since 98cf18f the default is the lab's own Mia-AiLab/GLM-5.3-Flash-EXL3-4bpw-TensorFold, same format, ~164 GiB in 83 shards). The experts are ~97% of the bytes, so quantizing them solves the memory problem without touching the small, sensitive parts. On the KLD panel, TR3 measures 0.0246 nats, the same as the official FP8 on the same stack, with 54% of the bytes (measuring quality).

Before writing the math down, one distinction that decides the role of each term: vLLM has two regimes for sizing the KV pool. By default, the profile run measures weights and activations and hands the KV whatever is left up to \(u\cdot M\). With --kv-cache-memory-bytes, the pool is a fixed number, and vLLM ignores \(u\) when sizing it (KV budget). The recipe uses the second regime.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{(a) profiled pool:}\quad & P_{KV} = u\,M - \tfrac{W}{TP} - A - G - \Omega \\ \text{(b) fixed pool, at runtime:}\quad & \tfrac{W}{TP} + P_{KV} + A + G + \Omega + I \;\le\; M - \text{OS} - \text{headroom} \\ \text{(b) fixed pool, at boot:}\quad & \text{free}_{\text{boot}} \;\ge\; u\,M \end{aligned} \]
  • \(W\): weight bytes (~164 GiB; ~79.65 GiB loaded per rank at TP=2)
  • \(TP\): tensor parallelism degree (2)
  • \(P_{KV}\): pool of KV blocks (in the recipe, fixed at 11 GiB)
  • \(A, G, \Omega\): peak activations, CUDA graphs and workspaces (the indexer and the scratch of E3, the kernel tier that processes the fat experts in prefill; see Step 8)
  • \(I\): memory vLLM itself takes at initialization (CUDA context and the like; ~9 GiB according to the README)
  • \(u\): --gpu-memory-utilization (0.85)
  • \(M\): visible unified memory (~121.69 GiB per GB10)
  • \(\text{OS}\), \(\text{headroom}\): what the operating system uses and the margin the host needs under load
  • \(\text{free}_{\text{boot}}\): free memory found by the startup check (on UMA, MemAvailable)

In regime (a), \(u\) is the knob that sizes the KV: each 0.01 is ~1.2 GiB taken from the host and handed to the pool. It was in this regime, before the fixed pool, that 0.87 took down a head during a 256k prefill (2026-09-06) and util was lowered to 0.85. In regime (b), nothing at runtime grows with \(u\). Consumption is the sum of fixed blocks (weights, the KDA copy, 11 GiB of KV, workspaces, activations and graphs, initialization), and the host's headroom is \(M\) minus that sum. Even so, \(u\) still matters, as a free-memory requirement at boot. vLLM's startup check refuses to come up if free memory is below \(u\cdot M\), and the launcher runs the same math beforehand: the preflight requires MemAvailable ≥ u×M + 2 GiB and, on restart, it waits until u×M + 1.5 GiB. That's why 0.88 booted in only 1 of 4 attempts: 0.88 × 121.69 = 107.09 GiB landed right at the edge of the free memory measured by the worker's check, 105.8–107.1 GiB.

In unified memory (UMA), the GPU and the operating system share the same LPDDR5X: everything vLLM consumes comes out of the kernel, the page cache and the vision encoder. In the figure, the two budgets become adjustable rulers.

Notice three things. The KV pool is the smallest block you actually control: the weights are a given and the host's headroom is non-negotiable. Implementation details cost gigabytes: the stock indexer workspace locks up max_model_len × 40 × 132 B (~4.2 GiB at 850k), versus ~0.1 GiB in rightsize mode. And, with a fixed pool, util sits wherever boot is comfortable: 0.85 asks for 103.4 GiB free, ~2.4 GiB below the measured window.

How much context fits: the math is in block IDs, not bytes

Only the 11 MLA layers grow with length: 656 B of the fp8_ds_mla record + ~33 B from the indexer per layer. That's ~7.6 KB per token per rank, ~7.1 GiB per million tokens. Even so, with the drafter's default pages, an 850k request “needs” 13.56 GiB, and ~7.4 GiB of that doesn't grow with context. Where does that fixed share come from? Not from the state itself: a request's KDA state is ~76 MiB (34 layers × 2.34 MB per rank), and the drafter's 2048-token window, ~20 MiB. The answer lies in how vLLM counts the pool.

In a hybrid model, the hybrid KV allocator uses a single BlockPool for all KV groups, with pages of the same size in bytes. Here there are seven groups: MLA (3584-token block, fp8_ds_mla record + indexer), the indexer's kpool tail (one circular block), four Mamba groups holding the state of the 34 KDA layers, and the sliding-window group of the DFlash2 drafter. A block ID is one 3584-token page across the 11 MLA layers + the 11 indexer ones:

\[ 1\ \text{ID} = 11 \times \underbrace{3584 \times 656}_{2{,}351{,}104\ \text{B}} + 11 \times \underbrace{896 \times 132}_{118{,}272\ \text{B}} = 27{,}163{,}136\ \text{B} \approx 25.9\ \text{MiB} \]
  • \(3584 \times 656\): MLA page (mla_page=2351104 in the boot log)
  • \(896 \times 132\): indexer page, one 132 B entry per pool of 4 tokens

Every group pays per ID, whether the page is full or not. Each Mamba group keeps the states of 9 KDA layers (~2.34 MB each) inside whole IDs and reserves several per request: the current state, the speculative ones and the superseded ones that can't be freed yet. The drafter, with 64-token blocks in slot-share, takes dozens. The log of the kv_capacity_log overlay shows this math in a 1M boot (2026-08-31): a 1M request reserved 280 MLA IDs + 1 kpool + 4 × 9 from the Mamba groups + ~97 from the drafter = 414 IDs. A 3584-token segment kept in the prefix cache costs 38 IDs: 1 MLA + 4 Mamba + 33 from the drafter.

Apply the same math to 850k. The 13.56 GiB are 536 IDs: 238 MLA (\(\lceil 850{,}000/3584 \rceil\)) and ~298 from the other groups. Those ~298 IDs are the “fixed” ~7.4 GiB, pure page overhead. Compact pages raise the drafter's block from 64 to 896 tokens, the largest multiple of 64 that divides 3584 and fits in the page, and the same window now fits in a handful of IDs. The 850k request drops to ~299 IDs. The 11 GiB pool holds 434 IDs: 434/536 = 0.81× (the earlier “doesn't fit”) and 434/299 = 1.45×, the measured 1,233,779 tokens. The math is ours, done from the published pools, and it reproduces the other measured points: 14 GiB = 553 IDs gives 1.03× without compact pages and 1.85× with them; 15 GiB gives 1.11× without. Not a single byte of VRAM was saved; only the demand for IDs went down. The README records the same trend: in one measured configuration, the IDs reserved per max-length request fell from 180 to 121 (−32.78%), and the drafter's admission, from 65 to 6 IDs.

Trap: lowering the context “to free up slots”

Reducing MAX_MODEL_LEN doesn't fit more conversations in this hybrid stack. The fixed ID share of each request (Mamba, kpool, drafter) doesn't shrink with length, and --max-model-len is a ceiling, not a reservation. What limits you is the sum of live tokens, and of IDs, in the pool.

On TensorFold, “does it fit?” has another ruler

In the TensorFold version of the recipe, the leftover math starts from what the system says is free: budget = MemAvailable at startup − a 14.5 GiB reserve (MEMORY_RESERVE_GIB). There's no util and no profile run. The engine estimates memory from the model's geometry and, if the requested window doesn't fit, refuses the configuration before loading the weights and reports the largest window that does; start.sh restarts once with it. The KV record shrinks, because there's no pad: 528 B per token and DSA layer, instead of 656 B. And capacity isn't counted in block IDs but in contiguous ranges of 2,048 tokens: at the measured start, the pool held 2,922,496 tokens for 1,048,576-token windows, shared by up to 4 requests (extents in the paged KV chapter; the full math is in the TensorFold recipe chapter).

The decision map: why each line exists

From the outside, a recipe looks like an arbitrary list of flags. Up close, almost none of them is a free choice: each flag is the consequence of a physical or software constraint. Think of a subway map. Each line is a constraint (“the weights don't fit”, “the GB10 has only one sparse-MLA kernel”, “CPU and GPU share the RAM”…) and each station is a decision that constraint imposes. The transfers are the points where two constraints cross. That's where the sneakiest bugs live.

Follow the speculation line and watch how a high-level decision (“use DFlash2 with k=7”) drills all the way down to a kernel number, EXL3_TEMP_ROWS_FUSED=32. A decode step with 4 sequences verifies 4 × (7 + 1) = 32 tokens. With top-8, each token visits a given expert at most once. In the worst case, all 32 tokens in the step pick the same expert, and it receives 32 rows. Up to that limit, the fused MoE runs in a single CUDA-graph-compatible launch. An expert that receives more rows than TEMP_ROWS in a step becomes a fat expert and goes to the prefill tiers (E2/E3). This is the kind of chain you need to be able to rebuild on your own.

The 32 comes back, through another chain

On TensorFold, the same number returns by another path. Copy drafts propose up to 15 tokens, so a verify window has up to 16 rows per request: the pending token and 15 drafts. With 4 requests that would be 64 rows, and the engine trims the longest tails until the combined window fits in 32. There is one CUDA graph for each count from 1 to 32 rows. Each row produces an fp32 partial of 4,096 values, or 16 KiB, so the whole window is 512 KiB, exactly the ceiling the recipe gives the one-shot RDMA all-gather (TF_ROCE_MAX_KB=512, worth +1.4% on code with 4 requests). The constraint is the same (a verification batch needs a graph and a fast transport), but the chain is different.

Step 1 · Nodes, accounts and the launcher that orchestrates it all

The topology is the simplest possible in distributed inference: two nodes, one GPU each, tensor parallelism of degree 2 (parallelism). The head (rank 0) runs the OpenAI server, the EngineCore (the process that runs the scheduler loop) and GPU 0's worker. The worker (rank 1) runs only GPU 1's worker, in headless mode. Every layer has two all-reduces, Megatron-style: one after the attention output projection (the KDA or MLA o_proj) and another after the MLP/MoE. Each one sums the partial halves from both ranks and crosses the ConnectX-7 cable over RoCEv2 (NCCL & RDMA).

So there are two traffic planes. The inference plane (NCCL over RDMA verbs, across the CX7) is what sets the speed. The orchestration plane, SSH from head to worker, is how the launcher does everything else: it copies the internal script and the overlays with scp, runs docker run/docker kill remotely and even ships the whole image with docker save | ssh docker load. That's why SSH has to work in BatchMode: no password, no prompt, and failing fast (ConnectTimeout=15).

The network: pinning interfaces, HCAs and GIDs per rank

The inference plane doesn't configure itself. In the kit, the 10.0.0.x addresses are loopback/management aliases, fine for the rendezvous but not for data. If you let NCCL and gloo pick the interface, ncclCommInitRank hangs. That's why the launcher pins three things per rank: the bootstrap Ethernet interface, the RDMA HCA that carries the all-reduces, and the RoCE v2 GID index. “Per rank” matters because the two nodes use different ports of the same QSFP cable. The launcher is a program, not a convenience script

start.sh is over 3,000 lines long because it takes on a responsibility that usually stays implicit: guaranteeing that both ranks get exactly the same configuration, and failing before taking down a healthy pair when something is wrong. Four mechanisms take care of that:

  • Explicit precedence. The caller's exports are captured before source .env and re-exported afterward: KEY=value ./start.sh beats the file, even with an explicit empty value (configuration precedence).
  • Empty ≠ unset. ${VAR-default} only replaces an unset variable; an empty one stays and means “stock” or an error. ${VAR:-default} replaces both. It's a one-character difference with a huge effect.
  • Validation before stop. Numeric ranges and the integrity of every mounted overlay are checked before taking down the pair that's serving.
  • Mutual exclusion. start, restart and stop compete for a flock on logs/cluster.lock.

Try MM_IMAGE_TOKENS with “export empty”. Since the script uses ${MM_IMAGE_TOKENS-2048}, the empty value survives, the --mm-processor-kwargs flag is omitted and vLLM falls back to the checkpoint's 8,000 tokens per image, above the encoder cache's 7,168. MAX_MODEL_LEN, which uses :-, falls back to the default. ABLIT is the deliberate exception: the launcher forces it to 0 after the .env, so only a caller export turns abliteration on (abliteration).

Step 2 · The image: pinned base, patches and stamp

The published vLLM image that already knows GLM-5.3-Flash can't serve it on the GB10. On the first forward it dies with pe_dim must be 64 for fp8_ds_mla. The reason: the only sparse-MLA kernel for SM12x expects a 64-dimension RoPE block, and this model is NoPE (qk_rope_head_dim=0). On top of that, the image doesn't know the exl3 method, doesn't ship DFlash2 and hangs in FlashInfer's autotune. The recipe fixes all of it in Docker layers, each one verifiable. It's the same idea as recipe engineering, applied here case by case.

Three principles show up in every layer. The first is pinning by digest: ARG BASE=…@sha256:905c0293… pins bytes, not a name. A tag can be republished; a digest can't. All the patches are exact anchors on top of that vLLM (0.1.dev20051+g487ecf187). The second is being fail-closed: every substitution requires exactly one occurrence of the target snippet and, at the end, a block of asserts rereads the source code with inspect.getsource to prove the patch “took”. A different upstream breaks the build, instead of silently producing a wrong image. The third is build = boot: the overlays that start reapplies also run during the build, in the same order and after the CPU tests, so that a freshly built image is identical to an image patched at boot.

Inside the NoPE → 576 patch

The FLASHINFER_MLA_SPARSE_SM120 kernel has a fixed geometry: a 512-dim latent + 64 RoPE dims = a 576-dim query. The patch uses rope_pad=64 when qk_rope_head_dim==0, does q = F.pad(q, (0, 64)) and writes a zeroed k_pe. It's exact, because zeros don't change \(q^\top k\). The cost is 128 wasted bytes per token per layer (656 B instead of 528 B). The remaining patches fix the indexer (511 pools × 4 + 3 tail = 2047 ≤ 2048 candidates, page 64, kernel block [64]), turn off FlashInfer's autotune and restrict PDL to SM9x/10x, because on SM12x it races with the KDA state kernels. Concepts in MLA, sparse attention and kernels.

It's worth watching the patch in motion, because it's the central architecture change in the recipe. For each token and MLA layer, the kernel reads a 656 B record: the 512-dimension NoPE latent in FP8 (1 B each), 4 FP32 scales (one per 128-dimension block) and 64 BF16 RoPE values. GLM-5.3-Flash has no RoPE, so those 128 B are zeros written just to complete the geometry. The same happens on the query side: \(q \in \mathbb{R}^{512}\) gets 64 zeros and becomes a 576-dimensional vector. In the product \(q^\top k\), the pad pairs contribute \(0 \cdot 0 = 0\), and the score is exactly that of the original NoPE MLA.

Making sure both nodes run the same image

At start, the ensure_image function begins from a simple fact: “the same tag” doesn't guarantee “the same bytes”. So it checks three things. The first is the recipe stamp: if the repository's SHA-256 doesn't match the LABEL, the image is rebuilt. The second is identity across nodes: head and worker are compared by an image key, and if the worker diverges it gets the image by pull or over SSH. The third is the self-check on the GPU. Patches at build time, an image named after the hash

The TensorFold version takes “build = boot” to the extreme: there are no runtime overlays. The 53 patches are unified diffs against the site-packages of TensorFold v0.6.0, installed on top of NVIDIA's PyTorch container. They're applied at image build, in name order, with patch -p0 --forward. A patch that doesn't apply breaks the build, and a verification import of the engine's modules seals the image. The tag is the hash of the patches themselves (SHA-256 of patches/*.patch plus the pip extras, 12 characters), and the published image is pulled by digest as long as that hash matches, because “a tag can be moved, a digest cannot”. The price is the opposite of an overlay's. Changing a patch takes another image, and every new TensorFold version means rebasing all 53. In v1.3 of the recipe, three patches became part of TensorFold itself and left the list. Concept: recipe engineering.

Recreating isn't restarting

Overlays are applied when each container comes up. That's why a docker restart isn't the same as ./start.sh restart, which does a stop (kill + rm) and brings up new containers with the overlays and flags remounted. To change a flag or undo a hotfix, recreate the pair.

Same weights, checked parity

In the TensorFold version, the weights are the same, pinned by Hub revision (MODEL_REVISION, DFLASH2_REVISION). Three runtime policies

Three items in the recipe aren't vllm serve flags but policies that overlays install in the scheduler and in the KV coordinator. Each one solves a problem that only shows up under real load.

SpeculationGLM53_ADAPTIVE_K=ema · set 2,4,7

Problem: with a fixed k=7, only 0.34 of the drafts are accepted on prose, and per-position acceptance drops from 0.75 to 0.06 along the block. Always verifying 8 positions per sequence spends target forward passes on drafts that are almost never accepted. Mechanism (overlay no. 18): the drafter keeps proposing 7, but the scheduler verifies only a prefix of 2, 4 or 7. The choice is made by a per-request EMA of the number of accepted drafts (α 0.25, margin 1.0, 4 steps before cutting). The length used is the minimum over the batch, because FLASHINFER_MLA_SPARSE_SM120 and the KDA backend only accept FULL CUDA graphs for uniform decode: every sequence in the step verifies the same number of positions. Hence the extra graphs for 3-, 5- and 8-token queries, whose multiples the launcher adds to the list. It's lossless at temperature 0, because the length is decided from past information and the rejection of each verified position remains exact. Measured (2026-09-08, 8 runs per prompt, 131k and 850k): Silk Road +21%, sky/sunset +13%, hash-map +10%, code +5–15%, counting unchanged. It ships disabled in .env.example (off); the Step 7 concurrency ladder was measured with it on. You can retune it without a reboot through glm53_adaptive_k.json ({"mode":"off"} goes back to k=7). Concept: DFlash2 and adaptive-k.

SchedulerGLM53_MIXED_PREFILL_CHUNK=fair (v5; v7 since #283)

Problem: a newcomer arrives with a long prompt while another conversation is decoding. Both extreme policies fail. skip leaves the newcomer waiting for the whole decode to finish (starvation). 0/off mixes in prefill without limit and makes the incumbent 10–36× slower at TP=2. Mechanism (overlay no. 5, patch_scheduler_decode_floor.py): decodes enter the step first, and only the prefill that contends with them builds up debt. From samples of solo and mixed steps, the overlay fits a step cost \(dt = a + b\cdot n\): a fixed part (~0.3 s per step with prefill on this kit) plus a per-token cost. It then picks the largest rung of the 128…2048 ladder whose estimated \(dt\) fits in GLM53_FAIR_PREFILL_MAX_STEP_MS=2000. Until samples exist, it probes with 256-token chunks. GLM53_FAIR_PREFILL_SHARE=0.30 is the fraction of engine time credited to the contended prefill. Measured (v5, share 0.20, 1000 ms step, 2026-09-15): a 2k newcomer had a TTFT of 11.6 s and a 30k one, 163.6 s, during another conversation's decode, with the incumbent at 20.6–20.9 tok/s versus 24.7 alone. With share 0.30, the 30k newcomer waits ~145 s instead of ~220 s, and the incumbent gets 1.3–1.4× slower (versus 1.16×). The max step went up to 2000 ms because, at 1000 ms, the fit got stuck whenever a 256-token mixed step cost ~1 s. Concept: scheduler and batching.

After the snapshot. Decode-floor v7 (b94fdfd, #283, 2026-09-30) keeps this policy and adds three things. A waiting request whose KV allocation was refused no longer stalls a prefill that could run: it keeps being retried and recovers once memory frees (#246). With --scheduling-policy priority, explicit priorities rank the fair-prefill candidates; under the default FCFS nothing changes (#221). And the installer migrates fail-closed (#180): pristine, v1, v2, v5 and v5+priority installs become v7; v3/v4 and drifted, duplicated or unmarked installs are refused unwritten. The boot line now says fair v7.

Prefix caching--enable-prefix-caching + overlays no. 8 and no. 9

Problem 1: the granularity. A prefix hit only counts at a boundary where every group has stored state. The hybrid coordinator aligns hits to the LCM of the block sizes: MLA 3584, Mamba 16, kernel block 64 → 3584. That's why a ~8k follow-up reuses 7168 tokens (2 pages), a ~12k one reuses 10752 and a ~16k one, 14336. Problem 2: the drafter mistaken for EAGLE. The dflash method counts as use_eagle(), and the coordinator marked all groups as EAGLE. As a result, MLA dropped the last 3584-token page of every hit (eagle block drop). Overlay no. 8 (patch_hybrid_prefix_hit.py) treats only the drafter's window group as EAGLE. With compact pages, the drafter's lookup ends exactly at the boundary: DFlash's context KV at position p is a projection of the target's hidden state at p, whereas in EAGLE it embeds token p+1. Result: a 28,672-token prefix with a 64-token tail had a repeat TTFT of 0.302 s versus 2.6–3.4 s (−90.02%). Problem 3: the drafter eats the cache. Of the 38 IDs in a cached segment, 33 belonged to the drafter, and those blocks went to the tail of the LRU, pushing MLA and Mamba toward eviction. Overlay no. 9 (patch_apc_per_group_retention.py) gives the drafter's group its own retention. With GLM53_APC_RETENTION_INTERVAL_SWA=0, it keeps only the reachable boundaries, with low eviction priority. In TP2 the variable is left empty (it inherits the dense grid); the TP3/TP4 templates and the long-coding profile use 0. Concept: prefix caching.

The three policies on the other engineDRAFT_POLICY=fnc7:0.3 · MULTI_PREFILL=1 · SHARED_PREFIX=1

Speculation. With no FULL uniform-decode graphs to respect, TensorFold picks each request's depth every round. That's up to 7 DFlash2 drafts, as long as the product of the confidences, computed under the request's own keyed noise, stays above 0.3 (fnc7:0.3). Copy drafts come before them. Because verification compares each draft with the target's keyed sample, the reply is the same under any policy. Measured against the previous policy, fc5:0.3: prose 47.2 → 50.6 tok/s, code 51.7 → 55.5 (two boots each).

Scheduler. A prompt chunk never shares a forward with decode rows. The engine alternates chunk and round, half of the iterations each (TF_GLM_FILL_SHARE 0.5), with chunks of 1,024 rows while other requests decode and of 2,048 when the prompt is alone. Prompts that wait together go into the same chunk (MULTI_PREFILL=1), because a short forward costs a nearly fixed ~0.12 s on the two Sparks: with 4 prose requests, TTFT fell from 590 to 340 ms and the aggregate rose from 103.4 to 108.8 tok/s.

Prompt reuse. Instead of hashed blocks aligned to 3,584 tokens, the engine keeps whole states at chosen points of the 64-token grid: at the end of the prompt, together with the head's logits row (the replay), at the end of the system block and where the prompt stops matching a kept one. An identical 64k prompt: from 34 s to under 0.07 s. A new conversation with the same 7.9k system prompt: from 4.24 s to 0.13 s. Details in the exact engine chapter.

Policy environment, identical on both ranks

Beyond the flags, dozens of variables travel as -e to both containers: GLM53_DENSE_FP8=all, GLM53_KDA_BF16_LARGE_M=1, EXL3_FAT_GROUPED=1, EXL3_TEMP_ROWS_FUSED=32, GLM53_INDEXER_WORKSPACE=rightsize, GLM53_DRAFT_KV_COMPACT=1, GLM53_MIXED_PREFILL_CHUNK=fair (share 0.30, max step 2000 ms), DEFAULT_MAX_NEW_TOKENS=65536, VLLM_EXECUTE_MODEL_TIMEOUT_SECONDS=1800… They're identical because “a rank that captures different CUDA graphs hangs NCCL after PIECEWISE”. PIECEWISE and FULL are vLLM's two CUDA graph modes: in the first, attention runs eagerly between captured pieces; in the second, the entire forward pass is a single graph (CUDA graphs & warmup). If one rank has graphs the other lacks, the two launch different collectives and NCCL waits forever.

Step 6 · Bring-up: the boot timeline

./start.sh follows a fixed sequence: preflight → image → download → optional build of the dense-h3 pair → pack validation → drafter → sync/NFS → internal scripts → docker run of the worker (--headless) and then of the head → wait for /health → shape warmup and canary → READY banner. The worker comes up first, and the head, which hosts the rendezvous and the API, follows right after. The figure follows a real restart: on 2026-09-19 it dropped from 259 to 122 s, and /health, counted from docker run, from 230 to 99 s.

Notice that the biggest block is the weight stream: 39 s, with each rank reading the whole checkpoint at 5.5–5.9 GB/s, close to the NVMe's O_DIRECT ceiling. It's only that fast because InstantTensor reads straight from disk. The only big lever left, according to the repository, is a loader that reads only each rank's half of the experts (~15–18 s less), not yet implemented. The cuts that took the restart from 259 to 122 s were almost all about waste, not computation. A .pth imported vLLM in every Python interpreter (100 → 6 s to the first log line). NVIDIA's JIT cache died with every docker rm and recompiled a 31 MiB DFlash2 cubin (30 → 0 s). With CG_ESTIMATE=0, the KV profile did a “rehearsal” graph capture whose result was thrown away (18 → 7 s). And the stop became kill-first and parallel.

A slower boot, an earlier refusal

The TensorFold version's ./start.sh has five visible stages. The first is setup, which only runs when something changed. The second is the checks: arguments validated by the engine's own parser in a container without a GPU, the link, the port and free memory, with a warning below ~110 GiB on either Spark. Then come the launch (rank 1 over ssh, then rank 0 with the API), the load and a smoke test with the same “Reply with OK.” as the canary, at temperature 0, without thinking and with 32 tokens. Loading takes 2–6 min for ~80 GiB per Spark (~2.2 min with the worker's weights over NFS), against the 122 s of restart with InstantTensor, and the first start of each image still compiles kernels. In exchange, a window that doesn't fit is refused before the weights, and the launcher restarts once with the largest window that does. On vLLM with a profiled pool, a pool that couldn't hold the 850k request only showed up after the profile run, with the weights already loaded.

Verifying by hash

In the TensorFold version, the canary is still there (the smoke test), but verification gains a stronger test, because the engine promises identical bits. "draft": false on a request returns the serial reference decoding. The same request with drafts, served alongside three others or resumed from a kept prompt, must give the same reply, bit for bit. The published checks: drafted = serial in 6 of 6 cases, concurrency in 22 of 22, and replies served 4 at a time identical to the ones served one at a time in 11 of 11 staggered cases and 11 of 11 sent in a burst. Quality was measured with the lossy defaults (FP8 KV and q4 dense weights): GSM8K 98.0% on 250 problems and HumanEval 97.6% on 164. And speed was measured with sparkDash through the API, from another machine, with the GPU clocks capped at 2,200 MHz. Interpretation: capping the clock removes boost variation between runs and between ranks, and in a two-rank engine the slower rank sets the pace of every round.

TensorFold is written per model family. Here one question from this chapter matters: if every flag follows from a constraint, what happens to the chain when the engine changes?

Constraints that stay, constraints that vanish

The answer splits constraints into two kinds. The physical and model ones don't depend on the engine: 164 GiB of weights (~80 GiB per rank), 128 GB of unified memory per node, a CX7 cable over RoCE v2, a hybrid of 34 KDA layers, 11 MLA layers without RoPE and an MoE of 288 experts, and a bandwidth-bound decode. They dictate the same decisions on both engines: EXL3 only on the experts, TP=2 with one rank per Spark, FP8 KV to fit context, speculation with DFlash2. The others are engine constraints, born from fitting the model into the software that serves it. The 512→576 pad exists because SM12x's only sparse-MLA kernel has a 576 geometry. The 3,584-token pages exist because the hybrid allocator demands the same page size in bytes for the KDA state and for the latent. Adaptive-k uses the minimum over the batch because FULL graphs only accept uniform decode. And fair v5 exists because chunked prefill mixes prefill and decode in the same step. In an engine written for the family, none of those four constraints exists: the pad simply vanishes, and the other three stations are taken over by the new engine's own decisions.

In exchange, the new engine brings constraints of its own. In v1.3.2, GLM's CUDA engine required exactly two ranks (no TP3 or TP4), served at most 4 requests at a time (MAX_SEGS = 4 in the code) and exposed a smaller API: no logprobs and no n>1 (HTTP 400), penalties accepted and ignored, and no API key. The first two constraints moved later (3 Sparks since v1.4, up to 8 requests since v1.5) and the API gained Anthropic's /v1/messages (v1.9); logprobs, n>1 and an API key are still absent (what changed since v1.3.2). It also imposes a contract that changes the design of almost every kernel, exactness, the subject of the next subsection. The figure puts both versions on the same subway map. Pull the lever and see which stations stay, which swap and which one vanishes.

Three readings. The weights line barely changes: the checkpoint is the same, and only the handling of the dense projections swaps (per-channel FP8 on vLLM, 4 bits with an MSE-chosen clip on TensorFold). The attention line shrinks the most: without the fixed-geometry kernel, the pad vanishes and the record drops from 656 to 528 B. And the output line belongs entirely to the engine. None of its stations comes from the hardware, and that's where the two engines diverge most in philosophy.

The output contract: drafted equals serial

On vLLM, speculation is “lossless” in distribution. Rejection sampling accepts a draft with probability \(\min(1, p/q)\) and resamples from the residual, so the output has the target's distribution, but a reply's bits can change with the batch, with the kernel path and with speculation. TensorFold swaps that for a sequence contract, with keyed sampling. The Gumbel noise that decides each position's token is a function of the seed (the request's seed or the prompt's SHA-256), of the absolute position and of the token id. Verifying a draft becomes comparing: the target samples every row of the window with the key and accepts the drafts as long as they equal the sample. Three equalities follow: reply with drafts = serial decoding, 4 requests together = each one alone and resumed prompt = prompt computed from scratch.

For a recipe, the consequence is practical: every optimization gets an objective test, “did the reply's hash change?”. That is what lets the engine try aggressive draft policies, batch requests into the same window, resume kept prompts and swap the transport between the Sparks without measuring quality again after every change. The price is in the kernels. One row's arithmetic can't depend on the other rows of the call, so split-K and tiles are fixed by the weight's shape, and the two ranks' partials are always added in the same order. Exactness is against the engine itself, not against the checkpoint. The recipe declares three defaults that trade bits for speed and for the 1M window: DENSE=q4 and KV=fp8 are lossy, and KDA_CHUNKED=1 stays close to the serial kernel without having its bits. Even with them, the three equalities still hold, and DENSE=bf16 KV=bf16 KDA_CHUNKED=0 serves the checkpoint as it is, with a 163,840-token window with vision on (196,608 with VISION=0).

Two ranks, one truth

Steps 1 to 4 revolve around one requirement: both ranks must execute exactly the same calls. The vLLM recipe guarantees that by construction. The launcher generates the same internal script, applies the same overlays in the same order and forwards the same environment to both containers. If a divergence slips through, nothing checks it at startup, and it shows up as a stalled NCCL, because the ranks captured different graphs. TensorFold also builds parity, passing every TENSORFOLD_*, TF_GLM_* and TF_ROCE_* variable to both ranks, and it checks it too. Before loading the weights, each rank packs into a vector of integers the settings that change collectives or drafts (drafter, context, prompt grid, copy drafts, hyper-connection split, --parallel…). Both all-gather that vector and refuse to start if they differ. That's checked parity. After startup, only rank 0 decides: everything that depends on time, on clients or on placement becomes one message of operations per iteration, which rank 1 applies through the same code. And after an error, the two ranks can no longer be trusted to agree: the broken state latches, and every later call fails until both restart.

Notice the pattern. When the divergence is in the bytes (a stale image, a typo in an argument), both engines defend themselves the same way: they compare by content and validate before stopping what's serving. The difference is in the trickiest case, the rank that comes up with another configuration. On vLLM it passes every check and only reveals itself as a stalled NCCL; on TensorFold it doesn't even get to load the weights. That's not the merit of a more careful launcher. An engine that promises identical bits on both ranks needs to know, already at startup, that both will make the same calls.

Which engine for your recipe?

Use this chapter's ruler: list the constraints and see which engine satisfies them. At v1.3.2, TensorFold made sense with exactly two nodes, up to four requests, a 1M context, a focus on single-conversation decode and history reuse (the agent pattern), with no need for logprobs, n>1 or authentication on the server itself, and accepting the lossy defaults or the exact mode's smaller window. vLLM won when the recipe needed 3 or 4 nodes, the full API surface, many simultaneous requests, abliteration or fast restarts. The line has moved since: the author recommends TensorFold for everyone, and it now covers 3 Sparks, 8 requests and abliteration; what still points to vLLM is 4 nodes off a branch, logprobs, n>1, a server-side API key and fast restarts. Either way, the engine is one more line of the recipe, and it also needs a constraint that justifies it.

What to take into your own recipe

Strip away the numbers and a method remains. (1) Do the leftover math before anything else, with the host's headroom as a first-class constraint. (2) For every flag, write down the constraint that forces it: if you can't, the flag is probably folklore. (3) Pin everything that has bytes (image, vLLM, extensions, weights, drafter, binaries) and stamp what you built. (4) Make the patches fail-closed and idempotent, applied in the same order on every rank. (5) Validate before stopping what's working. (6) Don't trust /health: use logs with a contract, a canary and A/B/A. The recipe-from-scratch tutorial applies exactly this method to a new model.
