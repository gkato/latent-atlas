Data: Module 1 becomes teacher and jury

Before they become training machines, the Sparks serve the Module 1 models, all with an OpenAI API on port 8888 (GLM-5.3-Flash-EXL3, qwen3.8-flash-next, deepseek-v4-flash-vision-exp). Since each recipe takes both nodes, they run one at a time. They play two roles: the teacher writes the answers, and a jury of judges (LLM as judge) decides which ones are good.

License first

Before publishing anything derived, check whether the teacher model's terms allow using its outputs to train another model.

  1. Seeds from the platform itself. The glossaries of the 50 Module 1 chapters (0 to 49) add up to ~1,170 unique terms (the research, done when there were 48, counted 1,079), and docs/APANHADO.md lists 1,484 recipe parameters. Each term becomes 2 to 4 questions.
  2. Teacher answers. GLM answers each question 4 times (n=4, temperature 0.9), which makes it possible to build pairs later. Cost [estimate]: 3,000 questions × 4 × ~400 tokens ≈ 4.8 M tokens; at ~100–150 tok/s aggregate, 9 to 13 hours of teacher time.
  3. Pairwise judging, with position swapping, by a judge from another family, with a rubric: technical correctness, answering what was asked, numbers and units, conciseness; ignore length and formatting.
  4. Cleaning and decontamination, and only then the split.

Why DeepSeek is the judge

Two biases decide who sits on the jury. Position bias is strong: with ChatGPT as the judge, just by reordering the answers, Vicuna-13B “won” 66 of 80 queries (Wang et al., 2023). Self-preference makes the evaluator favor its own outputs, with a strength proportional to its ability to recognize itself (Panickssery et al., 2024). The teacher is GLM and the student is Qwen3.8-27B, a relative of Qwen3.8-Flash-Next. Excluding both families leaves a single impartial judge: DeepSeek. GLM and Qwen join, at most, as secondary judges on a PoLL-style panel (Verga et al., 2024), with their agreement reported separately.

The script runs a tournament per question. Each of the \(\binom{4}{2} = 6\) pairs is judged twice, as (A, B) and as (B, A), and a win only counts when the judge agrees with itself in both orders:

\[ s(i,j)=\begin{cases}+1 & v(i,j)=\text{A}\ \text{and}\ v(j,i)=\text{B}\\ -1 & v(i,j)=\text{B}\ \text{and}\ v(j,i)=\text{A}\\ 0 & \text{otherwise}\end{cases}\qquad S_i=\sum_{j\neq i} s(i,j) \]
  • \(v(i,j)\): the judge's verdict with answer \(i\) in position A and \(j\) in position B
  • \(S_i\): answer \(i\)'s balance in the tournament (from −3 to +3 with 4 answers)
  • sft.jsonl gets the best answer if \(S_{\text{best}} \ge 1\)
  • pref.jsonl gets (best, worst) if \(S_{\text{best}} - S_{\text{worst}} \ge 2\): the minimum margin discards ambiguous pairs. Note that the balances sum to zero, so a single consistent verdict is enough for the margin to reach 2: a threshold of 2 only discards the questions where everything tied; 3 or 4 actually filter [our own math]
  • kto.jsonl gets the best with label: true and the worst with label: false

Raise the bias to 0.4. Without swapping, the answer that shows up most often in position A gets free points, and purity drops to around 80% in the figure's model: one pair in five teaches the model to prefer the worse answer. With swapping, the bias turns into a tie and the remaining pairs stay clean (93%). Now raise the minimum margin to 3. Swapping starts to cost real pairs: the yield drops from 85% (no swap) to 67% (with swap), and purity with swapping rises to 95%, versus 83% without. That's the quantity-versus-cleanliness trade-off the threshold controls; with 4 answers and margin 2, as in the recipe, almost every question yields a pair.

Cleaning, decontamination and split by term

Normalize, deduplicate (MinHash or n-grams) and remove from training every item that shares a normalized 13-gram with any evaluation set. The threshold of 13 follows the practice of the GPT-3 report, a number the research itself flags as “from memory; to be checked”. Decontamination matters twice as much here: the teacher may have memorized GSM8K, and “generate problems similar to these” produces near-copies.

Then split train, valid and test_dominio by glossary term, never by row: if the same term shows up in training and in the test, the test measures memorization. Suggested sizes: SFT 3,000–6,000 examples, DPO 1,500–3,000 pairs, KTO 3,000–6,000 labels and a test of 300–500 questions on unseen terms (a CI of ±4–6 points on the win rate). Drop the term column after the split.

The chat template is part of the data

The chat template from chapter 5 is the number-one cause of broken fine-tunes on Qwen3.x. Four decisions, checked against the Qwen3.8-27B template and the TRL code:

  1. Reasoning goes in reasoning_content, never inside content. The template renders the assistant as <think>\n{reasoning_content}\n</think>\n\n{content} and doesn't look for <think> in content: inline reasoning produces two thinking blocks.
  2. assistant_only_loss=True swaps the template for qwen3_8_training.jinja, which always emits the thinking block and marks the output with {% generation %}…{% endgeneration %}. Examples without reasoning_content become <think>\n\n</think>\n\n{content}: the model learns to answer without thinking.
  3. enable_thinking touches the system prompt: when undefined or true, the template injects “Reasoning effort is set to xhigh…”; with false, it doesn't. Train, evaluate and serve with the same chat_template_kwargs.
  4. The eos_token is <|im_end|>. That's right for chat; don't change it.

The empty <think>\n\n</think> block in “no reasoning” mode isn't decoration: with enable_thinking=false, the generation prompt already ends with it. Training like this and serving without thinking is consistent; training like this and serving with thinking produces a model that has forgotten how to think. To preserve reasoning, write it into reasoning_content and mix in a fraction of those examples.

Two LoRA scales: don't copy the LR between frameworks

Chapter 51 introduced LoRA. The detail that breaks recipes is that MLX and PEFT scale the low-rank term differently:

\[ y = W_0x + s\cdot B\,A\,x,\qquad s_{\text{PEFT}} = \frac{\alpha}{r},\qquad s_{\text{MLX}} = \texttt{scale}\ (\text{20 in } \texttt{mlx\_lm.lora},\ \text{10 in } \texttt{mlx-lm-lora}),\qquad \eta_{\text{PEFT}}\cdot s_{\text{PEFT}} \approx \eta_{\text{MLX}}\cdot s_{\text{MLX}} \]
  • \(W_0\): frozen weight; \(A\in\mathbb R^{r\times d_{in}}\) (random: \(U(\pm 1/\sqrt{d_{in}})\) in MLX) and \(B\in\mathbb R^{d_{out}\times r}\) (starts at zero)
  • \(r\): rank; \(\alpha\): PEFT's lora_alpha (default \(r=8,\ \alpha=8 \Rightarrow s=1\))
  • \(\eta\): learning rate. With Adam, the effective step of the product \(sBA\) scales with \(s\times\eta\) [research inference]
  • example: the mlx_lm.lora default (LR 1e-5 × scale 20) is equivalent to PEFT with LR 2e-4 and \(\alpha/r=1\)

By the same math, the mlx-lm-lora default (1e-5 × 10) is equivalent to ~1e-4 on the PEFT scale, too high for DPO, where TRL suggests ~1e-5 for adapters. That's why the Mac DPO uses 1e-6. Another difference: mlx_lm.lora trains the last 16 layers, while mlx_lm_lora.train trains all of them and fuses the adapter at the end by default.

Training memory has four parts: frozen weights, adapter state, activations and logits. The research's math uses the activation formula from Korthikanti et al. (about \(34\,s\,b\,h\) bytes per layer with FlashAttention):

\[ M \approx \underbrace{M_W}_{\text{weights}} + \underbrace{16\,P_{\text{LoRA}}}_{\text{weight+grad+Adam FP32}} + \underbrace{\big(2\,s\,b\,h\,L_t + 34\,s\,b\,h\big)}_{\text{activations with checkpointing}} + \underbrace{2\cdot 4\,b\,s\,V}_{\text{FP32 logits + gradient}},\qquad P_{\text{LoRA}} = r\,L_t\sum_{\text{linears}}(d_{in}+d_{out}) \]
  • \(M_W\): 2.26 GB in 4-bit (in DPO, two 8-bit copies of ≈ 4.3 GB each)
  • \(L_t\): trained layers (16); \(h\) = 2560; \(V\) = 151,936; \(s\): sequence; \(b\): micro-batch
  • in Qwen3-4B, \(\sum(d_{in}+d_{out})\) = 57,344 per layer (q, k, v, o, gate, up, down) → \(r=16,\ L_t=16\): 14.7 M parameters, ≈ 0.24 GB of state
  • without gradient checkpointing, activations become \(34\,s\,b\,h\,L_t\): 11.4 GB at \(s=2048,\ b=4\)

The term that grows the most isn't the model, it's the logits: \(4 \times 1024 \times 151{,}936 \times 4\) B ≈ 2.5 GB, plus the gradient. With the YAML defaults the total lands at ≈ 7–10 GB. Try it in the figure: set batch 8 and sequence 4096. The logits tensor alone becomes 19.9 GB, exceeds the 14.3 GB buffer, and the tank overflows (≈ 48 GB in total); no rank or layer count saves this configuration. Go back to the defaults and turn off grad_checkpoint: activations jump from 0.7 to 5.7 GB. If it blows up, use batch 2 with accumulation 4 (same effective batch), sequence 768 or, in mlx-lm-lora, --sft-loss-type chunked_nll. Time [estimate]: ≈ 15 GFLOP per token (8.0 GFLOP forward + backward through the trained layers and the lm_head + recomputation), or 65–130 tok/s; the ~1.2 M tokens of 600 iterations take 2.5 to 5 hours.

DPO on the Mac: fuse the SFT at 8 bits first

The DPO reference is the model after SFT. Fuse the SFT into its own checkpoint, and at 8 bits, not 4: the LoRA delta is small compared with the step of the 4-bit grid, and part of it would vanish (the merge section shows this cell by cell). mlx_lm_lora.train loads the reference as a second full copy, and two 8-bit copies fit.

# 1) fuse the SFT onto the BF16 base and quantize to 8 bits
mlx_lm.fuse --model mlx-community/Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507-bf16 --adapter-path adapters/mac_sft --save-path models/sft_bf16 --dequantize
mlx_lm.convert --hf-path models/sft_bf16 --mlx-path models/sft_8bit -q --q-bits 8 --q-group-size 64
# 2) DPO: QLoRA on the 8-bit; reference = the same frozen 8-bit
mlx_lm_lora.train --model models/sft_8bit --train --train-mode dpo \
  --data data/mlx_dpo --beta 0.1 --dpo-cpo-loss-type sigmoid \
  --reference-model-path models/sft_8bit \
  --num-layers 16 --batch-size 1 --gradient-accumulation-steps 8 \
  --learning-rate 1e-6 --lora-parameters '{"rank": 16, "dropout": 0.0, "scale": 10.0}' \
  --max-seq-length 1024 --grad-checkpoint --epochs 1 \
  --steps-per-eval 50 --save-every 100 --adapter-path adapters/mac_dpo

At the end, mlx_lm_lora.train fuses by default and writes the fused model (dequantized from the 8-bit) to --adapter-path. To keep only the adapter, pass a YAML with fuse: false (-c cfg/mac_dpo.yaml). If you left the default, adapters/mac_dpo is already the fused model: use it as models/final_bf16 and skip the mlx_lm.fuse in the final block, further below (the base, in that case, is the dequantized 8-bit, not sft_bf16: a small difference).

--dpo-cpo-loss-type accepts sigmoid, hinge, ipo and dpop, and --train-mode goes from sft to ppo, but there's no KTO or SimPO. For KTO on the Mac, convert the labels into pairs (best positive × worst negative) and use DPO. ORPO does without the reference: it drops one of the two 8-bit copies (−4.3 GB; in the figure, from ≈ 11.7 to ≈ 7.4 GB). DPO memory [estimate]: 11–14 GB. Time: ≈ 2 sequences × (15 + 8 GFLOP/token, counting the reference forward) × ~700 tokens ≈ 32 TFLOP, or 16–32 s per pair. Start with 300–500 pairs.

A small GRPO, and the end of the track

RL on the Mac works only with a small model, because every step generates text. With Qwen3-1.7B-4bit, groups of 4, accuracy and format rewards on GSM8K, --grpo-loss-type dr_grpo and --beta 0.0 (the reference isn't even evaluated), each step takes ~0.5–1 min [estimate]. Watch out for mlx-lm-lora's epsilon (clip width): 1e-4, much smaller than the classic 0.2.

At the end, fuse the adapter onto the BF16 version of the stage's starting point and re-quantize with a calibrated method. DWQ (mlx_lm.dwq) distills from BF16 to 4 bits by tuning scales and biases on your data:

mlx_lm.fuse --model models/sft_bf16 --adapter-path adapters/mac_dpo --save-path models/final_bf16 --dequantize
mlx_lm.dwq --model models/final_bf16 --bits 4 --group-size 64 --data-path data/mlx_sft \
  --num-samples 512 --max-seq-length 1025 --batch-size 2 --grad-checkpoint --mlx-path models/final_4bit_dwq
python kl_mlx.py models/final_bf16 models/final_4bit_dwq data/kl_texts.jsonl   # quantization error vs the merge
mlx_lm.server --model models/final_4bit_dwq --port 8080                         # the Latent Atlas copilot, MLX preset

The research's kl_mlx.py computes the mean per-token \(\mathrm{KL}(P\|Q)\) and top-1 agreement. Compare models at the same precision so you don't mix quantization with drift. An alternative to “train, then quantize” is mlx-lm-lora's QAT (--qat-enable --qat-bits 4), which projects the trainable weights onto the quantized grid at every step.

DDP, not FSDP: when the model fits, don't shard it

The reflex would be to use FSDP, as in NVIDIA's multi-node playbook. But the 27B in BF16 takes 55.6 GB, and each Spark sees 121.7 GiB: it fits whole on each node. So the choice is symmetric DDP: each Spark holds the model, processes half of the data and, at each optimizer step, only the LoRA gradients cross the RoCE link (217.6 M parameters at r=32, or 0.87 GB in FP32). With FSDP, every forward, backward and recomputation would all-gather the weights. With 2 ranks, each node receives the half it doesn't have: ≈ 3 all-gathers × 27.8 GB per direction per micro-batch. With accumulation of 16, that's ~1.3 TB per optimizer step, ≈ 67 s at 20 GB/s, as much as the compute itself (39–78 s) [estimate]. With DDP, the compute-to-communication ratio per step is huge, unlike the decode in chapter 38; with FSDP, the cable becomes a bottleneck for no reason.

Switch to FSDP and watch the cable become the bottleneck: the pink all-gather bar grows as long as the compute bar, and the weights per node drop by half without anyone needing that. Don't plan with NVIDIA's fine-tuning “peaks”

Likelihood displacement deserves an explanation. DPO doesn't optimize the probability of the chosen response; it optimizes the difference between the two responses' implicit rewards:

\[ \hat r_\theta(x,y) = \beta\,\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)},\qquad \texttt{rewards/margins} = \hat r_\theta(x,y_w) - \hat r_\theta(x,y_l) \]
  • \(\hat r_\theta\): DPO's implicit reward (it's what TRL logs under rewards/*)
  • \(y_w\), \(y_l\): chosen and rejected response; \(\beta\): strength of the anchor to the reference
  • logps/chosen = \(\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)\): it can fall while the margin rises, if \(\log\pi_\theta(y_l)\) falls even faster

If both probabilities fall, the mass goes somewhere, sometimes to responses with the opposite meaning. In the example from the paper that named the phenomenon (arXiv 2410.08847), training to refuse reduced Llama-3-8B-Instruct's refusal rate from 74.4% to 33.4%. Anchoring the chosen response with an NLL term (loss_type=["sigmoid","sft"]), raising β or filtering out very similar pairs (the paper's CHES score) mitigates the problem.

Pick DPO and the “problem” scenario, and watch the order in which the lamps light up: the logps/chosen lamp turns amber and then red well before the rewards/accuracies one. The drop in the chosen response's log-prob is the early warning; by the time reward accuracy touches 1.0, the damage is done. In GRPO, the same role belongs to mean length, which shoots up before entropy collapses.

Merge: putting the adapter back into the weights

Once past the gates, the adapter goes back inside the matrix. The merge is the computation \(W' = W_0 + s\,B\,A\): after it, inference pays nothing for LoRA. But the computation is only exact if \(W_0\) is the weight the adapter saw, at a precision able to represent \(s\,BA\). Hence three rules:

  1. Load the base in BF16, never the 4-bit from QLoRA training. merge_and_unload on 4-bit weights dequantizes, adds and re-quantizes, losing part of the delta.
  2. In MLX, use --dequantize. Without it, on a quantized base, mlx_lm.fuse dequantizes the layer, adds the delta and re-quantizes with the same bits, group and mode, recomputing scales and biases from the merged weight (RTN, no calibration).
  3. Save everything that isn't a weight: tokenizer, chat_template.jinja, preprocessor_config.json (it's a VLM) and generation_config.json.

Rule 2 comes down to simple math. fuse recomputes the group's scale from the merged weight (\(\Delta' \approx \Delta\), because \(\Delta W\) barely moves the group's min and max) and re-rounds everything. Deltas below ~\(\Delta/2\) get lost and, since the values were already on the old grid and now fall on a slightly shifted grid, the re-rounding also adds new noise. The inequality remains the rule of thumb:

\[ \begin{gathered} \hat w' = \Delta'\,\text{round}\!\Big(\frac{\hat w + \Delta W - m'}{\Delta'}\Big) + m',\qquad \Delta = \frac{\max W_g - \min W_g}{2^{b}-1} \\ |\Delta W| \lesssim \tfrac{\Delta}{2}\ \Rightarrow\ \text{the delta is lost in rounding} \end{gathered} \]
  • \(\hat w = q\,\Delta + m\): the dequantized weight before the merge, with \(q\) an integer from 0 to \(2^b - 1\) and \(m\) the group's minimum (the bias of MLX's affine mode); \(W_g\): the group's 64 weights
  • \(\Delta', m'\): scale and minimum recomputed on the merged weight; \(\Delta' \approx \Delta\), but the grid shifts, and \(\hat w\) no longer falls exactly on a level
  • \(b\) = 4 bits → 15 steps per group, so the step is 1/15 of the range; with 8 bits there are 255 steps, ~17× smaller
  • in a simulation with groups of 64 and weights \(N(0,\,0.02)\), \(|\Delta W|\) at 2% of the standard deviation gives a “preserved” of −0.60 at 4 bits (the merge adds more noise than delta) and +0.93 at 8 bits [illustrative simulation]
  • hence track A's “fuse the SFT at 8 bits”: the delta that vanishes on the 4-bit grid survives on the 8-bit one

Play with the delta's magnitude and with the grid. Bring \(|\Delta W|\) to 2% of the standard deviation at 4 bits: no code changes, but all 36 values move, because the grid was recomputed, and “preserved” goes negative. The merge made the weight worse instead of applying the delta. Raise the magnitude and the codes start jumping whole steps: the applied delta becomes a caricature of the intended one. At 8 bits the step is ~17× smaller and re-rounding costs little; in BF16, almost everything gets through. This does not rule out serving a fine-tune at 4 bits. A calibrated method (GPTQ, AWQ, DWQ) picks scales and roundings by looking at the merged model, which already contains the delta, and distributes the error so as to preserve the output. What fails is the shortcut: adding the delta onto a frozen 4-bit grid.

The QLoRA-specific problem

A QLoRA adapter learned to correct \(\mathrm{deq}(W_{\text{NF4}})\), not \(W_{\text{BF16}}\). Merging into \(W_{\text{BF16}}\) gives \(W_{\text{BF16}} + BA \neq \mathrm{deq}(W_{\text{NF4}}) + BA\), and the difference is the quantization error the adapter may have been compensating for. Merging into \(\mathrm{deq}(W_{\text{NF4}})\) preserves the trained function, but “bakes” the NF4 error into the model. Either way, measure the KLD against the trained model. In track A, the mismatch from merging onto BF16 is small and acceptable.

Re-quantizing: calibrate again, on the model you trained

The calibration from chapter 20 measures activations to choose scales and roundings. After the fine-tune, that measurement is stale. Four principles apply to any format:

  1. Nothing from the quantized base can be reused. Per-group scales, Hessians (GPTQ, EXL3), activation scales (AWQ), NVFP4 global scales and static KV scales were all estimated on the base. Redo everything from the merged BF16.
  2. Calibration data = usage distribution. Mix samples from your SFT/DPO data, in the real template, with generic chat. Domain-only overfits the quantization to the domain; generic-only underestimates the domain's activations.
  3. Keep in high precision what the original kept: embeddings, lm_head, GDN's in_proj_a/in_proj_b, the vision tower and the MTP head, which wasn't trained.
  4. Measure against the BF16 merge, not against the original base (next section).

Principle 2 is a compromise. A calibrated method chooses the scale that minimizes the error on the calibration distribution. If the domain has larger activations (units, numbers, flags that generic text barely contains), calibrating on generic data alone clips those activations, and calibrating on domain data alone stretches the scale and wastes resolution on everything else.

Each method in the recipe uses the calibration batch in its own way. The GPTQ from chapter 20 picks the quantized weights that best preserve the layer's output on the calibration activations:

\[ \min_{\hat W}\ \big\|W X - \hat W X\big\|_F^2,\qquad H = 2\,X X^\top \]
  • \(X\): the layer's input activations on your calibration batch (one column per token); \(\hat W\): the quantized weight
  • \(H\): the Hessian of this loss; GPTQ quantizes column by column and uses \(H^{-1}\) to push the error onto the columns that haven't been quantized yet
  • domain and generic data produce different \(X\), hence different \(H\), and change where GPTQ spends precision

AWQ looks for per-channel scales based on the mean magnitude of each input channel measured in \(X\), protecting the channels that carry the most activation. In NVFP4, activations get dynamic scales per block of 16 at every token, and calibration only fixes the static global scale (the checkpoint's input_scale). The toy in the figure below is a per-tensor clip, the same kind of static scale as the recipe's static_minmax FP8 KV.

Drag the fraction to 0%: the clip fits generic data only (2.5σ) and the domain error goes from 5% to almost 19%, nearly four times more. Take it to 100% and generic text pays: the clip stretches to 6.7σ and its error rises from 1.3% to 7.4%. The optimum is in the middle, which is why the research's quant_nvfp4.py uses 256 in-house samples + 256 from UltraChat, at sequence length 4096. Measuring the damage: two KLDs you must not mix up

Chapter 25 measured quantization with a single question: “is the served model still the same?”. In post-training there are two questions, each with its own reference, and swapping them is the most common mistake in this phase:

QuestionP (reference)Q (candidate)Expected
What did post-training change?original base, BF16fine-tuned, BF16 (merge or base + adapter)small on generic text; large on the domain, which is the intended effect
How much damage did quantization do?fine-tuned BF16 (merge)fine-tuned, quantizedon the order of what the same format causes on the base
Does the adapter work on a quantized base?BF16 base + LoRAquantized base + LoRA≈ the KLD of the quantized base alone
\[ \mathrm{KL}(P\,\|\,Q) = \frac{1}{|\mathcal T|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal T}\ \sum_{v} p_t(v)\,\big(\log p_t(v) - \log q_t(v)\big)\quad[\text{nats}] \]
  • \(p_t, q_t\): next-token distributions at position \(t\), with the same text forced on both models (teacher-forced)
  • \(\mathcal T\): evaluated positions (generic text, domain text and the model's own answers)
  • also report the per-token median and p90, the perplexity of P and of Q, and top-1 agreement

If you measure quantization against the base, post-training drift enters the computation, and on the domain it's huge by definition. The number comes out high, you blame NVFP4, and NVFP4 wasn't the problem.

Switch the reference to the base on domain text. The damage meter jumps from 0.013 to 1.04 nats, about 80 times more, and lands far from every anchor on the ruler: it looks as if NVFP4 destroyed the model. On generic text, the same switch barely changes anything (0.008 → 0.013 nats). That's why the mistake goes unnoticed when you only measure on generic text.

Three tools, in order of exactness. (1) exllamav3's eval/model_diff.py compares an EXL3 with the merged BF16 using real weights and kernels (-r 100 -l 2048). In it, B is the reference, so the BF16 goes in -mb; both fit on one Spark (≈ 72 GB). (2) For NVFP4 and W4A16, a two-pass HF script records P's top-64 log-probs and computes the truncated KL plus a tail term: a lower bound on the true KL, which dequantizes the weights and doesn't use the W4A4 kernels. (3) In situ, with the production kernels: two vLLM servers and POST /v1/completions with "max_tokens": 1 and "prompt_logprobs": 20; you get exact ΔNLL and top-1, plus a KL “over the common top” that is a proxy and must be reported as such.

The floor is still missing. Run P against P (another boot, another max-num-seqs) and treat the value as the noise floor, as the recipe does. Repeat the protocol on the public reference (Qwen/Qwen3.8-27B against NVIDIA's and RedHatAI's NVFP4s). The criterion: KL(final_BF16‖final_NVFP4) of the same order as KL(base_BF16‖reference NVFP4), measured with the same script, and the gates repeated on the quantized model. RedHatAI publishes a recovery between 98.27% and 100.88% for the base's NVFP4.

Serving: multi-LoRA, merged, and the drafter left behind

There are three paths to the server:

(A) Runtime adapter

vLLM loads the base and one or more adapters, and the request's "model" field picks the adapter. Zero merge cost and trivial A/B tests; it costs per-token LoRA kernels and memory per slot.

(B) Merge → BF16

Identical to what was trained, with no overhead, but it takes 55.6 GB and reads all of it at every decode step. It's the KLD reference and the evaluation server.

(C) Merge → re-quantize

Production speed and memory, which is what all of Module 1 optimizes. It requires fresh calibration and an error measured against (B). It's the natural destination on a GB10.

The deciding factor is decode's bandwidth ceiling. With ~230 GB/s effective, a 27B in BF16 gives ≈ 4 steps per second per stream; the reference NVFP4s (21.92 and 24.69 GB) give ≈ 9–10 tok/s without speculation.

How does a single forward serve a batch in which every sequence asks for a different adapter? The expensive part, \(W x\), is the same for all of them: vLLM does one base GEMM for the whole batch, as it would without LoRA. The low-rank term comes in on top, token by token, chosen by a slot index:

\[ y_t = W x_t + s\,B_{\sigma(t)}\big(A_{\sigma(t)}\,x_t\big),\qquad \sigma(t)\in\{-1,\ 0,\ \dots,\ \texttt{max\_loras}-1\} \]
  • \(x_t\): token \(t\)'s input activation (5120 values in Qwen3.8-27B); \(W\): the base weight, the same for the whole batch
  • \(\sigma(t)\): the slot of the adapter of the request the token belongs to; \(-1\) means “base only”
  • \(A_k\), \(B_k\): the matrices of the adapter occupying slot \(k\), read from preallocated buffers; \(s\): that adapter's scale \(\alpha/r\)

The buffers are tensors with a slot dimension. Per target module, \(A\) lives in a [max_loras × r_max × d_in] and \(B\) in a [max_loras × d_out × r_max] (the lora_a_stacked and lora_b_stacked in vLLM's code). When an adapter gets a slot, its weights are copied into slice \(k\), and the leftover rows up to \(r_{\max}\) stay at zero. The rest is two batched kernels, descendants of Punica's SGMV/BGMV, the “per-token LoRA kernels” of path (A). The shrink computes \(v_t = A_{\sigma(t)}x_t\), with \(r_{\max}\) outputs per token, fetching the right slice by index. The expand adds \(s\,B_{\sigma(t)}v_t\) to \(y_t\). Both skip tokens with \(\sigma = -1\), and a batch with no LoRA at all uses a different CUDA graph (has_lora) [reading of vLLM's code; the research only names the kernels].

That explains each flag. --max-loras is the number of stacked slices: an adapter without a free slice gives the shrink nowhere to read its weights from, and the request waits in the scheduler until a slot frees up. --max-lora-rank fixes the width of every slice: an r = 8 adapter in a buffer with \(r_{\max}\) = 64 takes up 1/8 of the slice, and the rest are zeros that cost memory and go through shrink and expand all the same. In decode, which is bandwidth-bound, every active slot also forces reading whole slices at every step [inference]. The memory of these slices is the research's formula:

\[ M_{\text{slots}} = \texttt{max\_loras}\times r_{\max}\sum_{\text{modules}}(d_{in}+d_{out})\times 2\ \text{B}\qquad\xrightarrow{\ \text{27B},\ 4\ \text{slots},\ r_{\max}=32\ }\ 4\times 217.6\,\text{M}\times 2\,\text{B}\approx 1.7\ \text{GB} \]
  • vLLM preallocates, per target module, \(A\) and \(B\) buffers for each slot, at the maximum rank [research estimate]
  • in Qwen3.8-27B with LoRA on all text linears: 108.8 M parameters per slot at r=16, 217.6 M at r=32, 435.2 M at r=64
  • small next to 55.6 GB of weights, but not negligible in a GB10's KV budget

Two interactions with Module 1: the prefix cache block hash includes the LoRA ID (the same prompt on two adapters doesn't share cache, because K and V differ), and the CUDA graph dispatcher has has_lora in its key (batches with and without LoRA use different graphs). Dynamic loading (POST /v1/load_lora_adapter) exists, but the documentation advises against it in production outside isolated, trusted environments.

Two tests in the figure. Set --max-loras to 1 with 3 adapters in the traffic: the batch only accepts one adapter at a time, and the amber counter of requests waiting for a slot shoots up. Then go to “inside the batch” mode, where the adapters have r = 16, and switch --max-lora-rank from 32 to 64: slot memory doubles, shrink and expand start doing twice the math, and the hatched part of the slices goes from half to three quarters, with no gain whatsoever.

Roledeepseek-v4-flash-vision-exp as judge

In the lab, DeepSeek is the only impartial judge. And, since vLLM has no LoRA for DeepseekV4ForCausalLM, any fine-tune of it can only reach the server through path (C): merge and re-quantization.

Seen from Module 2, abliteration is post-training's gradient-free neighbor: a closed-form rank-1 edit (\(W' = W - \alpha\,\hat r\,(\hat r^\top W)\)), estimated from hundreds of prompts in minutes, versus a rank-\(r\) update learned over hours. The merge figure shows it as a “rank-1 LoRA that nobody trained”. Three consequences. Order matters: abliterating and then running SFT can reintroduce refusals, and even benign SFT erodes refusals: Qi et al. broke through GPT-3.5 Turbo's safeguards with 10 adversarial examples for less than US$0.20 and showed that benign data also degrades safety, to a lesser degree; abliterating afterwards requires re-estimating the direction. The direction becomes a probe: projecting activations onto the refusal direction estimated on the base, before and after the fine-tune, anticipates a safety regression. And abliteration doesn't replace evaluation: the GLM recipe records “KLD not measured with ablit”, and removing refusals without governance is a policy decision, not an engineering one.

The checklist that closes the loop

  1. Before training: NCCL on NET/IB; torch pinned; GDN fast path measured; BF16 dtype in every config; ~217.6 M trainable parameters at r=32; the same chat_template_kwargs in data, evaluation and server; baseline recorded; data decontaminated and split by term; disk ≥ 4 × 56 GB.
  2. During: eval_loss falling; in DPO, rewards/accuracies below ~0.95 and logps/chosen stable; in GRPO, frac_reward_zero_std below ~0.5, no entropy collapse, and samples read; checkpoints every 100–200 steps.
  3. After: gates with CIs and the decision recorded before quantizing; merge verified and MTP re-added; calibration with domain + generic data; KLD against the merge comparable to the reference NVFP4's; gates repeated on the quantized model; MTP acceptance measured; vllm bench serve A/B/A; versions and hashes recorded; and a rollback plan.
