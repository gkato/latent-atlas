The routed experts of GLM-5.3-Flash add up to 311.65 billion parameters. In BF16 they would take 623 GB. In the TR3 checkpoint, which the recipe served in the snapshot studied here, they take 155.8 GB (4 bits per weight), and the model's KL divergence against the reference matches that of the official FP8 measured on the same stack (0.024555 vs. 0.024629 nats) with 54% of the bytes. The trick is not a smarter scale. The recipe rotates each matrix until it looks like Gaussian noise, encodes each block of 256 weights as a path through a trellis, and lets a handful of instructions (shift, integer multiply, lop3 and an FP16 add) rebuild each weight inside the kernel, with no table at all. By the end of this chapter you will know how to read a trellis tensor, explain the role of suh, svh and mcg, and decide when EXL3 is worth it in a recipe of your own.

The enemy: one outlier stretches the ruler

Recall the symmetric uniform quantization from the chapter Why and how to quantize. A group of weights shares a scale \(\Delta = a_{\max}/q_{\max}\). Each weight becomes the integer nearest to \(w/\Delta\), and the rounding error is spread uniformly over \([-\Delta/2,\ \Delta/2]\). The ruler always has the same number of tick marks. What decides the spacing between them is the largest weight in the group:

\[ \mathrm{MSE} \approx \frac{\Delta^2}{12} = \frac{a_{\max}^2}{12\,q_{\max}^2} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \frac{\mathrm{MSE}}{\sigma^2} \approx \frac{\kappa^2}{12\,q_{\max}^2}, \qquad \kappa = \frac{a_{\max}}{\sigma} \]
  • \(\Delta\): the ruler's step (distance between two neighboring levels)
  • \(a_{\max}\): largest \(|w|\) in the group; \(q_{\max} = 2^{b-1}-1\) (7 at 4 bits, 3 at 3 bits)
  • \(\sigma\): standard deviation (RMS) of the group's weights
  • \(\kappa\): crest factor, how many times the largest weight exceeds the typical one

The second form of the equation says it all: the relative error grows with \(\kappa^2\). In a Gaussian group of 128 weights, the largest value sits near \(3\sigma\). If a single weight is \(12\sigma\), the error of all the other 127 grows \((12/3)^2 = 16\) times. Those weights did nothing wrong. In the figure below, 32 Gaussian weights share a 4-bit ruler with one outlier. Increase the outlier and watch the tick marks move apart.

Notice two things. Without rotation, almost all the weights crowd into the 2 or 3 central levels, and the other tick marks sit idle, spent just so the outlier fits. With rotation, the outlier's energy spreads over the 32 coordinates, the crest factor plummets and the ruler becomes useful for everyone again. Measured on the 31 normal weights, the SQNR (signal-to-quantization-noise ratio) gain exceeds 10 dB with a 16σ outlier at 4 bits. At 3 bits without rotation it is worse: the normal weights are all rounded to zero.

The previous chapter fought the outlier locally: small blocks with their own scale (16 weights in NVFP4, 32 in MXFP4). Each outlier spoils only its own block, at a cost of 0.25 to 0.5 bit of scale per weight. This chapter attacks the problem globally: it changes the basis in which the weights are written so that the outlier ceases to exist. The measure of success is the matrix's incoherence:

\[ \mu(W) = \frac{\max_{ij} |W_{ij}|\,\sqrt{mn}}{\|W\|_F} \]
  • \(W\): an \(m\times n\) matrix; \(\|W\|_F\): Frobenius norm (square root of the sum of squares)
  • small \(\mu\) (on the order of \(\sqrt{2\ln(mn)}\) for Gaussian noise): no element stands out, the matrix is “incoherent”
  • \(\mu = \sqrt{mn}\): worst case, all the energy in a single element
Analogy

A class with one student who is 2.10 m tall and everyone else at 1.70 m. If the classroom ruler has to measure everyone with 15 tick marks, the marks end up 14 cm apart and all the “normal” students land on the same mark. The rotation does not cut the tall student down: it redistributes the centimeters among everyone, measures each one with a fine ruler and then undoes the redistribution.

Rotating without changing the math: the Hadamard transform

An orthogonal matrix \(U\) (with \(U^\top U = I\)) is a rotation, possibly combined with reflections: it preserves norms and dot products. That lets us insert rotations inside a linear layer without changing the result:

\[ y = xW = (xU)\,\underbrace{(U^\top W V)}_{\tilde W}\,V^\top, \qquad U^\top U = V^\top V = I \]
  • \(x\): activation (row vector of dimension \(d_{\text{in}}\)); \(W\): weights \(d_{\text{in}}\times d_{\text{out}}\)
  • \(U, V\): rotations on the input side and on the output side
  • \(\tilde W\): the matrix that is actually quantized and stored

We store \(\tilde W\) in 4 bits. At compute time, we rotate the activation with \(U\), multiply, and rotate the output back with \(V^\top\). The layer's function is exactly the same. What changes is the shape of the matrix: a “generic” rotation mixes each coordinate with all the others. By the concentration-of-measure phenomenon, each coordinate of a rotated vector behaves like a sum of many small contributions, and the result looks Gaussian, with variance \(\|v\|^2/n\). An isolated outlier turns into \(n\) contributions of size \(1/\sqrt n\).

An arbitrary dense rotation, however, would cost \(n^2\) multiplications per token. The Hadamard matrix solves this. Its entries are all \(\pm 1/\sqrt n\), and it has a recursive construction (Sylvester's) that turns into a fast algorithm:

\[ H_1 = [1], \qquad H_{2n} = \frac{1}{\sqrt 2}\begin{bmatrix} H_n & H_n \\ H_n & -H_n \end{bmatrix}, \qquad H_n^\top = H_n,\quad H_n H_n = I \]
  • \(n\): a power of 2 (128 in EXL3 and in the GLM indexer)
  • \(H\) is symmetric and orthogonal, so it is its own inverse: the same code rotates and unrotates

The FWHT (fast Walsh–Hadamard transform) applies \(H_n\) in \(\log_2 n\) stages. In each stage, pairs of positions \((a, b)\) become \((a+b,\ a-b)\), the famous butterfly. That is \(n\log_2 n\) additions and subtractions and no multiplications, except for the final \(1/\sqrt n\) factor. Step through below and watch the outlier dilute stage by stage.

Notice that the norm does not change. The classic 4-point example is \(H_4\,[8,\ 0.5,\ -0.3,\ 0.2] = [4.2,\ 3.5,\ 4.3,\ 4.0]\): both norms are 8.0237, and the outlier is gone. In the EXL3 kernel the transform has 128 points and 7 stages, and it runs inside a warp: the group of 32 threads that the GPU executes in lockstep, with the same instruction in the same cycle (how the GPU works is covered in Inside the GPU). Each thread holds 4 of the 128 elements in registers (each thread's private memory, the fastest on the GPU) and handles the first 2 stages on its own. The other 5 exchange values between threads with shuffles (__shfl_xor_sync, which hands each thread the value of the thread whose index differs from its own by an XOR) at distances 1, 2, 4, 8 and 16, without going through memory. It is the same butterfly as in the figure, only with 128 rows split across 32 threads.

Why “randomized”

Plain Hadamard has a blind spot: a vector equal to a row of \(H\) turns into a perfect spike, exactly what we wanted to avoid. Real weights are not adversaries, but regular structures do exist. The solution of QuIP#, QTIP and EXL3 is to first multiply by a diagonal of random signs \(S = \mathrm{diag}(\pm1)\), the randomized Hadamard (RHT). \(HS\) is still orthogonal and fast, and with overwhelming probability it bears no special relationship to the weights. For matrices, you rotate on both sides: \(\tilde W = H S_u\, W\, S_v H\).

Play with the intensity and the rotation side. An isolated spike only spreads over the whole matrix when we rotate both sides. A hot row (a strong input channel) only dilutes with the input rotation, \(HS_u\), which mixes the rows; rotating only the output leaves it intact. With a hot column the opposite happens. Since you don't know in advance where the outliers are, EXL3 rotates both sides.

Why it matters

The rotation does not “improve” the information: it is orthogonal, and so the quantization error measured in the rotated basis is identical to the error in the original basis, \(\|H(\tilde w - \hat{\tilde w})\| = \|\tilde w - \hat{\tilde w}\|\). What it changes is the distribution of the values to be quantized: from heavy-tailed and full of spikes to nearly Gaussian and i.i.d. That is precisely the kind of source for which vector quantization theory knows how to build near-optimal codes, as we will see next.

suh, svh: where the rotations live in EXL3 and in the indexer

In EXL3, each expert matrix is stored as a “fully Gaussian” matrix \(\hat W\), decoded from the trellis, plus two FP16 vectors that carry signs and magnitudes, the suh and svh. The recipe's NumPy oracle (kernel_lab/exl3/reference.py, function reconstruct_weight) writes the equivalent matrix like this:

\[ W = \mathrm{diag}(s_u)\; H_{128}\; \hat W\; H_{128}\; \mathrm{diag}(s_v) \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad y = xW = H_{128}\!\Big( H_{128}(x \odot s_u)\,\hat W \Big) \odot s_v \]
  • \(s_u\) = suh: FP16 vector of size \(d_{\text{in}}\) (random signs × the scale of each input channel)
  • \(s_v\) = svh: FP16 vector of size \(d_{\text{out}}\) (same thing, on the output side)
  • \(H_{128}\): block-diagonal Hadamard, applied to each 128-element slice, with scale \(1/\sqrt{128} = 0.088388347648\)
  • \(\hat W\): the matrix decoded from the trellis, with entries approximately \(\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)\) and no per-block scale

Read the second form from left to right, the way the kernel executes it: (1) multiply the activation by suh; (2) rotate with \(H_{128}\); (3) multiply by the decoded matrix, tile by tile; (4) rotate the output; (5) multiply by svh. At no point does a BF16 \(W\) exist in memory. The format has no per-tile or per-group scale: trellis is integer and the codebook is fixed. Interpretation: all of the tensor's magnitude information lives in suh and svh, the only floating-point tensors in the ABI.

The gate and up shortcut

In an expert's SwiGLU MLP, gate and up read the same input \(x\). If the quantizer used the same suh in both matrices, \(H_{128}(x\odot s_u)\) is a single vector for both. The overlay detects this at load time (_exl3_shared_w13_suh, with torch.equal across all experts) and switches to running a single input rotation. This is a prerequisite for two fast paths. The first is the ladder of prefill tiers for “fat” experts (the ones that receive many tokens; section 8): sorted (tokens sorted by expert, each fat expert in a contiguous slice) → E1 (batched: persistent buffers and gate+up behind a single input Hadamard) → E2 (GEMM directly on the trellis) → E3 (grouped: all of the layer's fat experts in 3 launches). Without a shared suh, the ceiling is sorted (shared_suh_absent). The second is the FAST decode kernel (shared_input). The fused epilogue (the final part of the kernel, where the result is still in registers) of the MoE kernels goes further and hands the down projection its input already rotated:

\[ h_2 = H_{128}\Big(\mathrm{SiLU}\big(H_{128}(g)\odot s_v^{g}\big)\ \odot\ H_{128}(u)\odot s_v^{u}\ \odot\ s_u^{d}\Big) \]

From scalar to vector: why codebooks

Until now, each weight was rounded on its own, and that is scalar quantization. In two dimensions, the decision cells of a scalar quantizer are axis-aligned squares. Vector quantization (VQ) quantizes \(d\) weights at once, picking the nearest neighbor in a codebook of \(2^{k d}\) vectors (\(k\) bits per dimension). It wins in two ways: cells that are “rounder” than cubes, like hexagons in 2D or the \(E_8\) lattice in 8D (space-filling gain), and points concentrated where the Gaussian has mass (shape gain). Rate-distortion theory gives the limit for a Gaussian source:

\[ D(R) = \sigma^2\, 2^{-2R} \qquad\text{vs.}\qquad D_{\text{Lloyd-Max}}(2) = 0.1175\,\sigma^2,\quad D_{\text{Lloyd-Max}}(4) = 0.0095\,\sigma^2 \]
  • \(R\): bits per weight; \(D\): the minimum MSE achievable with any code, as \(d\to\infty\)
  • Lloyd-Max: the best fixed-rate scalar quantizer for a Gaussian
  • At 2 bits, the limit is 0.0625σ², and the optimal scalar sits 2.7 dB above it; at 4 bits, the limit is 0.0039σ², and the gap reaches 3.9 dB (2.4× the MSE)

For Gaussian weights, that 2.4× is the money any 4-bit scalar quantizer leaves on the table, however clever its scale: INT4, FP4, MXFP4 and NVFP4 included. In the figure, 600 Gaussian points in 2D are quantized in two ways with the same number of bits.

In 2D the gain is modest: about 16% less MSE at 1.5 bits per dimension, about 6% at 2 bits and nothing at 1 bit (the square is already optimal for 4 points). It grows with the dimension, which is why research went after codes of dimension 8, 256 or more. Notice the hidden price, too. The codebook has \(2^{kd}\) entries: with \(d = 8\) and \(k = 2\) that is 65,536 vectors. QuIP# gets there by using the structure of the \(E_8\) lattice so it doesn't have to store the whole table. With \(k = 4\) and \(d = 8\) it would be \(2^{32}\) entries, and with \(d = 256\), \(2^{1024}\). Searching for the nearest neighbor in such tables is infeasible. Naive VQ stalls exactly where it would start to get close to the limit.

Deep dive: the QuIP → QuIP# → QTIP → EXL3 lineage

QuIP formalized incoherence processing (rotating before quantizing). QuIP# switched to randomized Hadamard and used an 8-dimensional vector codebook based on \(E_8\). QTIP (Tseng, Sun, Hou and De Sa, NeurIPS 2024) replaced the explicit codebook with a trellis, which behaves like a 256-dimensional VQ with trivial decoding. In the paper, Llama-2-70B at 2 bits has a perplexity of 3.70 on Wikitext2, vs. 3.91 for QuIP# and 3.83 for AQLM (external source). ExLlamaV3 describes its EXL3 format as “a streamlined variant of QTIP”.

Trellises: when the past picks your codebook

Trellis-coded quantization (TCQ) replaces the giant codebook with a state machine. The decoder keeps an \(L\)-bit state. Each new weight consumes only \(K\) bits of the stream, and the reconstructed value depends on the whole state. QTIP uses the simplest possible variant, the bitshift trellis:

\[ s_t = \big( (s_{t-1} \ll K)\ \big|\ b_t \big) \bmod 2^L, \qquad \hat w_t = c(s_t) \]
  • \(s_t\): the \(L\)-bit state at step \(t\); \(b_t\): the \(K\) new bits from the stream
  • \(c(\cdot)\): the codebook, a function from \(2^L\) states to real values
  • In GLM: \(L = 16\), \(K = 4\) (4 bits per weight), one dimension per step (\(V = 1\))

The consequence is that neighboring weights share \(L - K = 12\) bits of state. You cannot choose each weight freely: once you choose the state of \(\hat w_t\), 3/4 of the state of \(\hat w_{t+1}\) (12 of the 16 bits) has already been decided, and only 4 bits remain free. (In the toy in the figure below, with \(L = 4\) and \(K = 2\), the fraction is one half.) In exchange, each weight has access to a set of values that depends on the past. Seen from afar, the \(2^L = 65{,}536\) values form a fine-grained codebook, and each weight pays only \(K\) bits to use it. That is how the trellis imitates a VQ of enormous dimension.

Analogy: the bead necklace

Think of the bit stream as a necklace of colored beads, one 4-bit bead per weight, and of a magnifying glass that only sees 4 neighboring beads at a time. Weight \(t\) is “what the glass sees” when it is centered on bead \(t\). Sliding one position, the glass loses the oldest bead, gains a new one and keeps seeing the other 3. Each new bead changes the meaning of the previous 3, because the value read depends on the whole set. That is why you cannot pick the color of each bead in isolation: you have to plan the whole necklace. That planning is Viterbi's job.

Encoding is hard, decoding is a shift

Finding the best bit stream for a vector of weights is a shortest-path problem in a layered graph: \(2^L\) nodes per step, \(2^K\) edges leaving each node. The Viterbi algorithm solves it by dynamic programming, storing, for each state, the cost of the best path that ends there:

\[ J_t(s) = \min_{s' \to s}\ J_{t-1}(s') + \big(w_t - c(s)\big)^2, \qquad \text{total cost} = \min_s J_{T-1}(s) \]
  • \(J_t(s)\): the smallest accumulated error of a path that reaches state \(s\) at step \(t\)
  • \(s'\to s\): allowed transitions (the \(L-K\) high bits of \(s\) are the low bits of \(s'\))
  • in the real quantizer the error is weighted by the layer's Hessian (section 7), not a plain square
Analogy: the GPS that doesn't store whole routes

A GPS that has to get from A to B doesn't test every possible route, of which there are exponentially many. For each intersection, it keeps only two things: the cost of the best path up to there and which intersection that path came from. Any optimal route to B that passes through intersection X necessarily uses the best stretch up to X, and the rest can be thrown away. At the end, you just follow the “came from” arrows backward. In Viterbi, the intersections are the states \(s\) of each step, the cost is the accumulated error \(J_t(s)\) and the arrows are the survivors.

In the figure, a toy trellis with \(L = 4\) and \(K = 2\): 16 states, 2 bits per weight, 8 weights. Each column is a step, and each node sits at the height of its value \(c(s)\). The node's color is the accumulated cost \(J_t(s)\): amber is cheap, purple is expensive. Viterbi advances column by column and then retraces the winning path backward.

Notice that the mint path cannot jump to just any value: from a state whose low 2 bits are 10, only the 4 states that start with 10 are reachable. Even so, it passes close to the targets more often than the scalar's 4 fixed levels. In the simulations on this page, with this toy codebook, the trellis averages MSE ≈ 0.095σ², vs. 0.118σ² for the optimal scalar, with the same 16 bits. With \(L = 8\) the same simulation gives ≈ 0.075, and the theoretical limit is 0.0625. (Our own illustrative calculation: more states, closer to the limit.)

Decoding, on the other hand, is trivial. Each weight depends only on a contiguous window of \(L\) bits of the stream, so \(\hat w_t\) comes out of one bit shift and one evaluation of \(c\). Each GPU thread decodes its weight without waiting for the others. The cost of encoding is the opposite: with \(L = 16\) and \(K = 4\), Viterbi examines \(2^{16}\times 2^4 \approx 10^6\) transitions per step, or about \(2.7\times10^8\) per tile of 256 weights (our calculation). The ExLlamaV3 converter runs a fused Viterbi kernel on the GPU, once, offline.

Deep dive: tail-biting

In an ordinary trellis, the first weights would spend \(L\) bits to “fill up” the state, an overhead of \(L-K\) bits per block. In tail-biting, the stream is circular: the state of weight 0 is formed by the last symbols of the block. A tile of 256 weights costs exactly \(256\cdot K\) bits. The exact solution requires testing the \(2^{L-K}\) possible values of the “seam”, which are 4 in the toy and 4,096 in EXL3. QTIP uses an approximation with two Viterbi passes (external source).

Computed codebooks: MCG, mul1 and 3INST

What's left is the function \(c(s)\). With \(L = 16\), an FP16 table would have 65,536 entries, 128 KiB. That does not fit in a thread's registers and exceeds the size of shared memory (the fast memory that the threads of one block share, ~100 KB per SM, the GPU's multiprocessor, on consumer Blackwell). Even a smaller table would suffer from bank conflicts: shared memory is divided into 32 banks, each bank serves one address at a time, and when several of the 32 threads in a warp request different addresses in the same bank, the accesses get queued. With each thread looking up a random state, that is exactly what happens. QTIP proposes computed codebooks: the value is calculated from the state with 2 to 4 integer instructions, and the set of all 65,536 results comes out looking Gaussian, the distribution the weights have after the rotation. MCG (multiplicative congruential generator) is a lovely bit trick. The multiplication scrambles the 16-bit state into 32 bits. The mask 0x8FFF8FFF keeps, in each 16-bit half, the sign and the low 12 bits, and zeroes the top 3 bits of the exponent. The XOR with 0x3B603B60 forces those 3 bits to 011. Each half becomes an FP16 with \(|v| \in [0.125,\ 2)\) and a pseudorandom sign, and the sum of two of these nearly symmetric variables is bell-shaped. All of that fits in one integer multiply, one lop3 (an instruction that computes any logic function of 3 inputs; truth table 0x6A is \((a \,\&\, b) \oplus c\)) and one FP16 add, after a funnel shift and a 0xffff mask that cut the 16-bit window out of the stream (exl3_dq.cuh). Example with state 0x0001: \(x\) = 0xCBAC1FED, \(y\) = 0xB0CC348D, halves 0x348D = 0.284424 and 0xB0CC = −0.149902, sum 0.134521484375. That is the golden value the recipe's tests check.

mul1 relies on the Central Limit Theorem: the sum of 4 nearly uniform bytes has mean 510 and standard deviation \(\sqrt{4(256^2-1)/12} \approx 147.8\). The trick is 0x6400: it is the FP16 1024.0, whose mantissa step is exactly 1. Adding a small integer to its bits produces the FP16 \(1024 + \Sigma\) for free, and one FMA normalizes it. dp4a (4-byte dot product with accumulation) does the sum in a single instruction. The code comment explains the choice: “native on Blackwell where vabsdiff4 is emulated”.

Analogy: a die loaded on purpose, and always the same

A computed codebook works like a deterministic die. You call out a number from 0 to 65,535 (the state), the die “rolls” (multiplication and bit scrambling) and shows a value. The same number always gives the same value, which is why the decoder reproduces exactly what the encoder chose. If you roll all 65,536 numbers and make a histogram, the result looks like a Gaussian bell. The die doesn't need to store any table: the “table” is the computation itself. And the encoder (Viterbi) knows the die by heart, so it picks the numbers whose faces land closest to the weights.

Notice that MCG's standard deviation is 1.244, not 1. That doesn't matter, because the actual magnitude of each channel comes from suh and svh, calibrated together with the trellis. What matters is that the codebook is fixed and known to the kernel. A checkpoint encoded with a different codebook would produce plausible and completely wrong weights. That is why each matrix carries an int32 marker: the multiplier itself, reinterpreted as signed.

\[ \mathrm{bpw}_{\text{eff}} = \frac{8\,\big(B_{\text{trellis}} + 2(d_{\text{in}} + d_{\text{out}}) + 4\big)}{d_{\text{in}}\, d_{\text{out}}}, \qquad B_{\text{trellis}} = \frac{d_{\text{in}}\,d_{\text{out}}}{256}\cdot 16k\cdot 2\ \text{bytes} = \frac{d_{\text{in}}\,d_{\text{out}}\,k}{8} \]
  • \(2(d_{\text{in}}+d_{\text{out}})\): bytes of suh and svh (FP16); 4: the int32 marker
  • gate/up of a GLM expert, [4096 → 2048], K4: \(\mathrm{bpw}_{\text{eff}} = 4.0117\)
  • TP2 slice of gate/up, [4096 → 1024], where each of the 2 GPUs holds half of the outputs (section 9): 4.0196 (the 4096-input suh is replicated)

Change \(k\) and watch the whole tile cost \(16k\) words, while the window stays at 16 bits. That is why \(K = 2\) still works: the window covers 8 symbols of 2 bits, and neighbors share 14 bits. The served checkpoint's numbers match the math:

Where the bits come from: calibration and receipts

Viterbi does not minimize the error of the weights, but the error of the layer's output on the activations it actually sees. It is the same idea behind GPTQ and LDLQ, introduced in the fundamentals chapter:

\[ \mathcal{L}(\hat W) = \mathbb{E}_x\,\big\|x(\hat W - W)\big\|^2 = \mathrm{tr}\!\big((\hat W - W)^\top \mathbf{H}\,(\hat W - W)\big), \qquad \mathbf{H} = \mathbb{E}\big[x^\top x\big] \]
  • \(\mathbf H\): the layer's proxy Hessian (\(d_{\text{in}}\times d_{\text{in}}\)), estimated with calibration activations
  • strongly excited input directions weigh more, and the error of an already quantized column is compensated in the following ones (block feedback, BlockLDLQ in QTIP)

The ExLlamaV3 converter accumulates \(\mathbf H\) on the fly with a calibration set, by default 250 rows of 2,048 tokens (-cr/-cc, according to the converter's documentation), rotates, encodes with Viterbi and writes. There is also a calibration-free mode: the recipe's DFlash2 drafter declares caps["uncalibrated_quantize"] = True and is quantized against a synthetic Hessian, without a single forward pass. To trust a third-party checkpoint, the recipe relies on receipts published alongside the weights in the mirror: quantization/recipe.json (profile k4, tensor_policy uniform-routed-experts, target_tensor_parallel 2), exl3-mcg-storage-abi.json, materialization-receipt.json and runtime-results-v44.json, plus the independent KLD panel from section 9. Inside the kernel: decoding in the register

The GPU in 60 seconds

To understand why EXL3 is fast at decode, a four-floor map is enough. Data comes down from a slow, large floor to ever smaller and faster floors. (1) DRAM: where the weights live. On the DGX Spark that is 128 GB of LPDDR5X shared with the CPU, about 273 GB/s in theory, the slow road. (2) Shared memory (SMEM): ~100 KB per SM (each of the GPU's “big cores”), the shared workbench where the threads of a block keep data within reach. (3) Registers: each thread's private memory, where the math happens. (4) Tensor core: the unit that multiplies whole matrix blocks. Threads move in groups of 32, the warps, which execute the same instruction together. An mma.sync m16n8k16 is a whole-warp instruction: it multiplies a 16×16 block of A by a 16×8 block of B and accumulates a 16×8 in FP32, with the operands spread across the registers of the 32 threads. At decode, the bottleneck is road (1): every byte not read from DRAM is time saved. The full tour is in Inside the GPU.

EXL3 is weight-only: the activation comes in as FP16 (the overlay does x.half()), the weights are decoded to FP16 inside the registers and feed the tensor core with 16-bit mma.sync …m16n8k16. There is no FP4 or FP8 instruction on this path. That is why it runs on any GPU from Ampere onward (get_min_capability() = 80). The price is paid in integer ALU: multiply, lop3, dp4a and shifts per weight, plus the Hadamard shuffles. At decode, which is memory-bound, that ALU sits idle waiting for bytes, and reading 4 bits instead of 16 is what counts.

Analogy: unzipping in your hand, not on the desk

Picture the weights as zipped files in a warehouse across town (the DRAM). The naive path brings the zip over, unzips it on the desk (writes the FP16 matrix back to DRAM) and then has to fetch the big file again to use it. EXL3 brings only the zip and unzips it in hand, in the registers, the instant each weight goes into the tensor core. The road only carries the compressed bytes, and the big file never comes into existence.

Why does the threshold exist? Each tile has to be decoded again for every block of activation rows that passes through it. With few rows (decode), the decoding is amortized over little math and the bandwidth gain dominates. With thousands of rows (prefill), reconstructing the FP16 matrix once and using an efficient dense GEMM wins. ExLlamaV3's LinearEXL3 switches strategy above 144 rows (AUTO_RECONSTRUCT_THRESHOLD). The recipe reuses that number in DENSE_EXL3_PREFILL_BF16_MIN_M = 144, which keeps an already reconstructed BF16 copy (with the Hadamards applied) of some EXL3 dense projections for prefill.

In the experts, the story is the dispatch by M that you will revisit in Kernels, now told from the format's point of view:

  • Decode, thin experts: as long as the whole step has up to EXL3_TEMP_ROWS_FUSED tokens (32 with E3, because 4 sequences × (7 drafts + 1) = 32), the layer runs in a single exllamav3_ext.exl3_moe launch, which is graph-safe. Above that, the same fused kernel keeps serving the thin experts, the ones that received up to 32 rows. Only the experts with more than 32 rows become fat and go to E3. The single-launch decision looks at the step's total tokens; the thin/fat split looks at each expert (apply_exl3_fused_moe).
  • Two opt-in decode kernels specialize the thin path, and neither of them is the default. FAST K4/N256 (GLM53_EXL3_MOE_FAST=1; default 0, TP2 only, requires the patched image) eliminates the stock kernel's register spills and measured +7.71% to +14.59% decode in A/B/A. Its numerical study came out formally “INCONCLUSIVE”: 0 flips, but the stock controls themselves also fail the absolute KL gate. The 2-stage cooperative MoE is an opt-in overlay (selected by EXL3_OVERLAY_HOST, outside the default serve). It measured 80.0 tok/s structured on geometry 1, on 2026-09-16, in a serve with adaptive-k, dense FP8 and E3. The stock number the repository puts in the same table, 62.9, comes from a different serve (the one from 2026-08-28, with a 1M pool). The two do not form an A/B, and the repository itself says that paired baselines and the numerical gate on the second node are still missing (docs/cooperative-moe.md).
  • Prefill, fat experts: in a chunk, the hottest expert shows up in almost every token. E2 (exl3_fat_gemm) does the GEMM directly on the trellis, with the 64-word B tiles dequantized in registers and reused across all rows (+20–21% on cold prefill). Unlike the dense LinearEXL3, nothing is reconstructed in FP16: reusing each decoded tile across many rows already amortizes the decoding. E3 groups all of the layer's fat experts into 3 launches, going from 77–91 ms to 31 ms on the 7,168-token layer (+37–45% on prefill).
Pitfall: autotune inside a capture

This pitfall belongs to the dense EXL3 path (GLM53_DENSE_EXL3=1, the Exl3LinearMethod of section 9), not to the experts' exl3_moe. The exl3_gemm that the dense linears use has an autotuner (coop_autotune.cu) that tunes the kernel and synchronizes the stream the first time it sees a new “launch hash”. Inside a CUDA graph capture, that hangs (the hang was recorded with py-spy). That is why the recipe calls _dense_exl3_warmup_autotune, which walks only the EXL3 dense linears and warms up each shape × row bucket {1, 2, 4, 8, 16} before the capture (see CUDA graphs and warmup).

The same format on another engine: TensorFold's kernels

In the TensorFold version of the GLM recipe , nothing in the format changes: the same trellis words, the same MCG, the same suh and the same svh. What changes is who decodes. There is no ExLlamaV3 extension and no exl3_moe: TensorFold's GLM family ships its own CUDA kernels for EXL3 (exl3.cu), which read only 4-bit MCG on the routed experts, a path the project itself calls “experimental”. TensorFold v0.6.0 also has an EXL3 module shared by the other model families, which reads all three codebooks and widths from 1 to 8 bits, but GLM does not go through it. The pieces are the ones from this section. rot_in multiplies the activation by suh, applies \(H_{128}\) and stores the result in FP16. decode_tile turns each lane's 16-bit windows into FP16 pairs through the MCG and feeds mma.sync m16n8k16, accumulating in FP32. The epilogues run the 128-point FWHT in registers (32 lanes × 4 values, the butterfly from section 2) and multiply by svh. It is still weight-only: activations arrive in BF16 and enter the mma in FP16, and “EXL3 prompts never took FP8 activations”.

The difference is an engine rule: a row's bits may not depend on the other rows in the call, i.e. batch invariance. That rule is what makes a drafted reply come out identical to one generated a token at a time. Under it, the kernel does not pick a strategy by M, as with LinearEXL3's threshold of 144 or the thin/fat ladder. In TensorFold v0.6.0, a single grouped kernel served both phases; the recipe's patches gave each phase its own, one for decode and one for prompts. The decode kernel decodes each tile to feed 2 mma, which cover the up to 16 rows of an item (a group of rows routed to the same expert); there, the tile is operand B. In a prompt, each expert receives dozens of tokens, and decoding the same tile again every 16 rows is exactly the waste that motivated the 144 threshold. The way out is to compute the transpose, \(Y^\top = W^\top X^\top\). Reordered, a decoded tile already is the A fragment of \(W^\top\) in the m16n8k16 layout, and the tokens become the B fragments, read from shared memory. Each decoded tile feeds the 64 tokens of a pass: 8 mma instead of 2.

The vLLM version's E2 attacks the same cost another way: there the decoded tile stays operand B and is reused across all rows of a fat expert. Both designs amortize the decoding. TensorFold's does it per pass of 64 tokens, without looking at the batch size. The decoder, decode_tile, shows the trellis as seen from inside the warp. Each lane holds one 32-bit word of the tile, the 8 four-bit symbols of its 8 weights, and fetches the previous lane's word with a shuffle, because the first 16-bit windows straddle the boundary between the two. Lane 0 fetches lane 31's: that is section 5's tail-biting, done in registers. The prompt version, decode_tile_p (from another of the recipe's patches), extracts the same windows with fewer integer instructions. The byte-aligned ones come out with one PRMT each (__byte_perm, which builds a word by picking bytes from others) instead of a shift and a mask, and \(w \gg 4\) is computed once for two windows. The states are the same, and so are the bits.

The bit rule shows up in the sum. Each output of the prompt kernel is a single FP32 chain of mma over ascending k, with no split-K and no partials in memory. A token's bits depend only on its own values, and any slicing of the prompt yields the same state. They are not the bits of the decode kernel, which sums split and warp partials; but draft and serial go through the same prefill, so the equality between the two still holds. The gate/up epilogue is the \(h_2\) equation from section 3: FWHT, \(1/\sqrt{128}\), svh, the clamp before the SiLU, SiLU with the reference's BF16 roundings, the down's suh and another FWHT. In decode, the down applies the FWHT and svh to its own sums: “no Z round trip, no epilogue launch”. The full anatomy of these kernels is in Kernels.

Trellis read aheadTF_GLM_EXL3_LOADS=nc

In decode, the bottleneck is the DRAM highway (the “GPU in 60 seconds” box above), and what counts is the trellis arriving before the warp needs it. TensorFold's decode kernel read the trellis with 32-bit __ldg loads (the lane's own word in each tile), one k step ahead, and only after a chain of dependent reads: count → item → the item's rows. With nc, the recipe's default, it comes in 16-byte non-coherent loads (ld.global.nc.L1::no_allocate), through the read-only path and without filling L1 with words read once per window; nc2 and nc4 go 2 or 4 steps ahead. The words pass through a 1 KB area in the warp's slice of the reduction buffer and are reread in the layout that decode_tile and the mma expect, and the first steps' words are requested before the item's rows reach shared memory. It is data movement only, with the same bits. On its own, with one request: prose 48.36 → 48.78 tok/s and code 59.54 → 60.42 (A/Bs from config.sh, measured during development; the baseline is not the final recipe's). Together with L2 prefetch and the RDMA all-gather of up to 512 KiB: +2.7% on prose and +3.3% on code. The gain is small because none of this reads fewer bytes; it only changes when they arrive (interpretation).

Trade-off: a library or the engine's own kernels

In the vLLM version , EXL3 comes from a library pinned to a commit (ExLlamaV3 c5d9c657) plus the recipe's E2, E3 and FAST kernels, and dispatch by M picks the fastest path for each size: a single launch up to 32 tokens, E3 for fat experts, reconstruction in the dense linears above 144 rows. The price is that the batch can change the bits. In the TensorFold version , the kernels are written for a single shape: the experts' fast decode path only switches on with D = 4,096 and 1,024 expert columns per rank, that is, GLM on two ranks (in the v1.3.2 engine; TP=3 arrived in v1.4), and the reader only accepts MCG K4. In exchange, each row's bits are reproducible, and prompts get a kernel of their own. The only isolated gain published for the EXL3 prompt kernels comes from launch order: placing an item's column blocks side by side makes them all reread its rows while L2 still holds them, “the same kernel and arithmetic, 2.3-2.5x faster at 4,096 rows” (measured on the grouped kernel). End to end, the TensorFold version's cold prefill landed at ~1,950 tok/s up to 64k tokens, against 1,411–1,511 tok/s for the vLLM version between 8k and 96k. The measurements have different dates, tools and configurations (TensorFold's come from sparkDash, with the GPU clocks capped at 2,200 MHz), do not form a controlled A/B, and many other changes sit in between (splitting the hyper-connections across ranks, chunked KDA, 4-bit dense weights).

In vLLM and in the three recipes

vLLM has a pluggable quantization architecture (details in Inside vLLM). A plugin has three pieces. (0) The registration of the name, so that vLLM knows the method exists. (1) A QuantizationConfig, which reads the checkpoint's quantization_config (from_config) and, for each layer of the model, chooses which method runs it (get_quant_method). (2–4) The method itself, an object with three hooks: create_weights, process_weights_after_loading and apply. GLM's overlay/exl3.py implements all of the pieces:

  1. Registration. The Dockerfile inserts "exl3" into the Literal QuantizationMethods and a lazy import of Exl3Config into get_quantization_config. patch_model_overrides.py puts exl3 at the top of the overrides list, and override_quantization_method returns exl3 when the checkpoint says quant_method: exl3.
  2. Exl3Config: from_config and get_quant_method(layer, prefix). from_config reads bits, codebook and scope. get_quant_method dispatches: RoutedExperts goes to Exl3MoEMethod; LinearBase goes to Exl3LinearMethod (if the pack declares the layer as EXL3), Glm53DenseFp8Method (FP8 groups) or UnquantizedLinearMethod; ParallelLMHead only becomes EXL3 if declared.
  3. The create_weights hook. Allocates the parameters in the on-disk format, not in BF16.
  4. The process_weights_after_loading hook. After the bytes arrive: validates the marker, detects the shared suh, builds the pointer tables and the kernel's buffers.
  5. The apply hook. On every forward, runs the right kernel for that M.

In Exl3MoEMethod, per layer: w13_trellis [E, 2, H/16, I/16, 16·k] int16 (gate = 0, up = 1), w13_suh [E, 2, H], w13_svh [E, 2, I_local], w13_mcg [E, 2, 1], and the equivalent w2_*. Allocating a dense w13_weight is forbidden, and \(H\) and \(I\) must be multiples of 16 (“EXL3 trellis tiles are 16-wide”). apply sorts the tokens by expert, counts with scatter_add_ (bincount could synchronize with the host and break the CUDA graph), converts the activation to FP16 and calls exl3_moe with FP32 output. Nothing is expanded to BF16 at load time: with 288 experts across 43 layers, it would not fit. Measured result: “Model loading took 79.65 GiB” per rank.

Slicing without breaking the rotation

In tensor parallel (TP), each matrix is split across the GPUs (the ranks), and each rank stores and multiplies only its own slice. The classic MLP pair has two halves. Gate and up are column-parallel: each rank gets a portion of the outputs and computes its slice of \(h\) without talking to anyone. Down is row-parallel: each rank gets the portion of the inputs that corresponds to its slice of \(h\), computes a partial sum of \(y\), and an all-reduce adds up the pieces from all ranks. In the recipe's EXL3 (shard_exl3_col/shard_exl3_row), gate/up cut trellis along dim 1 and cut svh, with suh replicated. Down cuts trellis along dim 0 and cuts suh, with svh replicated. The runner does one all-reduce per layer.

Why doesn't this cut spoil the rotations? Because \(H_{128}\) is block-diagonal: it rotates each 128-element slice on its own, without mixing neighboring slices. If the cut falls on a multiple of 128, each block sits entirely within one rank, and the rotation stays local. On the output side of down, the Hadamard and ⊙svh are linear, so each rank can apply them to its own partial sum before the all-reduce:

\[ H_{128}\big([\,x_1 \;\; x_2\,]\big) = \big[\,H_{128}(x_1)\;\; H_{128}(x_2)\,\big], \qquad y = \sum_{r} H_{128}\big(\tilde x_r\, \hat W_r\big) \odot s_v = H_{128}\Big(\sum_{r} \tilde x_r\, \hat W_r\Big) \odot s_v \]
  • \(x_1, x_2\): the slices of a vector on the two ranks; the first equality only holds if the cut falls on a multiple of 128
  • \(\tilde x_r = H_{128}(h_r \odot s_{u,r})\): down's input slice on rank \(r\), already rotated; \(\hat W_r\): that rank's trellis rows
  • the sum over \(r\) is the all-reduce; since \(H_{128}\) and \(\odot s_v\) are linear, it makes no difference whether they are applied before or after it, which is why down's svh stays replicated
  • in GLM at TP2: 2,048/2 = 1,024 = 8 blocks of 128 = 64 tile columns per rank
Packed tiles don't split three ways

An expert's trellis has an intermediate width of 2,048, packed into 128 tiles of 16. For TP3, 128/3 = 42.67: the cut would fall in the middle of tile 42 (counting from zero) and in the middle of Hadamard block no. 5, and a tile cannot be split without decoding and repacking the stream. The loader's slicer (_narrow_tp) refuses any dimension that is not divisible by tp: “EXL3 TP shard: dim … is not divisible by tp=3”. That is why GLM on 3 nodes uses expert parallel (96 of the 288 experts, whole, per rank) instead of slicing, and dense EXL3 refuses TP3. Details in Beyond 2 nodes and Parallelism.

In the TensorFold recipe: no plugin, the same cut and a different sum

Without vLLM, there is no method registry and no create_weights/apply hooks. TensorFold has no quantization plugins: the glm5_next family reads the checkpoint's EXL3 tensors on its own, as part of the model, and only reads the formats it implements. Each Spark holds ~80 GiB of weights, practically the vLLM version's 79.65 GiB per rank. The cut is the one from this subsection: each rank gets 1,024 of every expert's 2,048 columns (8 blocks of 128), gate/up by column and down by row. Each rank's down applies \(H_{128}\) and svh to its partial sum in its own epilogue, as the linearity of the equation above allows. What changes is the final sum: instead of an all-reduce, the ranks exchange the block's FP32 partials in an all-gather, and each one adds them “rank 0 first”. It is the same sum, in the same order, on both ranks, so the bits come out identical, something an NCCL all-reduce does not guarantee (An exact engine). And the three-way split never comes up: GLM's CUDA engine requires exactly two ranks.

For the drafter, offset_draft_layer_prefixes renumbers the names: the pack declares model.layers.0…, but at runtime the drafter's layers come after the target's 45 (45 to 49). The dense-h3 profile ties this target to a 6 bpw EXL3 drafter, validated by hash (glm53_profile). The recipe itself warns that this profile has not yet been measured in an A/B against the default.

Drafter in 6 bpw EXL3tools/dflash2_exl3_quant.sh · BITS=6.0 · DRAFT_QUANT_KERNEL_PROJ=1 · DRAFT_QUANT_FC=1

The script uses the stock converter from the fork MiaAI-Lab/exllamav3@63b32f0 (v1.4.2, the PIN) through dflash2_exl3_convert.py, with two in-process tweaks. (1) k_proj/v_proj get the K = 16 sentinel (“don't quantize”), because serving builds the fused context KV from the K/V rows of qkv, which must be a flat tensor. (2) The kernel_projection of the dynamic convolution gets a qmap and enters the budget (DRAFT_QUANT_KERNEL_PROJ=1; 0 leaves it in BF16). DRAFT_QUANT_FC=1 does the same with fc, the input projection of the target's hidden states. The candidate_selector.hidden_projection (1 M parameters, which guides the selector) stays BF16. No calibration: synthetic Hessian. Why 6 bpw, and not 4 as in the experts? The repository does not justify the number. Interpretation: the drafter is small (5 layers), so each extra bit costs few bytes; it is quantized without data, which leaves less margin for error; and each of its errors lowers the drafts' acceptance rate, which is precisely what decides the speculation gain. It takes ~25 min on the GB10, including compiling the extension. dflash2_exl3_package.py writes scope=dflash2_draft, bits=6, head_bits=16 and bf16_shards=[1,2] for qkv. stage_dense_h3.py assembles the pair, and patch_dflash2_exl3.py teaches the drafter to read the pack. The builds are reproducible byte for byte and have not been measured in an A/B (see DFlash2 and DSpark).

Quality: why 4 bits are enough here

Choosing the checkpoint is a Pareto-frontier decision between bytes and fidelity. The independent panel (KLD between the model and the BF16 teacher, five cold runs, 25 sealed windows, 51,175 positions) evaluates the weights, not the GB10 overlay:

Reading the chart: EXL3 4 bpw (0.024555) ties the official FP8 on the same stack (0.024629) using 176 GB vs. 328. NVFP4, a scalar microscaling format of the same size (~180 GB), has 2.5× more divergence (0.060535). Interpretation: it is the difference between “a better scale” and “a better code”, but only in part. Part of the advantage comes from the format (rotation + trellis) and part from the quantizer, which in EXL3 uses the calibration Hessian during encoding; the repository does not say how the NVFP4 was produced. The “FP8 cross-stack” point (0.020615) sits below the others because its logits come from a different inference stack. Each stack (kernels, accumulation order) adds its own numerical divergence to everything measured on it (interpretation; the repository does not detail that stack). That is why the fair comparison is within the same stack (v44), where EXL3 and FP8 tie. TR3 K6 (6 bpw, 254 GB, 0.013723) is a different checkpoint and shows that there is still quality to be bought with bytes. On 2 × 128 GB, with a million-token KV and a drafter, 176 GB is the point where the numbers add up. How to measure KLD properly is the subject of Measuring the damage.

GLM · EXL3

Experts in a K4 MCG trellis with Hadamard rotation (suh/svh). Weight-only, FP16 MMA and custom kernels for decode (the fused exl3_moe; FAST and cooperative as opt-in) and prefill (E2/E3). Dense layers in FP8 Marlin by default, or EXL3 mul1 in the H3 profile. The indexer uses FWHT-128 before FP8.

Qwen · NVFP4

Experts in a scalar microscaling format (E2M1 + an E4M3 scale every 16 + a global FP32), no rotation: the outlier is contained by the small block. W4A4 on FlashInfer CUTLASS SM120, with native FP4 tensor cores. Prefill gains compute. As for quality, what the previous chapter measured applies.

DeepSeek · native MXFP4

The official checkpoint already ships the experts in MXFP4 (fp4_e8m0_k32), trained that way, and runs as W4A16 on b12x. The Hadamard rotation shows up in the indexer (rotate_activation before the FP4 simulation), in a regime learned during training (QAT). The GLM repository mentions a sibling, “DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash-EXL3-2.9bpw”, which is outside the scope of the original recipes; since October 2026 it serves V4.1-Flash on 2 Sparks through TensorFold, compared with four other deployments in the chapter 44 case study.

GLM · TensorFold

The same K4 MCG checkpoint, read by the engine's own kernels, with no plugin. Input rotation once per token (a single suh per layer), a \(Y^\top = W^\top X^\top\) prompt kernel and a trellis read in 16-byte loads in decode. Dense weights in affine 4 bits (DENSE=q4) by default. In v1.3.2, two ranks only (three since v1.4; see what changed), with each row's bits reproducible.

When to choose EXL3 in a recipe of your own

(1) Decode is bandwidth-bound and you need ≈ 4 bits or fewer with FP8 quality. (2) The hardware lacks tensor cores or mature kernels for the checkpoint's FP4 format: EXL3 only needs FP16 MMA (Ampere or newer). (3) There is an EXL3 quant with published receipts and KLD, or you have the GPU and the calibration data to produce one. (4) You are willing to pay the engineering cost: a quantization plugin like overlay/exl3.py, kernels for fat experts at prefill (the weak spot of weight-only), TP constraints (16-wide tiles, packed widths) and a parity oracle. If the model comes trained in a native format (DeepSeek's MXFP4) or there is an official NVFP4 with ready-made W4A4 kernels, the native format is usually the shortest path.

Mixed widths: EAQ and IQ2_XXS, lab formats

EXL3 doesn't force the same width on the whole model: each tensor has its own K. Mia's “EAQ” quantizations exploit that. The acronym is never expanded in any of the repositories, so we won't invent a meaning for it; what the repositories do say is where the bits go. In Mia-AiLab/DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash-1017-EXL3-EAQ-v1-2.9-bpw (196 GiB, served on two Sparks by TensorFold), the routed experts get 3 bits per weight, and 2 bits in layers 18–22; attention gets 5, the shared experts 4–5, the head 6 and the DSpark drafter blocks 4 (docs/requirements.md). That checkpoint's card has no perplexity or KL against the original model, and the recipe's README says so. The v1.1 pack, opt-in (EAQ_V1_1=1), keeps those widths but stores 27 indexer and compressor linears in BF16 (8 attn.wq_a, 8 attn.indexer.wq_b, 4 attn.compressor.wkv, 3 attn.compressor.wgate and 4 attn.indexer.wk): once again, the components that make selection decisions stay in high precision, as in chapter 20. Mixing formats costs engineering. The engine needed a mixed-pack loader (patch 0024), which runs those linears through cuBLAS, and a fixed row count per GEMM (0026, TF_DS_PLAIN_ROWS=16): cuBLAS picks its kernel and split-K from the matrix shape, and without the fix the replies with and without drafts matched on only 4 of 8 prompts (8 of 8 with it). The EXL3 kernels compute each row in a fixed order and never had the problem. In Ornith-1.5-122B (Mia-AiLab/Ornith-1.5-122B-EXL3-EAQv1-4-bpw, on a single Spark), the trellis average is 4.049 bpw, and the quant card gives KL 0.0135 and 96.7% top-1 agreement against BF16 (not measured in the recipe).

IQ2_XXS comes from ggml (the base of llama.cpp) and goes lower: 2.0625 bits per weight, in groups of 256, with the weights stored as indices into a fixed grid of values. It's a codebook, like the ones in section 4, and the Nemotron kernel expands it to int8 in registers and runs it on the int8 tensor cores, with activations quantized per 32. Curiously, the pack that uses it comes from the vendor itself: nvidia/NVIDIA-Nemotron-3.5-Bolt-74B-A9B-24GB (ModelOpt, quant_algo: MIXED_PRECISION) puts routed and shared experts in IQ2_XXS, the Mamba projections in NVFP4 W4A16 (group 16) and attention, latent projections and lm_head in Q8_0 (group 32). That's 22.45 GiB on the Hub and 51.1 GB of unified memory once loaded with the recipe's defaults.

What carries over beyond the DGX Spark is the idea, not the format: allocating bits per tensor, by each tensor's sensitivity and by the bytes it reads per step. On an H200 node or a Blackwell cluster, the same decision shows up in ModelOpt's mixed-precision recipes (the Bolt pack itself declares MIXED_PRECISION) and in FP8 + NVFP4 checkpoints served by TensorRT-LLM, vLLM or SGLang. EXL3 and IQ2_XXS, on the other hand, remain lab formats: their kernels come from community projects (ExLlamaV3, ggml) or from plugins and engines like the ones in these recipes, and even NVIDIA's pack needed custom kernels in TensorFold (our reading of the ecosystem).

The weights fit and stay correct. The next bottleneck, at long context, is another tensor that grows with every token: the KV cache. There you will meet the Hadamard rotation again (in the indexer's cache) and power-of-2 scales, now applied to keys and values.
