An LLM can only read one thing: a queue of vectors, one per token. To it, “seeing” a photo means receiving that photo already translated into hundreds or thousands of vectors that live in the same space as the text embeddings. This chapter follows that translation end to end: pixels, patches, vision tower, translator, placeholders in the prompt, positions and, finally, the inference engine. At every stage you will watch a recipe number being born. That is how we explain why GLM caps each image at 2,048 tokens, why Qwen replicates the vision tower on both GPUs and why DeepSeek had to build the entire tower in a boot-time hotfix. And since the GLM recipe gained a second version on a different engine, TensorFold , you will also see a third way to house the tower: on a single rank, with reserved memory and a token budget that the images of a request share among themselves.

Pixels are not tokens: the image becomes a grid of patches

Start with what the GPU receives. A color photo is a tensor \([3, H, W]\): three channels (red, green and blue) with one number per pixel. A 1920×1080 frame holds 1920 · 1080 · 3 ≈ 6.2 million numbers. If every pixel became a token, that would be ~2 million tokens per photo, and attention, which costs \(O(n^2)\) (chapter 6), would blow up. The Vision Transformer (ViT) solves this by cutting the image into little squares of \(p \times p\) pixels, the patches. Each patch is then treated as a “visual word”.

Each patch is flattened into a vector of \(3p^2\) numbers and multiplied by a learned matrix \(W_E\). This plays the role of the text embedding (chapter 5), with one difference: there, the token id picks a row of the table; here, the “lookup” is a matrix multiplication over continuous numbers.

\[ \mathbf{x}_i = W_E\,\operatorname{vec}(P_i) + \mathbf{b}, \qquad W_E \in \mathbb{R}^{\,d \times 3p^2 T_p}, \qquad N = \frac{H}{p}\cdot\frac{W}{p} \]
  • \(P_i\): the \(i\)-th patch, with 3 channels × \(p\) × \(p\) pixels (× \(T_p\) frames)
  • \(\operatorname{vec}\): flatten the block into a column vector
  • \(p\): patch side: 14 in DeepSeek and GLM, 16 in Qwen
  • \(T_p\): temporal patch: 1 in DeepSeek, 2 in GLM and Qwen
  • \(d\): tower width: 1024 (DeepSeek, GLM) or 1152 (Qwen)
  • \(N\): number of patches in the image

Multiplying every patch by the same matrix is exactly a convolution with kernel \(p\) and stride \(p\). That is why checkpoints store this weight in convolution shape: patch_embed.proj [1024, 3, 2, 14, 14] in GLM and [1152, 3, 2, 16, 16] in Qwen. DeepSeek writes the same computation as a 588 → 1024 Linear, because 3 · 14 · 14 = 588. In the figure below, a conveyor belt picks up one patch at a time, unrolls its pixels into a vector and projects it into the tower's space. Hover over the image to choose the patch.

Notice three things. First, the number of patches grows with area: doubling width and height quadruples the patches. Second, GLM and Qwen see two frames per patch. With temporal_patch_size = 2, a still image becomes a “video” of two identical frames, and the same tower serves both images and videos. Third, the vector that comes out has nothing semantic about it yet. It is just a linear projection of the pixels; the meaning comes from the tower's blocks.

Analogy: the tile mural

Picture a tile mural. The ViT does not examine the wall drop of paint by drop of paint. It fills out a card of 1,024 numbers for each tile and then lets the cards talk to each other. It is in that conversation that the orange tile finds out it is a cat's ear, because the one below it has whiskers.

Before the image is cut into patches, preprocessing adjusts it. The processor resizes to multiples of the patch (and of the merge factor, which shows up in section 3), normalizes the values and sometimes pads the borders. DeepSeek converts to RGB, caps the width/height ratio at 8, upscales images with fewer than 147,456 pixels (384²), pads with gray 127 to keep the aspect ratio and normalizes with \((x - 0.5)/0.5\), leaving the pixels in \([-1, 1]\) in bf16. Qwen uses a mean and standard deviation of 0.5; GLM uses CLIP's mean and standard deviation.

Why patch size matters

The patch is the model's “visual acuity”. Detail smaller than a patch, such as a 6-pixel letter, has to be reconstructed from the mix of pixels inside a single vector. A smaller patch sees better, but produces more vectors, and the cost of attention inside the tower grows with the square of that number. Every vision model lives between these two forces. That is why the three processors in the recipes resize images to fit a token budget before cutting them (section 8).

The vision tower: a Transformer that looks in every direction

After the embedding, the \(N\) vectors go through a stack of Transformer blocks, the vision tower. It is the block from chapter 7: pre-normalization, multi-head attention, MLP and residual stream. The decisive difference lies in the attention mask.

In an LLM, token \(i\) only sees tokens \(0 \dots i\). That is the causal mask, which exists because text is generated from left to right and the future has not been written yet. In an image there is no future: all patches arrive together. That is why the ViT uses bidirectional attention, in which each patch queries all the others, including those that come “after” it in reading order. In the figure, click a patch to watch it ask questions. The lines show where the attention weight goes, computed illustratively: similar colors and close neighbors attract more weight.

In causal mode, the top-left patch sees only itself, and the cat's ear cannot consult the paw right below it. The mask would throw information away for no reason, because the tower does not generate anything. The attention cost is still \(O(N^2)\), but \(N\) is kept under control by the token budget.

Deep dive: and inside the LLM?

After the translator (section 3), the image tokens enter the decoder's sequence and obey its causal mask, like any text; that is how vLLM treats them in all three recipes. The official DeepSeek Vision-Exp reference goes further on two points: it grants bidirectional visibility among the tokens of the same image (from IMAGE_START to IMAGE_END), widening the sliding window to cover them, and it requires that span to be prefilled in a single chunk. The inventory found neither behavior in the hotfix: there, image tokens stay causal, with a window of 128, and chunked prefill may split the image across steps (the vectors wait in the encoder cache, section 6). The impact has not been measured; the question remains open.

Pitfall: an epsilon that ruins descriptions

GLM's config.json declares rms_norm_eps = 1e-5 for the tower, and the vLLM image used by the recipe forces 1e-6. The code comment, quoted in the inventory, says that serving with 1e-5 makes the RMSNorm drift and produces “repetitive and degraded” image descriptions. The tower is small, but numerically sensitive: a normalization detail changes what the LLM “sees”.

In the TensorFold lane: the same tower, rewritten and checked bit for bit

The second version of the GLM recipe swaps vLLM for TensorFold v0.6.0 with 53 patches (An exact engine). Upstream, GLM-5.3-Flash images only worked on the MLX backend, on the Mac. That is why patch 0003-glm-vision rewrites the tower for CUDA in plain PyTorch, with BF16 weights, piece by piece from the GLM column of the table above: the conv3d over 3×2×14×14 patches, the 24 blocks with RMSNorm (on q and k too), 2D RoPE, attention within each image or frame pair and a SwiGLU MLP with clamp, then the 2×2 reduction and the merger up to the LLM's 4,096 columns. The processors and the tower follow those of Hugging Face transformers 5.17 and were checked bit for bit against them. One detail shows how delicate this is: the code uses the reference's convolutions, not the matrix multiplications that section 1 showed to be mathematically equal. Both add the same terms in a different order, and floating-point addition is not associative (chapter 4). According to the code comment, across the 24 blocks those last-bit differences grow into ~3%. It is the epsilon lesson above seen from another angle: in a tower, the reference's exact arithmetic is part of the model.

Position in two dimensions: 2D RoPE

Attention is blind to order: shuffle the patches and the weighted sums do not change. Each patch has to be told where it is. RoPE (chapter 8) does this by rotating pairs of dimensions of \(\mathbf{q}\) and \(\mathbf{k}\) by an angle proportional to the position. That way, the product \(\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{k}\) comes to depend on the relative distance. In an image, position has two coordinates, the row \(h\) and the column \(w\). 2D RoPE handles this with a split: half of the pairs rotate with the row, the other half with the column.

\[ \omega_k = \Theta^{-2k/32},\ \ k = 0,\dots,15 \qquad \phi_k = h\,\omega_k, \qquad \phi_{16+k} = w\,\omega_k \] \[ \big\langle R(\boldsymbol\phi^{(q)})\,\mathbf{q},\ R(\boldsymbol\phi^{(k)})\,\mathbf{k} \big\rangle = f\big(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{k},\ h_q - h_k,\ w_q - w_k\big) \]
  • \(\Theta\): vision_rope_theta = 10,000 in DeepSeek
  • \(\omega_k\): the 16 frequencies per axis (rope_dim = 1024 / 16 / 2 = 32)
  • \(h, w\): row and column of the patch in the tower's grid
  • \(\phi\): the 32 angles, one per pair, of each 64-dimensional head
  • \(R(\phi)\): block-by-block rotation of the pairs
  • \(f\): the result depends only on the differences \(\Delta h\) and \(\Delta w\)

The figure shows this as two clotheslines of clocks. Move the patch around the grid: the hands in the top row turn when the row changes, those in the bottom row when the column changes. The clocks on the left are fast and those on the right, extremely slow. Together they form a unique fingerprint of the position.

The cross pattern in the map is no accident: row and column contribute separate terms, so patches in the same row (or column) already get half of the maximum similarity. That is why DeepSeek does without an absolute positional embedding; the sine and cosine table is generated on demand for any \((n_h, n_w)\) grid by get_vision_cos_sin. Qwen combines both techniques: a learned absolute grid of 48×48 positions (2,304 vectors), bilinearly interpolated for each image and added to the axial RoPE. GLM applies 2D RoPE to half of the dimensions (partial_rotary_factor 0.5); since GLM's text side is NoPE, this is the only RoPE in the target model (the DFlash2 drafter served alongside it is a separate model).

The translator: merger, aligner and projector

At the end of the tower there are \(N\) vectors of width \(d\), and two problems keep us from dumping them into the LLM. The first is width: 1024 or 1152, against 4096 in GLM and DeepSeek and 2560 in Qwen. The second is quantity: 14-pixel patches would cost a fortune in prefill and in KV cache. The piece that solves both at once goes by three names: merger (Qwen, GLM), aligner (DeepSeek) or projector. It gathers \(k \times k\) neighboring patches, concatenates their vectors and passes them through a small MLP that lands on the LLM's width.

\[ \mathbf{z}_j = W_2\,\sigma\!\big(W_1\,[\mathbf{x}_{j,1};\ \mathbf{x}_{j,2};\ \dots;\ \mathbf{x}_{j,k^2}] + \mathbf{b}_1\big) + \mathbf{b}_2, \qquad \mathbf{z}_j \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{LLM}}}, \qquad N_{\text{tokens}} = \frac{N}{k^2} \]
  • \(\mathbf{x}_{j,m}\): the \(k^2\) tower output vectors in group \(j\)
  • \([\,;\,]\): concatenation, with \(k^2 d\) numbers
  • \(\sigma\): activation (GELU in Qwen and DeepSeek; SwiGLU in GLM)
  • \(k\): spatial_merge_size = 2 (GLM, Qwen) or vision_downsample_ratio = 3 (DeepSeek)
  • \(d_{\text{LLM}}\): width of the LLM's residual stream
  • in Qwen, each \(\mathbf{x}_{j,m}\) first goes through a LayerNorm over 1152 numbers (merger.norm [1152]), applied patch by patch, before the concatenation

Concatenating neighbors is the trick known as space-to-depth (or pixel unshuffle): the spatial resolution drops \(k\) times along each axis and the vector's depth grows \(k^2\) times, without losing a single number. In DeepSeek this is literally F.unfold(x, 3, stride=3). Follow the steps in the figure and switch models to watch the numbers change.

In step 4, notice that a single LLM token now summarizes a whole region of the image, and that region measures \(p \cdot k\) pixels on each side:

GLM

  • 2×2 via a reduction convolution (downsample [4096, 1024, 2, 2])
  • SwiGLU 4096 → 10240 → 4096, with swiglu_limit = 10 (clamp)
  • 1 token ≈ 28×28 px (× 2 frames)

Qwen

  • LayerNorm over 1152 on each patch, then 2×2 concatenated: 4 × 1152 = 4608
  • fc1 4608 → 4608 → GELU → fc2 4608 → 2560
  • 1 token ≈ 32×32 px

DeepSeek

  • 3×3 with F.unfold: 9 × 1024 = 9216
  • Linear → 4096 → GELU → Linear 4096 (≈ 54.5 M parameters)
  • 1 token ≈ 42×42 px

This \(p \cdot k\) sets the exchange rate between image and context. Per unit of area, GLM spends \(42^2/28^2 = 2.25\) times as many tokens as DeepSeek, and Qwen \(42^2/32^2 \approx 1.7\) times as many. More tokens mean more detail (good for reading text in screenshots) and more prefill, KV and memory. DeepSeek picks the economical extreme: at most 384 tokens per image.

Why a small MLP is enough

The LLM only understands vectors similar to the ones it saw during training. The projector is the “dictionary” that carries the tower's features into that region of the space, hence the name aligner: it aligns the two spaces. It does not need to be deep, because the heavy lifting of recognition (edges, textures, objects) has already been done by the tower's blocks, and the reasoning is left to the LLM.

Deep dive: why vision stays in BF16

In all three recipes the tower is not quantized: in GLM it gets quant_config=None even in the FP8 checkpoint, in Qwen NVFP4 model.visual* is on the quantization exclusion list, and in DeepSeek it is BF16 as well. Interpretation: the tower adds up to ~0.5 B parameters and only runs during the prefill of image tokens, so quantizing it would save little and put at risk precisely the precision of perception. Then there are the kernels. In the alternative checkpoint local-inference-lab/…, with MXFP8 in the vision part, mm_mxfp8 requires dimensions that are multiples of 32, and visual.blocks.*.mlp.linear_fc1 [4304, 1152] does not pass (4304 mod 32 = 16); the Qwen recipe's MXFP8 patch sends all of visual.* to BF16 emulation (chapter 21).

Stitching the image into the prompt: placeholders and the image block

Now there are \(M\) vectors at the LLM's width. How do they get into the middle of a conversation? All three recipes use the same trick, placeholders. The chat template writes a marker where the image was. The multimodal processor expands that marker into exactly \(M\) copies of the image token. The embedding layer turns every id into a vector and, in the last step, the rows of the copies are overwritten with the tower's vectors, an operation called masked scatter.

\[ \mathbf{e}_i = \begin{cases} E[x_i] & \text{if } x_i \neq \texttt{IMG} \\ \mathbf{z}_{\pi(i)} & \text{if } x_i = \texttt{IMG} \end{cases} \]
  • \(x_i\): token id at position \(i\)
  • \(E\): embedding matrix [vocabulary × hidden]
  • \(\texttt{IMG}\): the placeholder id
  • \(\mathbf{z}\): the translator's outputs
  • \(\pi(i)\): which visual vector goes to position \(i\), that is, the order in which the grid is read

DeepSeek's image block: the “N” layout and alignment to 4

DeepSeek builds a more elaborate block (build_image_block), in a format we will call the N layout:

  1. Line breaks. Each row of the Aligner's grid gets a newline token at the end. Without it, a 10×12-token image and a 12×10 one would be the same queue of 120 vectors.
  2. Zigzag over row pairs. The rows are grouped two by two and traversed column by column: (r0,c0), (r1,c0), (r0,c1), (r1,c1)… The path looks like the letter N, and 4 consecutive tokens form a small 2×2 square of the image.
  3. Alignment pad. Before IMAGE_START come \(3 - (s \bmod 4)\) pad tokens, the compress_pad, so that the image body starts at a position that is a multiple of 4.
  4. Closing. A final pad brings the body up to a multiple of 4, and IMAGE_END closes the block.

Why 4? Because half of DeepSeek-V4's attention layers compress the KV 4 tokens at a time: these are the C4A layers from chapter 13. The inventory's reading, which is an interpretation, is that with this alignment each compressed entry covers a 2×2 piece of the same image and never mixes text with image.

\[ n_{\text{block}} = \underbrace{3 - (s \bmod 4)}_{\text{compress\_pad}} + 1 + n_h\,(n_w + 1) + (n_h \bmod 2)(n_w + 1) + n_{\text{final pad}} + 1 \]
  • \(s\): position where the block starts in the prompt (start_pos)
  • \(n_h, n_w\): the Aligner's grid, \(\lceil \text{tower grid}/3 \rceil\) on each axis
  • \((n_h \bmod 2)(n_w+1)\): a pad row when the number of rows is odd
  • \(n_{\text{final pad}}\): 0 or 2 tokens to close out a multiple of 4
  • the two “+1”s: IMAGE_START and IMAGE_END

Here is the code's own example, worked by hand. The tower sees 19 rows × 40 columns of patches (the docstring writes “40×19”, width first). The Aligner divides each axis by 3: \(n_h = \lceil 19/3 \rceil = 7\), \(n_w = \lceil 40/3 \rceil = 14\). Each row has \(14 + 1 = 15\) tokens counting the ↵, the body has \(7 \cdot 15 = 105\), and since \(n_h\) is odd a pad row of 15 is added. The 8 rows form 4 pairs of 15 tokens (60, even), so the final pad is 0: \(\text{pad} + 1 + 105 + 15 + 0 + 1 = 122 + \text{pad}\), from 122 to 125. With the axes swapped (\(n_h = 14\), \(n_w = 7\)), the count would come to 114. In the figure, push the image to the right with the slider and watch the pad adjust. Then turn the alignment off and look for the red groups, where a compressed entry mixes text and image.

Keep this consequence in mind

The block's length depends on where the image lands in the prompt, so the same photo can take up 122, 123, 124 or 125 tokens. In section 6 you will see that vLLM's encoder cache identifies images by their bytes alone, and that this combination brought the engine down (issue #172).

Where does each image token sit? mRoPE, NoPE and video

Inside the tower, each patch knew its row and its column. After the translator, the image becomes a queue of tokens in the middle of the text. How will the LLM know that token 17 sits below token 3? The three recipes give three answers:

↵

DeepSeek: a queue with line breaks

Positions in the LLM are 1D, just like in text. The structure comes from the layout: newline tokens, the 2×2 zigzag and learned delimiters. The geometry arrives already encoded in the vectors by the tower's 2D RoPE.

🧭

Qwen: 3D mRoPE

Each token gets a triple (t, h, w). For text, the three coordinates are equal. For images and video, they describe the grid in space and time.

∅

GLM: NoPE in the text

GLM's LLM has no RoPE at all: order comes from the KDA layers. Spatial information arrives embedded in the vectors, courtesy of the tower's 2D RoPE, and the delimiters mark where the image starts and ends.

In Qwen's mRoPE (Multimodal RoPE), each token's position stops being a number and becomes a triple \((t, h, w)\). For text, the three coordinates are equal, \((p, p, p)\), and mRoPE reduces to ordinary RoPE. For the tokens of an image that starts at position \(s\), \(t\) stays constant while \(h, w\) sweep the grid: \((s, s + i, s + j)\). The text that follows continues at 1 + the largest coordinate used by the image, not at \(s\) + the number of tokens. An image of 1,000 tokens “occupies” only ~\(\max(H, W)\) positions.

\[ \mathbf{p}_{\text{text}} = (p,p,p), \qquad \mathbf{p}_{\text{img}}(\tau, i, j) = (s+\tau,\ s+i,\ s+j), \qquad p_{\text{next}} = \max(\mathbf{p}) + 1 \] \[ \phi_m = p_{a(m)}\,\omega_m, \qquad \omega_m = \Theta^{-2m/64}, \qquad m = 0,\dots,31, \qquad a(m) \in \{t, h, w\} \]
  • \(s\): first position of the image
  • \(i, j\): row and column after the merge; \(\tau\): temporal group (video)
  • \(\Theta = 10^7\) in Qwen
  • \(a(m)\): the axis that drives pair \(m\). mrope_section [11, 11, 10] gives 11 pairs to \(t\), 11 to \(h\) and 10 to \(w\)
  • mrope_interleaved: true: the axes alternate (t, h, w, t, h, w…) instead of occupying contiguous bands
  • only 64 of each head's 256 dimensions rotate (partial_rotary_factor 0.25)

Why interleave? With contiguous bands, \(t\) would get only the highest frequencies and \(w\) only the lowest, and each axis would “see” a different part of the spectrum. By interleaving, all three axes get frequencies from across the whole spectrum. That is the motivation described by the Qwen3-VL family, from which the architecture inherits the scheme. In the figure, each token shows its triple. The clocks below turn with the highlighted token, colored by the axis that drives them.

Notice the jump after the image: the text that follows starts at 8, not at 4 + 12 = 16. The model learns to read the grid through the \((h, w)\) pair, without spending positions as if the image were a long sentence.

Pitfall: overrides that wipe out mRoPE

The mRoPE keys live inside rope_parameters, together with \(\Theta\) and the partial factor. An override that replaces the whole dictionary, such as turning on YaRN for 1M context, has to repeat mrope_section and mrope_interleaved, as the official card does; in the Qwen recipe, it also goes nested under text_config (chapter 8, chapter 43). The point to check: the payload in the recipe's start.sh sends only rope_type, factor and original_max_position_embeddings. If vLLM replaces the whole dictionary, \(\Theta\), the rotary fraction and the mRoPE sections fall back to their defaults. The inventory flags this as “to be verified”, and the repo treats 1M as unvalidated. When you recreate the recipe, use the card's complete dictionary.

Video: time as the third dimension

Video is a stack of frames, and GLM and Qwen treat time the same way they treat space. With temporal_patch_size = 2, two consecutive frames form a single 3D patch (a 2×\(p\)×\(p\) Conv3d), so each pair of frames becomes one grid of tokens: \(\text{grid}_t = \text{frames}/2\). A still image is just the case of a single group with the frame duplicated. Each recipe handles video its own way:

  • The video processor samples at 2 fps, with a ceiling of 240,000 tokens (max_image_tokens of the video_processor). Each temporal group becomes [image_start] tokens [image_end] followed by the timestamp as text (“1.0 seconds”). By default, vLLM decodes 32 frames per video; VIDEO_NUM_FRAMES changes that via --media-io-kwargs '{"video":{"num_frames":N}}'.
  • Video uses <|video_pad|> (248057) and the same 2-frame 3D patch, so each \(T/2\) group becomes a grid of tokens with positions \((s+\tau, s+i, s+j)\) in mRoPE. The README fixes the encoder budget at 16,384 tokens per input: larger images are resized, longer videos are sampled sparsely. The video limits live in video_preprocessor_config.json: shortest_edge 4096 and longest_edge 25,165,824 pixels.
  • There is no video encoder in the official weights. A GIF is decoded as a still image: only the first frame.

Why does Qwen drop frames? A video token covers 32×32 pixels across 2 frames: 2,048 “pixel-frames”. The card suggests longest_edge = 469,762,048 for hour-long videos and says this yields 224K tokens; indeed, 469,762,048 / 2,048 = 229,376 = 224 × 1,024 (interpretation: the ceiling adds up the pixels of all frames). By the same arithmetic, the default of 25,165,824 gives ~12K tokens. One minute at 2 frames/s (an illustrative rate) is 60 groups; at 300 tokens per group, that is 18K tokens, above the 16,384. Instead of squashing every frame, fewer groups are sampled.

In the figure, the film strip is sampled, grouped into pairs, and each pair becomes a block of placeholders. In GLM, each block carries its own timestamp; turn on the bug to see the documented case that the GLM overlay fixes. In Qwen, raise the duration and the tokens per group until you blow past 16,384 and watch whole groups get dropped.

Overlay #3overlay/patch_glm_video_placeholders.py

vLLM's glm4_1v processor builds one image block per timestamp, and with GLM-5.3's timestamps a 1 s clip could produce 3 timestamps while video_grid_thw said \(T = 1\): 300 slots for 100 tokens. The patch replaces _construct_video_placeholder and aligns the timestamps to grid_t (_align_timestamps, in the style of glm46v). The same file, installed as an import hook in a .pth, also turns off the indexer's persistent_topk (if False and … select_k in (512, 1024, 2048)), which overflowed the GB10's shared memory, and it stopped importing vLLM into every Python interpreter: the first log line dropped from 100 to 6 s.

The 2026-09-14 incident

An 11.9 MB video attached in a chat reached vLLM as ~33 full-resolution images: the client broke the video apart and sent the frames as images. vLLM never splits a video_url into several encoder items; the incident came through the image path (according to start.sh), and VIDEO_NUM_FRAMES, which only samples genuine video_urls, would not have helped. At 8,000 tokens per image, each frame cost ~7.2K tokens: 236,544 tokens of encode. Node 0 was left with 44 MB free, and the kernel killed VLLM::Worker_TP. The file size is deceptive: 11.9 MB of H.264 is ~33 frames, each as expensive as a full page. With the 2,048 ceiling, the same attachment costs 67,320 tokens and gets served.

Video and positionsTENSORFOLD_GLM_VIDEO_FRAMES=128 · TENSORFOLD_GLM_VIDEO_TOKENS=16384

The TensorFold lane's video processor (patch 0003, a mirror of Glm5NextVideoProcessor) also samples at 2 frames per second, but the ceiling is different: up to 128 frames, or 64 s at 2 per second. In a longer clip (up to an hour, decoded with PyAV), the 128 are spread over the whole clip. vLLM's default, for comparison, is to decode 32 frames. The 16,384-token ceiling applies to the whole clip, not to each frame: the resizer picks a single canvas on which every pair fits, so 128 frames (64 pairs) get at most 256 tokens per pair (our calculation). The frame count is forced to be even (the last one repeats), and each pair becomes <|begin_of_image|>…<|end_of_image|> followed by the pair's time as text ({s:.1f} seconds). Since each timestamp is taken from its own pair, there is exactly one per temporal group, and the mismatch that overlay #3 fixes in vLLM has nowhere to arise (our reading of the code). The position of the visual tokens takes no math either. The injection replaces the rows of the <|image|> placeholders (154854) with the tower's vectors in the four residual streams of the hyper-connections. And, since GLM's MLA layers have no rotary part, the positions are simply the token positions, and nothing else in the prompt changes. It is the “NoPE in the text” card above, written as code.

Serving vision in vLLM: from image_url to the encoder cache

Borrowed from future chapters

Five ideas in this section get a chapter of their own later on; for now, one line each is enough:

  • Chunked prefill: the prompt goes in as chunks that share each step with the decodes (chapter 32).
  • UMA: on the GB10, CPU and GPU share the same memory; host RAM comes out of the model's share (chapter 27).
  • CUDA graph: kernels recorded once and replayed without the CPU (chapter 29).
  • MTP as a drafter: guesses the next tokens; the acceptance rate measures how many of them the model confirms (chapter 36).
  • Tensor parallel: each matrix is sliced across GPUs, with an all-reduce of the partial sums (chapter 38).

The front door: API formats

In the OpenAI-compatible API (chapter 34), a message with an image swaps the string content for a list of parts: text and image_url (or video_url). The URL can be http(s)://, which the server downloads, or a data URL with the bytes in base64. The example comes from the DeepSeek recipe's README:

{"model":"deepseek-v4-flash-vision-exp","messages":[{"role":"user","content":[
  {"type":"image_url","image_url":{"url":"data:image/jpeg;base64,..."}},
  {"type":"text","text":"What is in this picture?"}
]}]}

Local paths (file://) only work if the server declares --allowed-local-media-path. Qwen does not declare it, so media arrives only by URL or base64, without exposing the server's disk. DeepSeek also accepts the <image>path</image> form mentioned in the README.

Role rulesimage_url only in user messages

Under Vision-Exp's official rule, images (JPEG, PNG, GIF, WebP) only go into user messages; an image/image_url part or the raw token <｜deepseek_image｜> in system, assistant, tool or function gets an HTTP 400. The hotfix tuned the rule over three rounds: text that merely mentions <image> was let through (#165); tool results stopped being scanned, so that a cat of the hotfix itself would not get a 400 (#167); <image>…</image> pairs quoted in system/assistant prose became plain text (#181). What remains is pitfall #178: typical OpenAI clients do not retry a 400, the rejected message stays in the history and every following turn keeps failing until someone removes it. A tool screenshot goes in a user turn.

Limits apply per request, and that has a side effect. Chat Completions is stateless: the client resends the entire history, images included, on every turn. With GLM's old {"image":4}, a session worked up to the fourth image. From the fifth one on, every turn failed with “At most 4 image(s) may be provided in one prompt”, even text-only ones. That is the “poisoned” session in docs/images-per-request.md.

The internal conveyor belt

Inside the engine, an image passes through seven stations. Follow the steps, toggle the encoder's parallelism mode and resend the same image to watch the caches kick in.

The pieces, one by one:

processor
The multimodal processor runs on the CPU: it decodes, resizes, normalizes and cuts, expands the placeholder and computes the multimodal hash. The result goes into the processor cache (--mm-processor-cache-gb, 4 GiB of RAM by default).
scheduler
When the prefill chunk reaches the placeholders, it has the encoder run the whole image, within a per-step encoder budget.
encoder cache
Keeps the tower's output on the GPU until chunked prefill consumes it, possibly over several steps (the encoder cache). According to the GLM recipe, it is the size of max_num_batched_tokens: 7,168.
LLM
embed_input_ids scatters the vectors into the placeholder rows; from there on it is a normal prefill. The image hash goes into the prefix caching keys: the same image at the same position reuses the KV.
Tokens per image--mm-processor-kwargs '{"max_image_tokens":2048}'

MM_IMAGE_TOKENS=2048. The checkpoint asks for max_image_tokens = 8,000, more than the 7,168 of MAX_NUM_BATCHED_TOKENS, which the recipe documents as the size of the encoder cache: a full image would not fit. A 1080p frame measures 2,691 tokens uncapped, 2,040 with 2,048 and 1,008 with 1,024. Leaving it empty omits the flag and goes back to 8,000 (the script uses ${MM_IMAGE_TOKENS-2048}, so “exporting it empty” survives instead of falling back to the default). For document-level detail in a single image, the recipe suggests raising it toward 7,168 while keeping an eye on the head node's MemAvailable.

Profiling and limits--skip-mm-profiling · --limit-mm-per-prompt '{"image":48,"video":1}'

At boot, vLLM's profile run pushes a fake batch of maximum-size media through the tower, measures the activation peak and reserves that memory before handing the rest to the KV. On the GB10's UMA this profile hits OOM, so the recipe turns on --skip-mm-profiling (SKIP_MM_PROFILING=1). The price is permanent: nothing is reserved for the tower, and every multimodal token is encoded with memory the model has already spent. --limit-mm-per-prompt becomes mere validation (going from 4 to 16 images changed the KV from 12.93 to 12.89 GiB), so the ceiling has to fit what the machine can actually encode. LIMIT_MM went from 4 to 100 and is now 48 (start-tp4.sh still carries 100): a worst case of 48 × 2,048 ≈ 98K tokens, against 800K before. Above the ceiling, the API answers HTTP 500 “At most N image(s)…”, an error instead of a dead engine.

Host RAM--mm-processor-cache-gb 1 · --language-model-only

MM_PROCESSOR_CACHE_GB=1. By default vLLM keeps 4 GiB of RAM for processed media, and on UMA that is 4 GiB the model loses. A 1 GiB cache still avoids reprocessing the images that stateless clients resend; 0 turns the cache off. LANGUAGE_MODEL_ONLY=0 keeps vision on, and 1 passes --language-model-only: the tower (1.13 GB in BF16) is not even loaded, and that memory is freed up. In the MiMo tutorial in chapter 46, the text-only lane gains ~1.6 GiB of KV per rank.

Slice or replicate the tower: --mm-encoder-tp-mode

Under tensor parallel, vLLM's default is to slice the tower as well (weights): each GPU keeps half of every vision matrix and the two add up partial results with an all-reduce. The alternative, data, replicates the whole tower on each GPU and splits the images between them. That costs memory, because the weights are duplicated, and saves communication and divisibility requirements (chapter 38).

Replicated encoder--mm-encoder-tp-mode data

MM_ENCODER_TP_MODE=data is the .env.sample default. The tower's MLP has an intermediate size of 4304; with weights at TP=2, each GPU would get 2152 columns, which is not a multiple of 16 (2152 = 16 · 134.5), and loading crashes. The .env attributes the requirement to the NVFP4 kernels; since visual.* is in BF16, the exact kernel is not documented, but the crash is measured. In data mode, each GPU keeps the whole tower (0.84 GiB in BF16), ~0.42 GiB more than the halves of weights mode, and encodes whole images without any all-reduce.

In , TP=2 uses the default mode (weights): 16 heads and 4096 split by 2 with no trouble. At TP=3, start-tp3.sh passes MM_ENCODER_TP_MODE=data, following the divisibility logic of chapter 40. Interpretation: neither the 16 heads nor the 4096 intermediate size divide by 3.

In the TensorFold lane: the tower on a single rank and a per-request budget

Both vLLM modes start from the same principle: the two GPUs work on vision, either by slicing the matrices (weights) or by splitting the images (data). The lane picks a third way out: only rank 0 loads the tower, with 1.05 GiB of BF16 weights plus 0.75 GiB of workspace (~1.8 GiB), and rank 1 never sees a pixel. The vectors of an image all stay on rank 0. When a prefill chunk reaches the image rows, rank 0 hands rank 1 only that chunk's rows, through an all-gather (rank 1 contributes zeros), and neither of them holds a second copy. Encoding runs before rank 1 is told about the request: if it fails, both stop together instead of diverging.

DesignTower weightsCommunicationWho encodes
weights (vLLM; GLM at TP=2)half of each matrix on each GPUall-reduce of the partial sums inside the towerboth GPUs, together, each image
data (vLLM; Qwen, GLM at TP=3)the whole tower on each GPU (0.84 GiB in Qwen)none in the towereach GPU, different whole images

The price is asymmetry: only rank 0 pays for vision memory. With KV=bf16 and the dense weights in fp8 or bf16, the tower costs rank 0 ~66 thousand tokens of window, according to the recipe (with 4-bit dense weights, the default, the smaller weights give that room back), and VISION=0 gives the ~1.8 GiB back to the cache. In exchange, no matrix is sliced, and the tower is free of the divisibility requirements that brought down Qwen's weights mode (our interpretation). While rank 0 encodes, rank 1 waits (our reading of the code: the encode happens when the request is admitted, before any message goes to rank 1). The recipe does not publish the encode time on its own, only the end-to-end total further below.

The second difference is a philosophy of memory. The vLLM lane turns on --skip-mm-profiling, and nothing is reserved for the tower. TensorFold does the opposite: the vision workspace is part of rank 0's startup budget (chapter 27), and the token ceilings exist so that a request's encode fits in it. The measured peaks recorded in patch 0003's code give the scale: 0.47 GiB for a 2,048-token image, 0.56 for four, 1.08 for a 16,384-token clip and 1.73 for an 8,000-token image (the checkpoint's maximum) encoded all at once, in 31,724 patches. Later, patch 0004 cut the workspace from 1.5 to 0.75 GiB and the tower's pass from 16,384 to 8,192 patches. A 2,048-token image fits in a single pass (2,048 tokens × 4 patches from the 2×2 merge), and the tower's scratch now takes turns with that of a prompt chunk (~1 GiB at 164 thousand tokens).

The third difference is the one that changes behavior the most: the budget applies per request, not just per image. Each image has a ceiling of 2,048 tokens, as in the vLLM lane (a 1080p frame costs 2,040), but the images of a request share 16,384 tokens, and the clips share 32,768:

\[ c_{\text{img}}(n) = \max\!\Big(16,\ \min\big(2048,\ \big\lfloor 16384/n \big\rfloor\big)\Big), \qquad c_{\text{clip}}(m) = \max\!\Big(64,\ \min\big(16384,\ \big\lfloor 32768/m \big\rfloor\big)\Big) \]
  • \(n\): images in the request, up to 50 (TENSORFOLD_GLM_MAX_IMAGES); \(m\): clips, up to 4 (TENSORFOLD_GLM_MAX_VIDEOS)
  • 2048 and 16384 inside the \(\min\): per-item ceilings (TENSORFOLD_GLM_IMAGE_TOKENS, TENSORFOLD_GLM_VIDEO_TOKENS)
  • 16384 and 32768 in the numerator: the request's budgets (TENSORFOLD_GLM_REQUEST_IMAGE_TOKENS, TENSORFOLD_GLM_REQUEST_VIDEO_TOKENS)
  • 16 and 64: floors, so that no item vanishes

Up to 8 images and 2 clips, nobody loses anything, because 16,384 / 8 = 2,048 and the canvases do not change. The recipe checked that replies with up to 4 images and 2 videos came out byte for byte identical to the previous version's. From the 9th image on, each one shrinks: with 50, the ceiling is 327 tokens. With 3 or 4 clips, 10,922 or 8,192 each. The contrast with the vLLM lane lies in the worst case. There, the per-item ceiling multiplies by the number of items, 48 × 2,048 = 98,304 encode tokens with nothing reserved. Here, the worst case for images is constant: 16,384 tokens, whether there are 9 or 50 images. By the formula, the attachment from the incident in section 5 (33 frames) would get 496 tokens per frame, at most 16,368 in total (our calculation), against 67,320 with the vLLM lane's 2,048 ceiling and 236,544 with no ceiling. The price is resolution. Each image gets coarser, and a detail that had a token all to itself now shares the token with its neighbors. In the figure, drag the number of images and compare the two lanes. That was the default through v1.10. Since 2026-10-10 (36df86e), scripts/config.sh sets the image budget to MAX_IMAGES × IMAGE_TOKENS = 50 × 2,048 = 102,400 tokens and reserves 1,440 MiB of tower workspace instead of 768: with the shared 16,384, each picture past the 8th shrank all the earlier ones, their content keys changed and the kept prompt state matched only up to the first picture, so an agent reading screenshots at ~420k tokens re-read 187–191k tokens (150–167 s) after each one (two Sparks, v1.10, PARALLEL=4). TENSORFOLD_GLM_REQUEST_IMAGE_TOKENS=16384 brings back the constant worst case. The lesson holds for any stack: if an item's preprocessing depends on the rest of the request, a content-keyed cache stops matching.

Measured in v1.1 of the recipe, still on TensorFold v0.5.0 (the changelog does not detail the measurement's conditions): 50 images in one request were answered in 12.5 s, with 16,239 prompt tokens, and free memory on the head never dropped below 6.75 GiB; with 4 videos, the minimum was 6.90 GiB. To fit, patch 0050 also lowered the memory peak. Each image is fitted to its canvas while it is decoded, so only one full-size image occupies host RAM at a time, and the tower writes its rows straight into the request's preallocated output, without the extra copy of a torch.cat. In the GB10's unified memory, host RAM and GPU memory are the same pool.

What is left out of reuse

In v1.3.2's TensorFold lane, prompts with images were not kept for reuse: there was no kept prompt state for them, no replay, and in multi-prefill they filled alone, without sharing the forward with other prompts. In the vLLM lane, by contrast, the image hash goes into the prefix caching keys, and the same image at the same position reuses the KV. Our interpretation: an agent that resends a conversation with screenshots every turn finds no state to resume in this lane and pays for the prefill again, while the same conversation with text only resumes where it left off. Since v1.4 (patch 0054, issue #11) prompts with pictures resume too, their kept states told apart by each picture's content, and only the pictures past the resume point are encoded again. In v1.3.2 there was also a scheduling detail. When the pool fills up and the engine needs to send a stream back to the queue to recompute it later, it only picks streams without images and without a grammar; if all of them have one of the two, the newest one fails with NoRoom.

Edge cases that became hotfixes

#172 · salted hashsalt_mm_image_hash(digest, num_tokens)

Remember section 4: the block has 122 + compress_pad tokens, and the pad depends on start_pos % 4. vLLM's encoder cache identified the image by its bytes alone, so the same photo at a different offset reused outputs with the wrong layout (125 placeholders for 124 embeddings), and the EngineCore died. The fix salts the hash with the number of tokens ("{digest}:vlexp-ntok{num_tokens}"), turns off the processor cache for images and makes embed_input_ids reject mismatched counts with a clear error. The general lesson: a cache key must include everything the value depends on.

#109 · encoder-only stepspatches/hotfix-vllm-empty-encoder-output.py

vLLM can split encode, prefill and decode into different instances. An encoder-only instance (the “encoder cache producer”) publishes embeddings and does not sample tokens, but the Anemll 0.1.1 image made up token 0 in those steps ([[0] for _ in req_ids]): the scheduler appended a phantom token, advanced grammars and stop checks, skewed the speculation accounting and got stuck in a loop. The backport of upstream 7ca49fb returns an empty row per request and finishes the encoder-only request (FINISHED_STOPPED) once the prompt has been consumed. Always on and fail-closed: it validates both files before writing either of them.

In the recipe: the drafter does not see the image

The log warns: “Draft model … does not support external multimodal embeddings”. The MTP drafts from text alone, so the acceptance rate drops on prompts with images or video. Live, the recipe verified OCR of 48 px text in a 3840×2160 frame (≈ 8.2K vision tokens), questions about color, shape and scene, and the refusal to read text that is not in the image. Since it is a reasoning model, the thinking consumes max_tokens first: if content comes back empty with finish_reason: "length", raise max_tokens (2K to 4K is enough for most questions).

Grafted vision: DeepSeek Vision-Exp and modality routing

GLM and Qwen arrived in vLLM with vision already implemented in the upstream code. DeepSeek went through three architectures before getting native eyes. The first two were not successive stages: they coexisted in the text-only era, and the first one on the list was removed before the second.

  1. MoonViT lane (deleted first). A MoonViT tower plugged into DeepSeek, with a projector trained in the repository itself (there were training and evaluation scripts). It was removed while the sidecar lived on; the CHANGELOG records that, from then on, vision was “just the Qwen3-VL sidecar + MCP”.
  2. Qwen3-VL sidecar (removed on 2026-08-31). A vision sidecar: a second model, the Qwen3-VL-4B AWQ VLM, ran next to the text-only DeepSeek and described the images via MCP (ds4f-vision); the main model never saw a pixel. The cost was memory: with ENABLE_VL_SIDECAR=1 and GPU_MEMORY_UTILIZATION_VISION = 0.80, the main KV dropped to 13.37 GiB (1.37 M tokens), plus 1.54 GiB (84K) for the VL, against 18.08 GiB (~2.49 M) text-only at 0.835. It was the only vision path until Vision-Exp.
  3. Native Vision-Exp (current). The official DeepSeek-V4-Flash-Vision-Exp checkpoint ships a 32-layer ViT, an Aligner and its own routing bias. The Anemll 0.1.1 runtime, however, only knows the text DeepseekV4ForCausalLM.

For Vision-Exp, patches/hotfix-dsv4-vision-exp.py and the patches/vision_exp/ folder build vision inside the container, at boot, with a fail-closed import hook appended to the end of nvidia/model.py. In order, the hook:

  1. Builds the tower in pure PyTorch. It wraps the model's __init__ to create vision (ViT), aligner and the image_start/end/newline/pad vectors, adapted from the official reference (MIT) without the custom kernels.
  2. Maps the weights. It extends the WeightsMapper to vision.*, aligner.*, image_* and bias_vl, and takes vision./aligner. out of the stacked w1 → gate_up_proj mapping: in the Aligner, w1 is a whole Linear, not half of a fused MLP (chapter 24).
  3. Registers the model as multimodal. It adds SupportsMultiModal and a processor of its own (resize, N layout, salted hash); get_language_model() returns the wrapper itself, so that DSpark and Eagle3 still find .model.
  4. Keeps the raw ids. With an image, the runner passes inputs_embeds, but the MoE hash layers need the ids: requires_raw_input_tokens = True.
  5. Adjusts the drafter and the chat encoder. It remaps ffn.gate.bias_vl → e_score_correction_bias_vl in DSpark's loader, lets image_url survive vLLM's parser and restores the “images only in user” rule.
  6. Leaves the limit to the operator. get_supported_mm_limits() now returns None: the processor used to declare {"image": 16}, and vLLM applied min(16, --limit-mm-per-prompt), so asking for 32 had no effect.

The vision_* fields in config.json control the tower. The hotfix code has defaults (vision_args_from_config): patch 14, downsample 3, max_n_token 384, min_pixels 147,456, max_wh_ratio 8, dim 1024, 16 heads, inter_dim 2816 and rope_theta 10,000. The most important default is vision_n_layers = 0, which turns vision off. The Vision-Exp checkpoint ships 32 and activates the tower and the bias_vl; the text-only 0731 does not ship the field, so it has no vision.

Modality-conditioned MoE routing

Recall DeepSeek-V4's MoE (chapter 11). In layers 3 to 42, the router computes scores \(s\) and picks the top-6 of \(s + b\), where \(b\) is the auxiliary-loss-free balancing bias (e_score_correction_bias). In layers 0 to 2, hash routing takes the router out of the selection: a fixed table tid2eid[token_id] decides the 6 experts from the token's id.

\[ \mathcal{E}(x) = \begin{cases} \texttt{tid2eid}[\,\mathrm{id}(x)\,] & \text{layers 0–2, text} \\ \operatorname{top6}\big(s(x) + \mathbf{b}\big) & \text{layers 3–42, text} \\ \operatorname{top6}\big(s(x) + \mathbf{b}_{\text{vl}}\big) & \text{all layers, image rows} \end{cases} \]
  • \(\mathcal{E}(x)\): the 6 routed experts chosen for token \(x\)
  • \(s(x) = \sqrt{\operatorname{softplus}(x W_g^\top)}\): the router's scores (sqrtsoftplus)
  • \(\mathbf{b}\): e_score_correction_bias, the text bias
  • \(\mathbf{b}_{\text{vl}}\): e_score_correction_bias_vl, the images' own bias, present only in Vision-Exp
  • the weights still come from \(s\) without the bias, normalized and multiplied by 1.5

Now put this together with section 4: every row of an image block has the same id, 129264. Without special handling, the hash table sent every image token, from every photo, to the same 6 experts, tid2eid[129264], in layers 0 to 2. It was a traffic jam of hundreds of tokens through 6 doors, and e_score_correction_bias_vl was loaded and then ignored. That is issue #175. The fix implements the official reference's modality routing: image rows route with the bias_vl and without the hash table.

Without the fix, six cells burn while the rest of the grid cools down; with it, the image load spreads out just like the text load. There is a performance subtlety. To choose the path, each gate would need to know whether the batch is text-only, image-only or mixed, and asking the GPU (.sum().item()) is a host-device sync: 43 gates, 43 waits per forward. The hotfix classifies the batch once, in embed_input_ids, reusing the placeholder count already read for the #172 check (zero extra syncs; a text-only batch does not even pay that one), and publishes the result in a cell that all 43 gates read. Mixed batches are split per row. During CUDA graph capture nothing is resolved and the text path applies: the decode graphs only replay text, and the image prefill runs in eager mode.

Why this matters to recipe writers

A placeholder looks harmless, because “it is just an id that later turns into an embedding”. But any component that looks at the id, rather than at the vector, sees hundreds of identical tokens. That is what happened with the MoE hash table. By extension (our own interpretation), any id-addressed mechanism, such as Qwen's N-grams (chapter 16) or repetition penalties, deserves the same question: what happens to the image tokens? The inventory has no quality number for #175, only the fix to the mechanism.

A recipe for your recipe

When a new multimodal model shows up, go through this list before launching the first vllm serve:

  1. Read the tower. In vision_config and preprocessor_config.json, write down the patch \(p\), the merge \(k\), the temporal patch, the width, the intermediate size and the pixel budget. Compute \(p \cdot k\): that is the side of one token in pixels.
  2. Work out the exchange rate. Tokens per image ≈ area / \((pk)^2\), capped by the budget. Do the math for the real images in your use case: screenshots, documents, video frames.
  3. Fit it into the engine. Tokens per image ≤ encoder cache (≈ max_num_batched_tokens). If the checkpoint asks for more, cap it with --mm-processor-kwargs, as GLM did with 2,048 and the MiMo tutorial did with max_pixels 2,073,600.
  4. Choose limits with the worst case in mind. Items per prompt × tokens per item, remembering that stateless clients resend the history. With --skip-mm-profiling, these limits are the only memory protection you have. A per-request budget shared by the items, like the TensorFold lane's, keeps the worst case constant.
  5. Decide whether to slice or replicate the tower. Check the tower's heads and intermediate size against the TP degree and the kernels' tiles. When in doubt, --mm-encoder-tp-mode data: the tower is small. An engine of your own can also keep it on a single rank, as TensorFold does.
  6. Look for anything that reads the id. Hash tables, N-grams and the drafter: what do they do with hundreds of identical placeholders?
  7. Test like a user. Fine-grained OCR, sessions with many images, video, and images in forbidden roles. Measure the drafter's acceptance rate on prompts with images.
