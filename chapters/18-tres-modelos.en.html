In the previous chapters you took the parts apart one by one: experts and routers, MLA, sparse attention, linear attention, hyper-connections, N-grams and MTP and vision. Now it's time to assemble the three recipe models in full and put them side by side on the workbench — GLM-5.3-Flash, Qwen3.8-Flash-Next and DeepSeek-V4-Flash. The goal is to learn what Mia does before writing the first line of start.sh: open the config.json, draw the model layer by layer, count parameters and bytes, and list everything the inference engine will have to support. By the end you should be able to look at a config you have never seen and predict, without downloading a single weight, how much it weighs, how much it costs per token and where it is going to hurt.

One skeleton, three creatures

Start with what the three have in common, which is quite a lot. All of them are autoregressive decoder-only Transformers: a token comes in, becomes a vector in the embedding, goes through a stack of blocks and comes out as 130 to 250 thousand logits, one for each token in the vocabulary. Each block has the two stages of the classic Transformer block. The first is the token mixer (the mixer), which lets the token look at the past. The second is the feed-forward network (FFN), which processes each token on its own. Both hang off a residual stream, where each stage reads the current state and writes its contribution.

What changes is which part fills each slot. Think of the block as a chassis with four slots: (1) the mixer, (2) the FFN, (3) the width of the residual and (4) the accessories (extra embeddings, built-in drafter, vision tower). In the figure below, switch models and watch the parts change on the same chassis. The little dots running along the lanes are a token's information flowing through the block.

Three convergences jump out of the figure, and none of them is a coincidence. First, nobody uses full attention in every layer: all three are hybrids. GLM and Qwen replace 3 out of every 4 layers with fixed-state linear attention, and DeepSeek compresses the KV of its layers by 4 or by 128. Second, all three widen the residual to 4 streams. Third, all three are fine-grained MoEs, with hundreds of small experts plus one shared expert, which is why they have 15 to 30 times more total parameters than active ones. The first choice attacks the KV cache at long context, the second the training stability of deep networks and the third the FLOP cost per token. Each one creates a new requirement for the engine.

The "active" numbers above follow a single convention, the same one used in the parameters figure later on: they count the lm_head and leave out the embedding, which is just a lookup of one table row. The official cards don't all follow the same rule, and the parameters section shows where each one diverges.

Analogy: same platform, different bodies

Automakers share the same platform (floor pan, suspension, axles) across models that look like they have nothing to do with each other. A mechanic who knows the platform solves 80% of the problems of any of them, but the other 20% (one's turbo engine, the other's air suspension) call for specific tools. vLLM is the mechanic. The decoder-only skeleton is the platform. Each model brings its own exotic parts, and each exotic part becomes a patch.

Why dissect before serving

A good share of the modifications in the three recipes comes from a single line of config.json. qk_rope_head_dim: 0 in GLM produced the NoPE overlay in the Dockerfile. In Qwen, the N-gram table stored in FP8 but declared outside NVFP4 produced the quantization dispatch patch (and the ple_embedding_dtype field passed via override). num_nextn_predict_layers: 3 in DeepSeek Vision-Exp forced the use of 6 speculative tokens. The rest comes from the hardware, not the config: PDL disabled on SM12x, the missing kernels for sm_121, the GB10's unified memory. If you read the config carefully, you can predict the first half of the problems before the first boot, instead of discovering them in a traceback after 7 minutes of loading.

Pocket kit: terms that will only be covered in depth later

This chapter looks at the models through the eyes of someone who is going to serve them, so a few terms from Parts IV to VIII show up ahead of time. Here is the minimum you need to follow along (hover over the underlined terms to see their definitions):

rank · TP · EP · all-reduce
A rank is each process in a distributed group; in the recipes, 1 GPU per DGX Spark, that is, 2 ranks. In tensor parallel (TP=2), each matrix is sliced across the ranks. In a projection sliced along the input dimension (RowParallel), each rank computes a partial sum, and an all-reduce adds up the pieces and hands the result to everyone. In expert parallel (EP), each rank holds whole experts. Covered in depth in parallelism and NCCL and RDMA.
bpw · EXL3 · NVFP4 · MXFP4 · W4A16
bpw means bits per weight. EXL3 4 bpw: weights rotated by Hadamard and encoded in a trellis, ~4 bits each. NVFP4: FP4 (E2M1) numbers with an FP8 scale every 16 values, ~4.5 bits per value. MXFP4: FP4 with a power-of-2 scale (E8M0) every 32, 4.25 bits. W4A16: 4-bit weights and 16-bit activations; the kernel dequantizes and multiplies in BF16 (Marlin is a kernel of this kind). See quantization fundamentals, FP8, FP4 and microscaling and Hadamard and trellis.
GMU · pool · page · block
The GMU (--gpu-memory-utilization) is the fraction of memory vLLM is allowed to use. What is left after the weights and activations becomes the KV pool, split into pages of equal size in bytes. The block is how many tokens of one layer fit in a page. In a hybrid model, all groups use the same uniform page, and the recurrent state enters as a group of type MambaSpec. See PagedAttention and KV.
speculation · k · CUDA graphs
In speculative decoding, a cheap drafter proposes k tokens, and the big model verifies the k + 1 positions (the last accepted token and the k drafts) in a single forward pass, accepting the longest correct prefix. FR-Spec is the variant that computes the draft over a reduced vocabulary. A CUDA graph records a step's kernel sequence so it can be replayed with no CPU cost, but it only works for the sizes that were captured. See speculation fundamentals and CUDA graphs.

The layer-by-layer map

The first drawing every recipe makes is the layer map: for each index i, which mixer, which FFN and what special role the layer has. Out of this map come vLLM's KV groups, the layers a drafter "listens" to, the layers abliteration edits and the number of network synchronizations per step. In the explorer below, each column is a layer: the top rectangle is the mixer, the bottom one is the FFN. A pulse travels along the stack like a token in the forward pass. Click a layer to see its tensors and the block diagram. Switch the color mode to see the KV cost per token or each layer's role in the recipe.

Read GLM's map out loud. There are 45 decoder layers (num_hidden_layers: 45, indices 0–44). The rule i mod 4 = 3 marks the 11 sparse MLA layers (3, 7, 11, …, 43), and the other 34 are KDA: Kimi Linear's 3:1 pattern. Layers 0–2 have a dense MLP (first_k_dense_replace: 3) and the rest are MoE. There is also a 46th entry, the MTP layer at index 45: an MLA+MoE without mHC that predicts the next token. That's why ablit/LAYER_MAP.json has 46 rows, and that's why the README sometimes talks about "12 MLA layers": that count includes the MTP.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{mixer}_{\text{GLM}}(i) &= \begin{cases} \text{MLA-DSA} & i \bmod 4 = 3\\ \text{KDA} & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \\[6pt] \text{mixer}_{\text{Qwen}}(i) &= \begin{cases} \text{QSA} & (i+1) \bmod 4 = 0\\ \text{GDN} & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \end{aligned} \]
  • \(i\): layer index, starting at 0 (it is the N in layers.N in the tensor names)
  • \(i \bmod 4 = 3 \iff (i+1) \bmod 4 = 0\): the two rules are the same one, written two ways
  • KDA, GDN: fixed-state linear attention (chapter 14)
  • MLA-DSA, QSA: sparse softmax attention with an indexer (chapter 13)
Parameternum_hidden_layers: 45 (+ MTP at index 45)

What it is: the depth of the decoder, 34 KDA + 11 sparse NoPE MLA layers at positions 3, 7, …, 43. What it decides in the recipe: (a) the KV groups, with the 11 MLA layers (and their 11 indexer caches) in one attention group and the 34 KDA layers in Mamba-style state groups; (b) the DFlash2 captures, which read the outputs of layers 5, 14, 24, 33 and 42 (all KDA; the log shows 6, 15, 25, 34, 43 because the hook captures at idx+1); (c) the scope of abliteration, which edits layers 15–45 and keeps 0–14 as "anchors". vLLM uses num_hidden_layers as the index of the first MTP layer. With SPEC_METHOD=mtp, that layer 45 becomes the fallback drafter (k = 2). And tools/pack_profile.py rejects any pack that doesn't have exactly 45 layers and hidden 4096.

Qwen follows the same rule with 48 layers: 36 GDN and 12 QSA (3, 7, …, 47), with MoE in all of them. The map reveals two details. The N-gram table lives in the layer at index 1: the config says ple_layer_ids: [2], but that count starts at 1. And the MTP layer, which is called mtp.layers.0 in the checkpoint, is instantiated by vLLM as mtp.layers.48, because it continues the backbone's numbering (num_hidden_layers + i). The recipe needed an entire patch just to create that alias.

DeepSeek is the most different of the three. Its main dimensions (hidden 4096, 64 heads × head_dim 512, 43 layers) hide an attention with no equivalent in the other two: a single 512-dim vector per token (num_key_value_heads: 1) that serves as both key and value. Layers 0 and 1 are sliding window only. From 2 to 42, they alternate between C4A (KV compressed 4:1, with an indexer) and C128A (KV compressed 128:1 and read in full). The first three route experts by a hash of the token id. Layers 40–42 feed DSpark, whose three stages show up as "virtual" layers 43–45 in the compress_ratios vector.

Parameterhidden 4096 · 64 × 512 · 43 layers

Why these three dimensions matter: 64 heads aren't divisible by 3. At TP=3, the recipe pads the 8 groups of the output projection to 9 (64 → 72 heads), with the extra group zeroed and its attention sink set to −∞, so it doesn't steal mass from the softmax. At TP=2, each decode step does 43 all-reduces of wo_b (RowParallel) plus 43 for the MoE. Adding the drafter, embeddings, logits and Markov head, that's ≈108 collectives per step, 30–50 µs each on a RoCE network without GPUDirect: 3 to 5 ms per step just for synchronization. Depth turns into network latency.

Deep dive: counting layers is counting collectives

With tensor parallel, every row-sliced projection ends with an all-reduce. That's why the number of sub-blocks in the map is, almost literally, the number of synchronizations per step. Qwen records ~106 collectives per decode step (36 GDN + 12 attention + 48 MoE + logits gather + 9 from the draft), of ~20 KB each, costing 2–3 ms. The NCCL and RDMA chapter comes back to this math. For now, keep the rule in mind: the layer map is also the map of network traffic.

Reading config.json field by field

config.json is the model's floor plan. Each field becomes at least one of three things: the shape of a tensor (how many rows and columns a matrix has), a kernel requirement (a KV layout, a supported head count) or a term in the memory math. The engine, in turn, reads the file by field name, and that's where the traps lie.

Three of them show up in the recipes.

  1. Different names for the same thing. The GLM checkpoint uses SGLang's names (num_experts_per_tok, hc_mult, mla_use_nope, linear_attn_config). Without the aliases in the Glm5NextTextConfig class, vLLM would fall back on the class default and route 7 experts per token instead of the 8 it was trained with. There would be no error whatsoever, just a subtly worse model.
  2. The model_type picks the code. vLLM's registry maps architectures to a Python class. For Qwen, the day-0 image uses vllm/models/qwen3_8_flash_next/… and vLLM 0.30 uses vllm/models/qwen4_exp/…. A patch bind-mounted onto one path has no effect on the other.
  3. Where the override lands. In Qwen, a --hf-overrides with rope_parameters at the top level of the dictionary never reached text_config, and YaRN became a silent no-op. The recipe's fix (start.sh:919-946) was to nest the override inside "text_config". Even so, the CHANGELOG notes that 1M with YaRN has not yet been validated in the kit, and YARN_ENABLE remains false by default.

In the figure, each model's config appears the way you would read it in an editor, organized by topic. Click any line: the panel explains the field and the mini map lights up the layers it affects.

Two Qwen fields deserve a closer look, because they define the hybrid. full_attention_interval: 4 says that 1 in every 4 layers is "full" attention (here, sparse QSA) and the rest are Gated DeltaNet. layer_types carries the same information spelled out, in 48 entries. Meanwhile, output_gate_type: sigmoid says how the GDN output is filtered: it is multiplied elementwise by \( \sigma(W_z x) \) before out_proj. It's a gate bounded between 0 and 1, which the technical report preferred over the SiLU of the original GDN. The QSA layers have a similar gate built into q_proj itself, which is twice as wide (24 × 256 × 2 = 12,288): half becomes the query and half becomes each head's output gate.

\[ y = W_o\Big[\,\sigma(W_z\,x)\ \odot\ \mathrm{RMSNorm}(o)\,\Big] \]
  • \(x \in \mathbb{R}^{2560}\): the layer input (read from the Gated Residual)
  • \(o\): output of the GDN mixer, 48 heads × 128 = 6144
  • \(W_z\): in_proj_z \([6144 \times 2560]\)
  • \(\sigma\): sigmoid (output_gate_type); \(\odot\): elementwise product
  • \(W_o\): out_proj \([2560 \times 6144]\)
Trap: fields that mislead (or do nothing)

Not every field means what it seems to. In GLM, head_dim: 0 is harmless, because MLA uses qk_*_head_dim and KDA uses linear_attn_config.head_dim. indexer_rope_interleave: true has no effect either, since the indexer inherits rope_dim = 0. GLM's vision tower says rms_norm_eps: 1e-5, but vLLM forces 1e-6, because with 1e-5 the image descriptions degrade and become repetitive. In DeepSeek Vision-Exp, rms_norm_eps is 1e-20 (versus 1e-6 in 0731), a real change whose effect the repository hasn't measured. Moral: read the model's code in the engine too, not just the config.

Total parameters, active parameters and FLOPs per token

The "320B" in a model's name tells you how much memory it takes up, but not how fast it runs. For that you need two numbers: the total parameters, which have to live in memory, and the active parameters, which each token actually uses. In an MoE, they split apart like this:

\[ \begin{aligned} N_{\text{total}} &= N_{\text{dense}} + L_{\text{MoE}}\,(E+s)\,n_{\text{exp}} \\ N_{\text{active}} &= N_{\text{dense}} + L_{\text{MoE}}\,(k+s)\,n_{\text{exp}} \end{aligned} \]
  • \(N_{\text{dense}}\): what every token uses (mixers, norms, routers, wide residual, lm_head)
  • \(L_{\text{MoE}}\): number of MoE layers
  • \(E\): routed experts per layer; \(k\): top-k; \(s\): shared experts
  • \(n_{\text{exp}}\): parameters of one expert (SwiGLU: \(3 \times d \times d_{\text{int}}\))

Do GLM's math by hand. One SwiGLU expert has 3 × 4096 × 2048 = 25,165,824 parameters. There are 288 per layer across 42 decoder MoE layers, which comes to 304.4 B, or 304.4 / 321.3 ≈ 95% of the whole model (321.3 B, counting MTP and vision). The MTP layer is also MoE, with its own 288 experts: adding it in, that's 43 × 288 × 25.17 M = 311.65 B, or 97.0% of the model. But each decoder token wakes up only 8 experts per layer: 8 × 25.2 M × 42 = 8.46 B. Adding the mixers (6.06 B), the shared experts and dense MLPs (1.51 B), and the routers and mHC (0.09 B), you get 16.1 B in the layers. With the lm_head, 16.7 B. Also counting the embedding, as the card does, 17.4 B, close to the "18B active".

This difference in convention shows up in all three cards. GLM counts the embedding and the lm_head, Qwen's "6B" counts neither (6.0 B; with the lm_head, 6.7 B), and DeepSeek's "13B" counts only the lm_head (13.2 B). The figure uses a single rule for all three, lm_head yes and embedding no, and does the same math with the real numbers of each component.

Three takeaways. (1) In all three, 96–97% of the parameters are routed experts: 311.6 of 321.3 B in GLM (counting those in the MTP), 120.8 of 125.7 B in Qwen's backbone and 277.0 of 284.3 B in DeepSeek. That's why quantizing only the experts (EXL3 4 bpw in GLM, NVFP4 in Qwen, MXFP4 in DeepSeek) already solves memory, while the rest, small and sensitive, stays at 8 or 16 bits. Part IV explains why in detail. (2) Qwen has the most extreme ratio: 51.2 B parameters sit in an N-gram table from which each token reads only 16 rows of 160 numbers, that is, 2,560 parameters. It's capacity with almost no FLOPs. (3) Among the active parameters, the mixer weighs a lot: in GLM, the 34 KDA layers add up to 4.7 B, more than half of the active experts.

FLOPs per token: the weights and the context

The most widely used rule of thumb is "FLOPs per token ≈ 2 × active parameters": each weight takes part in one multiplication and one addition. It only counts multiplications by weights. Attention, however, also multiplies the query by every key it reads, and that cost grows with the context. The full accounting has two more terms:

\[ \begin{aligned} \frac{\text{FLOPs}}{\text{token}} \;\approx\;& \underbrace{2\,N_{\text{active}}}_{\text{weights}} \;+\; \underbrace{\sum_{\ell\,\in\,\text{attention}} 4\, h_\ell\, d_\ell\, L_{\text{eff},\ell}}_{\text{attention}} \\ &+\; \underbrace{\sum_{\ell\,\in\,\text{indexer}} 2\, n_I\, d_I\, \frac{L}{r_I}}_{\text{indexer}} \end{aligned} \]
  • factor 2 for the weights: each weight takes part in one multiplication and one addition; it doesn't depend on the context
  • \(h_\ell\), \(d_\ell\): query heads and dimension of each product in layer \(\ell\); the factor 4 adds up \(q\cdot k\) (2) and the weighted average of the values (2)
  • \(L_{\text{eff},\ell}\): how many keys each query actually reads. It is \(L\) in full attention, 2048 + tail in MLA-DSA and QSA, 128 + 512 in C4A, 128 + \(L/128\) in C128A and 0 in the linear layers (KDA/GDN), which only update the state
  • \(n_I\), \(d_I\): heads and dimension of the indexer (32 × 128 in GLM, 4 × 128 in Qwen, 64 × 128 in DeepSeek)
  • \(r_I\): compression of the indexer keys (4 in all three); the indexer scores all \(L/r_I\) compressed keys, which is why it grows with \(L\)

It's the third term that runs up the bill at long context. Take DeepSeek. From the weights, it's 2 × 13.2 B ≈ 26 GFLOP per token, whatever the context. But at 900K tokens, the indexer of the 21 C4A layers scores 225,000 compressed keys per token: 2 × 64 × 128 × 225,000 × 21 ≈ 77 GFLOP (by the formula), three times the cost of the weights. In a 1024-token prefill chunk, that's ≈ 80 TFLOP, and twice that, because the indexer is replicated on both ranks. In bytes, at 1M each query token scans 262,144 keys × 132 B × 21 layers ≈ 0.73 GB, versus ~0.1 GB for the attention itself. The measured effect is that prefill drops from ≈ 2,563 tok/s at 2K of context to ≈ 875 tok/s at 900K, while single-sequence decode varies little (≈ 62–83 tok/s from 256 to 128K tokens).

Now you can see why GLM and Qwen cap \(L_{\text{eff}}\) at 2048 + tail. The second term is expensive per key: 64 heads × 576 dimensions in GLM's MLA kernel, 24 heads × 256 in Qwen's QSA. With the cap, that term stops growing after 2048 tokens. The only thing that keeps growing is the indexer, which has a few cheap heads over keys already compressed by 4. It's the same trade DeepSeek makes by reading 512 compressed entries in the C4A layers. The price is that quality now depends on the indexer choosing well.

Deep dive: 19.5 B or 17.4 B?

With the exact shapes, a KDA layer has 137,732,288 parameters, not ~170 M, and an MLA + indexer layer has 124,914,432. That gives KDA = 4.68 B and MLA + indexer = 1.37 B, or 16.1 B in the layers and 17.4 B with embedding and lm_head (GLM architecture inventory). Swapping ~170 M for 137.7 M across the 34 KDA layers removes only 1.1 B, so that doesn't explain the gap to 19.5 B. The simplest explanation is an addition error in the document (interpretation). The roofline lesson survives the error: at 947 tokens/s of prefill, ~36 GFLOP/token comes to 34 TFLOPS in total, ~14% of the BF16 peak of two GB10s. In other words, prefill was not FLOP-bound, and the recipe went looking for the bottleneck in reading the expert weights. Use the shapes column to count memory and bytes, and treat the repository's column as an order of magnitude.

How many experts does a step wake up?

The active count holds for one token. In decode with speculation, the engine verifies several tokens per step: 8 in GLM (DFlash2 with k = 7), 4 in Qwen (MTP = 3), 7 in DeepSeek (DSpark with k = 6). Each of these tokens picks its own experts. If the routings were independent, uniform draws, the expected number of distinct experts read per layer would be:

\[ \mathbb{E}[\text{distinct}] = E\left[\,1 - \left(1 - \frac{k}{E}\right)^{t}\,\right] \]
  • \(E\): routed experts per layer (288, 512, 256)
  • \(k\): each token's top-k (8, 10, 6)
  • \(t\): tokens verified in the same step
  • \((1-k/E)^t\): probability that an expert is not chosen by any of the \(t\) tokens

With t = 8, GLM wakes up ~58 of the 288 experts per layer (≈ 57 in the approximation of 64 independent picks used in other chapters), seven times more than a single token. At 6 MiB per expert per rank (4 bpw, half at TP=2) and 42 layers, that's ~15 GB read per rank per step just for experts, the biggest slice of a decode step's time. Qwen, with 2.77 MB experts, reads ~32 unique experts per layer by the repository's count. The uniform formula gives ~39. The difference probably comes from neighboring tokens sharing experts and from real routing not being uniform (interpretation). DeepSeek reads at most 42 per layer (6 × 7), at 6.7 MB per rank: ~12 GB per step.

Why it matters

Memory follows the total parameter count, but decode latency follows the bytes read per step, and those grow with the number of distinct experts. Speculation is still worth it, because the dense weights are read once for all \(t\) tokens. But in an MoE it pays off less than in a dense model: each extra token wakes up new experts. That's why the GLM recipe measures acceptance position by position and has GLM53_ADAPTIVE_K, which verifies only 2, 4 or 7 drafts when acceptance drops (Part VII).

KV and state: per token and per sequence

The third number you need to pull out of the config is how much each sequence costs in memory on top of the weights. In all three models there are two kinds of cost. One grows with the context: the KV of the attention layers. The other is fixed per sequence: the recurrent state of the linear layers, the sliding windows and the drafter's window.

\[ \begin{aligned} M_{\text{seq}}(L) \;=\;& L \sum_{\ell\,\in\,\text{attention}} \frac{b_\ell}{r_\ell} \;+\; L \sum_{\ell\,\in\,\text{indexer}} \frac{b^{I}_\ell}{r^{I}_\ell} \\ &+\; \sum_{\ell\,\in\,\text{linear}} S_\ell \;+\; \sum_{\ell\,\in\,\text{windows}} W_\ell\, b_\ell \end{aligned} \]
  • \(L\): tokens in the sequence's context
  • \(b_\ell\): bytes of one KV entry in layer \(\ell\), per rank
  • \(r_\ell\): compression ratio (1, 4 or 128)
  • \(b^{I}_\ell\), \(r^{I}_\ell\): the same for the indexer's key cache, which is a separate tensor: 132 B per entry every 4 tokens in GLM and DeepSeek (33 B/token per layer)
  • \(S_\ell\): bytes of the recurrent state (constant in \(L\))
  • \(W_\ell\): size of the sliding window

The first step is knowing how many bytes one KV entry takes. The figure takes each model's cache row apart byte by byte. It also shows a pattern that repeats in all three: the indexer keys are compressed in blocks of 4 tokens before they go into the cache.

Look at GLM: 128 of the 656 bytes are zeros. The model is NoPE in the MLA layers (qk_rope_head_dim: 0), but the GB10's only sparse MLA kernel requires the fp8_ds_mla record with 64 RoPE dimensions. The recipe fills that part with zeros. This is mathematically exact, because a null block adds zero to the \(q\cdot k\) product, and it costs ~24% more KV in those 11 layers. DeepSeek uses 584 B: 448 dimensions in FP8 trained with QAT, 64 RoPE dimensions in BF16 and 8 bytes of power-of-2 scales. Then it divides that cost by 4 or by 128. Qwen stores K and V separately, for 2 heads of 256 (one per GPU at TP=2).

Now stretch the context. In the calculator, drag the sequence length and watch the part that depends on \(L\) grow while the fixed part stays put. Each model's marker shows the KV pool measured at the recipe's boot.

At 1M = 1,048,576 tokens (220, the calculator's value), per rank: GLM grows by 7,579 B/token (11 layers × (656 + 33)), 7.40 GiB, plus ~76 MiB of fixed KDA state and ~20 MiB for the drafter window. DeepSeek grows by ~3,850 B/token (3,066 from the C4A layers, 91 from the C128A layers and 693 from the indexer), 3.76 GiB, plus ~3.4 MB fixed for the windows. Qwen, with FP8 KV, grows by 6,656 B/token per GPU in the 13 layers with KV (12 QSA + the MTP), 6.50 GiB, and has ~27 MiB of GDN state in BF16. But Qwen only reaches 1M with YaRN, because its native context is 262,144, and the kit hasn't validated 1M with YaRN yet. Turn on the comparison and see what the hybrid design avoided: without it, the same models would pay from ~3.7× (Qwen: 24,576 versus 6,656 B/token) to ~6.5× (DeepSeek: 25,112 versus 3,850) more in KV alone, with GLM in between (29,520 versus 7,579, 3.9×). And a classic BF16 MHA in GLM would cost 2.95 MB per token, almost 400 times more.

Deep dive: why KV doesn't shrink with TP in GLM and DeepSeek

Tensor parallel splits heads across GPUs. In GLM, however, the MLA KV is a single 512-dim latent per token, and in DeepSeek it is a single vector (num_key_value_heads: 1). There are no KV heads to split, so each rank stores the whole cache (the indexer's too). More ranks only help indirectly, because each rank holds fewer weights and has more free memory: at TP=3 DeepSeek reaches ~35 GiB of pool per rank, 5.0 M tokens. Qwen is different: 2 KV heads become 1 per GPU and the KV per GPU is cut in half.

Trap: physical bytes are not the allocator's accounting

vLLM uses a single page size for all the KV groups of a hybrid model (chapter 31). In GLM, the attention block has to fit the entire KDA state: 2,342,912 / 656 = 3,571.5 tokens, rounded up to the next multiple of 256, gives the 3,584-token block that appears in the boot log. In Qwen, the attention block matches the GDN page (1,664 tokens with SSM in BF16). In DeepSeek, the allocator reports 2.33 M tokens and "2.22× at 1M", but counts ~7.8 KB/token, twice the physical cost. Do the physical math to understand the model and trust the boot log to size the recipe.

Innovations and lineage

None of these models came out of nowhere. Each one is the latest chapter of a family, and the families trade ideas all the time. Understanding lineage helps you predict the next model: an idea that appears in one lab tends to reappear in the others months later. And the support the engine already has for the old version is the starting point for the patch for the new one.

🧬

GLM-5.3-Flash

KDA + NoPE MLA at 3:1; DSA indexer over pools of 4 keys (512 pools = 2048 tokens + tail); mHC with 20-iteration Sinkhorn; 288 experts with sigmoid routing and no auxiliary loss, ×2.5.

🧪

Qwen3.8-Flash-Next

QSA (compressed indexer r = 4, budget 2048); Gated Residual (elementwise read, scalar write, no mixing matrix); 51 B N-gram embedding in the 2nd layer (index 1: ple_layer_ids: [2] counts from 1); MTP with QSA.

🔭

DeepSeek-V4-Flash

CSA/HCA with a 128-token window; K = V in a single 512-dim vector and inverse RoPE on the output; sqrtsoftplus and hash routing in layers 0–2; DSpark with a Markov head.

In the animation, each subway line is a family and each station is a model. The dashed lines between families are ideas that crossed over from one to another. Step through and watch the ideas travel. Look at the end of GLM's line: GLM-5.3 and GLM-5.3-Flash are siblings, with GLM-5 as their common ancestor, not one descended from the other. The architectures are different (GlmMoeDsaForCausalLM in 5.3, KDA + mHC in Flash), and the sources don't even agree on the big sibling's size: 753B in the recipe-from-scratch tutorial, 744B in the InferenceX cited by the inventory.

Three lines of force jump out of the animation. DeepSeek-V2's MLA stores the KV as a 512-dim latent plus a 64-dim RoPE key. The 512 latent made it to GLM, but without the RoPE part (NoPE) and with 256/256 heads instead of 128. In V4 the idea was taken to the extreme: the 512 vector is the key and the value themselves. V3.2's indexer became a family: GLM applies it over pools of 4 keys, Qwen reinvented it as QSA with blocks of 4 and V4 has it pick entries that are already compressed. And the linear attention that Qwen3-Next popularized (3 GDN : 1 attention), refined by Kimi Linear with KDA, became the skeleton of two of the three models.

Deep dive: where lineage becomes engineering

The kernel zoo preserves lineage like a fossil record. FlashInfer for the GB10 instantiates sparse MLA kernels for only three geometries: DSV3_2 and GLM_NSA (\(d_{qk}\) = 576, 656 B record, top-k ∈ {128, 512, 1024, 2048}) and DSV4 (\(d_{qk}\) = 512, 584 B, top-k ≤ 1024). GLM-5.3-Flash needs \(d_{qk}\) = 512 with top-k 2048, a combination for which no kernel exists. The way out was to dress up as "GLM_NSA" with 64 RoPE zeros. When you go to serve a new model, first ask which ancestor it inherits its attention geometry from. That ancestor is what the engine already knows how to run.

What the engine needs to support

Now bring it all together into a list of requirements. Every row of the map and every config field that departs from the "standard Transformer" turns into a question for the engine. Is there a kernel for this attention on my hardware? Is there a cache layout? Does the allocator know how to page this state? Does the drafter know how to read this residual? In the matrix below, each cell is a traffic light. Green: the vLLM in the recipe's image already supports it, at most with a flag. Amber: the recipe needed a patch, overlay or hotfix. Gray: doesn't apply. Click a cell to read how each recipe solved it.

Read the matrix first by column and then by row. By column, Qwen is the one that works best out of the box in the mixers and the residual, because the day-0 image already shipped GDN and Gated Residual. On the other hand, it's the only one with a 51 GB embedding the loader didn't know how to read. GLM and DeepSeek pile up patches because their attention geometries don't match the sm_121 kernels. By row, three requirements came out amber in all three models: sparse attention, hybrid KV and drafter. The reading I propose (interpretation, not a measurement) is that this doesn't look like chance: these are the three areas where 2026 architectures changed the most and where GB10 kernel support fell furthest behind. When a new model shows up, it's worth looking for work there first.

  1. Sum up parameters by tensor type and decide what to quantize. The bulk is almost always the experts.
  2. For each layer type, ask: which kernel exists on my hardware? What KV layout does it require? Can I transform my model exactly into that layout, with zero padding, fusion or a reshape?
  3. For each kind of state (full KV, compressed KV, window, recurrent state, draft), compute bytes per token and per sequence, and how the allocator will page it. The block has to hold the largest state.
  4. For the drafter: which layers the features come from, how to materialize them through a non-standard residual, what mask it needs (causal or bidirectional within the block) and which KV dtype its backend accepts.
  5. For parallelism: list every dimension that will be split (heads, groups, intermediate sizes, vocabulary, experts) and check divisibility. Use expert parallel when padding would break a packed format. The chapters on TP=3 and TP=4 and on recipe engineering turn this checklist into a method.

Exercise: from config.json to a prediction

Time to apply the method to a model that hasn't appeared on this trail yet: MiMo-V2.6-Flash-MOPD (Xiaomi, September 2026), chosen in the recipe from scratch tutorial. You get only the config.json and two facts from the card (309 B total, 15 B active). Before advancing each step of the figure, try to answer on your own: how many layers of each type? How much does it weigh per rank at TP=2? How many KV bytes per token? How much fits in the pool? What is going to break in the engine?

Notice how the whole chapter's method fit into seven steps. The layer map tells you where the growing KV is: here, 9 global layers. The parameter count tells you whether it fits: ~82 GiB per rank. The bytes-per-token math tells you how much context fits: 5,760 B/token/rank in FP8. And the allocator's accounting corrects the physical math: with groups of 5 layers, the 9 global layers take up 10 "slots", 11% more, which is why the 16.74 GiB pool yields ~2.81 M tokens instead of the 3.12 M from straight division. And the list of non-standard parts tells you where the work will be. K and V with different dimensions, a fused QKV pre-sliced for TP=4, sinks only in the window layers and MXFP4 experts are four requirements you already know how to look for. Three of them have already come up in this chapter: K ≠ V in GLM's absorbed MLA (576 for the key, counting the RoPE pad, and 512 for the value), DeepSeek's attention sinks and DeepSeek's MXFP4 experts. The QKV pre-sliced for a different TP is new, and it's exactly the kind of surprise the method helps you find early.
