DPO swapped RLHF's RL loop for a logistic regression over pairs of responses and became the standard for open post-training. But the recipe carries three hidden assumptions. It treats the preference label as a probability that can be pushed all the way to infinity. It requires pairs (this response is better than that one). And at every step it pays for a second full model, the reference, scoring each response by the sum of its tokens' log-probs. Each famous variation tweaks one of these pieces. IPO swaps the logit for a parabola with a finite target and gives the temperature \(\tau\) back its power to hold the policy close to the reference. KTO drops the pairs and learns from a simple “good” or “bad”, using Kahneman and Tversky's prospect theory. SimPO throws away the reference, scores each response by its mean log-prob and demands a margin. Along the way come ORPO, CPO, R-DPO, β-DPO and DPOP. By the end you will compute all of these losses on the same toy pair, see where each one pushes the model, and know which one to pick, and how to configure it, for your data and your memory.

The theme: one loss, three assumptions

Let's pin down the notation, which is the same as in the previous chapter and holds for the whole chapter. \(x\) is the prompt; \(y\) is a complete response, with \(|y|\) tokens; \(y_w\) is the preferred one (winner, “chosen”) and \(y_l\) the dispreferred one (loser, “rejected”). \(\pi_\theta\) is the model being trained and \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) is the frozen reference model, almost always the post-SFT model itself. The log-prob of a response is the sum of its tokens' log-probs, \(\log\pi(y\mid x)=\sum_t \log\pi(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})\). The central piece is the log-ratio between policy and reference, measured in nats:

\[ h_\theta(x,y)=\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)},\qquad \Delta h = h_\theta(x,y_w)-h_\theta(x,y_l),\qquad \mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}=-\mathbb E_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal D}\big[\log\sigma(\beta\,\Delta h)\big] \]
  • \(h_\theta\): how much the policy raised (\(>0\)) or lowered (\(<0\)) the response relative to the reference
  • \(\beta h_\theta\): DPO's implicit reward
  • \(\Delta h\): the log-ratio margin between preferred and rejected
  • \(\sigma(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})\): the sigmoid; \(-\log\sigma(z)\) is the logistic loss
  • \(\beta\): price of the KL in the RL objective and scale of the logit in the loss (DPO, Eq. 7)
  • \(\mathcal D\): the dataset of triples \((x, y_w, y_l)\)

Read this loss as a three-part machine. A score per response (\(\beta h\)). A contrast function between the two scores (the logistic of the difference). And an anchor that keeps the policy from running away, which in DPO lives inside the score, in the division by \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\). Almost the whole family fits this mold. What changes from one method to another is which piece gets swapped, and each swap attacks one of DPO's assumptions.

∞

Assumption 1: the label is elastic

The Bradley–Terry model behind DPO turns preferences into reward differences through the logit. A pair preferred 100% of the time asks for an infinite margin. IPO swaps the contrast: a parabola with a finite target.

±

Assumption 2: data comes in pairs

Production logs bring “liked” and “disliked”, not pairs. KTO swaps the data and the contrast: each response is judged on its own against a reference point.

Σ

Assumption 3: the reference and the sum

The reference costs memory, and scoring by the sum makes long responses dominate the gradient and feeds verbosity (the raw sum, without a reference, even punishes a long preferred response). SimPO swaps the score and the anchor: per-token mean, no reference, plus a margin \(\gamma\).

To compare apples with apples, the whole chapter uses the same toy pair from the research note. The numbers are illustrative (computed by script, not taken from any paper), but they were chosen to show the typical case of verbosity bias: the preferred response is the longer one.

IPO: when DPO believes the labels too much

First: the right name

You will find IPO expanded as Identity Policy Optimization. The name circulates for a reason: the OAIF paper (Guo et al., 2024), whose authors include Bilal Piot, also a coauthor of IPO, writes exactly “identity policy optimisation”. But the original paper, by Azar, Rowland, Piot, Guo, Calandriello, Valko and Munos (Google DeepMind, 2023; AISTATS 2024), defines a family called ΨPO, “Ψ-preference optimisation”, and calls the case where Ψ is the identity function “Identity-PO (IPO)”, without expanding the PO. By construction, the canonical expansion is Identity Preference Optimization. It is one and the same method; in this chapter we use the paper's name.

The paper's title is ambitious: A General Theoretical Paradigm to Understand Learning from Human Preferences. The idea is to take a step back from DPO. Instead of maximizing a reward, maximize a function \(\Psi\) of the probability of beating the responses that generated the data, without straying from the reference:

\[ \max_\pi\ \mathbb E_{x\sim\rho,\ y\sim\pi(\cdot\mid x),\ y'\sim\mu(\cdot\mid x)}\Big[\Psi\big(p^*(y\succ y'\mid x)\big)\Big]-\tau\,\mathbb D_{\text{KL}}(\pi\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}) \]
  • \(\Psi:[0,1]\to\mathbb R\): a non-decreasing function that turns preference probability into “value”
  • \(p^*(y\succ y'\mid x)\): the true probability that a human prefers \(y\) to \(y'\)
  • \(\mu\): the policy that generated the dataset's pairs
  • \(\rho\): the prompt distribution
  • \(\tau>0\): KL strength, the same role as DPO's \(\beta\)
  • \(\mathbb D_{\text{KL}}\): the KL divergence to the reference

This is the ΨPO objective (IPO, Eq. 6). By the same derivation that gives DPO's closed-form solution, the optimum is the reference “tilted” by the expected value: \(\pi^*(y)\propto\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)\exp\big(\tau^{-1}\,\mathbb E_{y'\sim\mu}[\Psi(p^*(y\succ y'))]\big)\) (Eq. 7). The paper's Proposition 1 closes the loop: if \(\Psi\) is the logit, \(\Psi(q)=\log\frac{q}{1-q}\), and preferences follow Bradley–Terry, then \(\mathbb E_{y'}[\Psi(p^*(y\succ y'))]=r(y)-\mathbb E_{y'}[r(y')]\): the reward itself, up to a constant. In other words, RLHF and DPO have the same optimal policy as ΨPO with Ψ = logit. DPO is not “the” preference method; it is one member of a family, the one that chose the logit.

The problem with the logit

The logit is unbounded. Near 1 it blows up: taking the preference probability from 0.98 to 0.99 requires +0.70 in logit, more than going from 0.5 to 0.6, which takes +0.41 (our calculation). In DPO this becomes a concrete demand on the margin. If, in a pair, the preferred response wins with frequency \(p\), the logistic loss is minimized when \(\beta\,\Delta h^*=\operatorname{logit}(p)\), that is, \(\Delta h^*=\operatorname{logit}(p)/\beta\). The reason fits in one line: the expected loss is \(-p\log\sigma(z)-(1-p)\log\sigma(-z)\), with \(z=\beta\Delta h\), and its derivative, \(\sigma(z)-p\), vanishes when \(\sigma(z)=p\), that is, \(z=\operatorname{logit}(p)\). For a deterministic preference, \(p=1\), the optimal margin is infinite, whatever \(\beta\) is, and the optimal policy drives \(\pi(y_l)\) to zero. In the paper's words (paraphrased), the KL regularization becomes weaker and weaker the more deterministic the preferences are.

Now add finite data. Most datasets have a single label per pair. With one label, the empirical frequency \(\hat p\) can only be 0 or 1: every pair in the dataset is “deterministic”, even if the true preference is a lukewarm 0.8. With \(n\) labels and true preference \(p^*\), the chance that all of them agree is \(p^{*n}+(1-p^*)^n\) (our calculation): 1 with \(n=1\), 0.52 with \(n=3\) and 0.33 with \(n=5\), for \(p^*=0.8\). The paper notes that classic RLHF suffers less from this for a curious reason: the reward model is underfit. It never reaches infinite rewards, because it is trained with regularization and early stopping, and that underfitting regularizes the policy. DPO, by eliminating the reward model, loses this implicit protection.

With one label per pair, all of DPO's points run off to infinity, while IPO's stop at \(\pm 1/(2\tau)\). Raise \(n\) and the points spread across the middle of the curve, but DPO is still hostage to the unanimous pairs. IPO's curve is a bounded straight line, and that is what the next section derives.

The IPO loss: a parabola with a finite target

Choose \(\Psi=\) identity. The objective becomes \(\max_\pi\ p^*_\rho(\pi\succ\mu)-\tau\,\mathbb D_{\text{KL}}(\pi\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}})\) (IPO, Eq. 8): maximize the probability of beating the data policy, which never exceeds 1, without straying too far from the reference. Without the logit, nothing demands an infinite margin. What remains is to turn this into a loss that can be trained from samples.

The paper's path goes through root-finding equations. Define \(g(y)=\mathbb E_{y'\sim\mu}[\Psi(p^*(y\succ y'))]\) and the cross log-ratio \(h_\pi(y,y')=\log\frac{\pi(y)\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y')}{\pi(y')\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y)}\), which is exactly our \(\Delta h\). The optimal policy satisfies \(h^*(y,y')=\tau^{-1}\big(g(y)-g(y')\big)\) for every pair (Eq. 11). With \(\Psi=I\), the right-hand side is \(\tau^{-1}\big(p^*(y\succ\mu)-p^*(y'\succ\mu)\big)\). An equality that must hold on every pair becomes a least-squares regression:

\[ L(\pi)=\mathbb E_{y,y'\sim\mu}\Big[\Big(h_\pi(y,y')-\frac{p^*(y\succ\mu)-p^*(y'\succ\mu)}{\tau}\Big)^2\Big] \]
  • \(p^*(y\succ\mu)\): the probability that \(y\) beats a response drawn from the data policy
  • \(h_\pi(y,y')\): the log-ratio margin between \(y\) and \(y'\)
  • Theorem 2: if \(\operatorname{Supp}(\mu)=\operatorname{Supp}(\pi_{\text{ref}})\), \(L\) has a unique minimum, both local and global

In logits, \(L\) is a strictly convex quadratic, except along the direction \((1,\dots,1)\), which doesn't change the softmax (IPO, Eq. 13 and Theorem 2). We don't know \(p^*\), but Proposition 3 shows that replacing it with a Bernoulli label \(I(y,y')\), which is 1 when \(y\) wins and 0 when it loses, gives the same loss up to a constant (Eq. 16):

\[ L(\pi)=\mathbb E_{y,y'\sim\mu,\ I\sim\text{Bernoulli}(p^*(y\succ y'))}\Big[\big(h_\pi(y,y')-\tau^{-1}I(y,y')\big)^2\Big]+C \]
  • \(I(y,y')\): one annotator's label for the ordered pair \((y,y')\)
  • \(C\): a constant that does not depend on \(\pi\)

Each pair in the dataset then contributes twice, as \((y_w, y_l, I=1)\) and \((y_l, y_w, I=0)\). In the swapped order the margin flips sign, \(h(y_l,y_w)=-h\), and the residual becomes \((-h-0)^2=h^2\). The symmetry settles the math: \(\tfrac12\big[(h-\tau^{-1})^2+h^2\big]=\big(h-\tfrac{1}{2\tau}\big)^2+\tfrac{1}{4\tau^2}\). The same calculation gives the straight line of the previous figure. If the preferred response wins with frequency \(p\), the average of the two orders, \(\tfrac12\big[p(h-\tau^{-1})^2+(1-p)h^2\big]+\tfrac12\big[(1-p)(h+\tau^{-1})^2+p\,h^2\big]\), has derivative \(2h-(2p-1)\tau^{-1}\) and is minimized at \(h^*=(2p-1)/(2\tau)\): the mint line, which equals \(1/(2\tau)\) when \(p=1\). With \(p=1\) and the constant dropped, what remains is the IPO loss (Eq. 17, Algorithm 1):

\[ \mathcal L_{\text{IPO}}=\mathbb E_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal D}\Big[\Big(\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l\mid x)}{\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}-\frac{1}{2\tau}\Big)^2\Big]=\mathbb E\Big[\Big(\Delta h-\frac{1}{2\tau}\Big)^2\Big] \]
  • \(\Delta h\): the same log-ratio margin as in DPO
  • \(1/(2\tau)\): the finite target for the margin; large \(\tau\) = small target = stays close to the reference
  • at the start, \(\pi_\theta=\pi_{\text{ref}}\) and \(\Delta h=0\)

Instead of pushing the margin to infinity, IPO regresses it toward a target. The gradient shows the qualitative difference (derivation from the research note):

\[ \nabla_\theta\mathcal L_{\text{IPO}}=2\,\mathbb E\Big[\Big(\Delta h-\frac{1}{2\tau}\Big)\big(\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)-\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)\big)\Big] \qquad \text{vs.}\qquad \nabla_\theta\mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}=-\beta\,\mathbb E\Big[\sigma(-\beta\Delta h)\big(\nabla\log\pi_\theta(y_w)-\nabla\log\pi_\theta(y_l)\big)\Big] \]
  • IPO, \(\Delta h<1/(2\tau)\): the coefficient is negative and descent raises \(y_w\) and lowers \(y_l\), as in DPO
  • IPO, \(\Delta h>1/(2\tau)\): the coefficient flips sign and descent pulls back
  • DPO: the weight \(\sigma(-\beta\Delta h)\) only tends to zero; it never flips sign

On our pair, in the paper's form (sum of log-probs), IPO gives very different verdicts depending on \(\tau\). With \(\tau=0.1\), the target is 5 and the pair (\(\Delta h=3\)) still falls short: loss 4 and derivative −4 with respect to \(\Delta h\), so descent raises the margin. With \(\tau=0.5\), the target is 1 and the pair has already passed it: loss 4, derivative +4, and descent pulls back. With \(\tau=1\), target 0.5, loss 6.25 and derivative +5. DPO, on the same pair, always pushes forward.

Two toy experiments from the paper

Two actions, deterministic preference (IPO §5.3.1): \(p^*(y_1\succ y_2)=1\), with uniform \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) and \(\mu\). DPO converges to \(\pi(y_1)=1\) for any \(\tau\). In IPO, \(p^*(y_1\succ\mu)=3/4\) and \(p^*(y_2\succ\mu)=1/4\). With uniform \(\mu\), half the time \(y'=y_1\) (a tie with itself, ½) and half the time \(y'=y_2\) (a win, 1): \((\tfrac12+1)/2=3/4\); for \(y_2\), \((0+\tfrac12)/2=1/4\). By Eq. 11, the difference, 1/2, divided by \(\tau\) is the optimal log-ratio between \(y_1\) and \(y_2\); with a uniform reference, the optimum is \(\pi^*(y_1)=\sigma(0.5\,\tau^{-1})\): 0.9933 with \(\tau=0.1\); 0.8808 with 0.25; 0.7311 with 0.5; 0.6225 with 1; 0.5622 with 2; 0.5250 with 5. As \(\tau\to\infty\) it goes back to uniform, and as \(\tau\to0\) it becomes deterministic: \(\tau\) controls the distance from the reference again.

Three actions, total order (IPO §5.4): \(\mathcal D_1=\{(a,b),(b,c),(a,c)\}\), a softmax policy with \(\theta\in\mathbb R^3\), uniform reference, Adam with LR 0.01 for 18,000 steps (the paper uses mini-batch 9; the research note's reproduction, which the figure replays live, uses the full batch). Since the reference is uniform and the softmax cancels in differences, a pair's margin is just \(\theta_w-\theta_l\). IPO has a closed-form solution (derivation from the note): \(\theta_a-\theta_b=\theta_b-\theta_c=1/(3\tau)\).

DPO learns the order and doesn't stop: for any \(\tau\), within 18,000 steps \(a\) takes everything and \(c\) vanishes, even though no data says \(c\) is unacceptable, only that it is worse. IPO learns the same order, but parks where \(\tau\) tells it to: with \(\tau=1\), \(c\) still keeps 23%. The paper shows a second scenario in which an action with no wins at all is zeroed out by DPO for every \(\tau\), while IPO keeps it close to the reference.

\(\tau\) in practice: the normalization trap

In TRL, IPO is the DPOTrainer itself with loss_type "ipo", and the beta parameter plays the role of \(\tau\). Crucial detail: the current code normalizes per token. It uses each response's mean per-token log-ratio before subtracting \(1/(2\beta)\). The comment in the code says this isn't explicit in the paper, but was confirmed with the authors, and that the paper's results correspond to the normalized form. On our pair, \(\Delta h_{\text{norm}}=1/20-(-2)/10=0.25\), and the same \(\tau\) now means something else:

Watch the scale of τ

In the normalized form, \(\Delta h\) is about \(|y|\) times smaller than with the sum. A \(\tau=0.1\) asks for 5 nats per token, a huge departure from the reference; \(\tau\in[0.5,\ 2]\) gives targets from 1 to 0.25 nat per token. The sweep published in the SimPO paper (Table 7) uses \(\tau\in\{0.01,\ 0.1,\ 0.5,\ 1.0\}\) (without stating whether the implementation sums or averages per token). Values of \(\tau\) across implementations (sum × mean) are not comparable: check the code before copying a hyperparameter.

When to use it. When labels are deterministic or there is only one per pair, when data is scarce or noisy, and when you want real anchoring to the reference without relying on early stopping. In the Direct Alignment Algorithms comparison by Rafailov, Chittepu, Park et al. (NeurIPS 2024; TL;DR with Pythia 1B to 6.9B), IPO seemed less prone to over-optimization, especially at 6.9B, and it was the only one in which the implicit reward accuracy showed some correlation, a weak one, with final quality, a correlation the authors attribute to model scale, not to the method. The risk is the opposite of DPO's: underfitting if \(1/(2\tau)\) is too small. In the Smaug paper (Pal et al., 2024), on a math dataset with low edit-distance pairs, IPO “does not improve”.

KTO: prospect theory becomes a loss function

KTO, by Ethayarajh, Xu, Muennighoff, Jurafsky and Kiela (ICML 2024; we use arXiv v5, from September 8, 2026), starts with an unexpected question: why does DPO work so well? The proposed answer is that DPO's loss unintentionally embeds a model of how humans perceive value. If that is what matters, you can write a loss that models this bias on purpose and, as a bonus, does away with pairs.

Prospect theory in five minutes

Offer someone a bet that pays $100 with an 80% chance and nothing with 20%. The expected value is $80. Even so, many people accept a guaranteed $60 rather than bet. Kahneman and Tversky's prospect theory explains this with two ideas: humans evaluate outcomes relative to a reference point, not in absolute terms, and they are loss-averse. Formally there is a value function \(v\), which maps an outcome to subjective value relative to \(z_0\), and a weighting function \(\omega\), which distorts probabilities (for example, by overestimating rare events); the utility is \(u(Z)=\sum_z\omega_z\,v(z-z_0)\). KTO ignores \(\omega\), because humans don't see the LLM's full distribution. The classic form from Tversky and Kahneman (1992) is (KTO, Eq. 4):

\[ v(z;\lambda,\alpha,z_0)=\begin{cases}(z-z_0)^{\alpha} & z\ge z_0\\[2pt] -\lambda\,(z_0-z)^{\alpha} & z<z_0\end{cases}\qquad \alpha\approx0.88,\ \ \lambda\approx2.25 \]
  • \(z_0\): the reference point (the psychological “zero”)
  • \(\alpha<1\): concavity in gains (risk aversion) and convexity in losses
  • \(\lambda>1\): loss aversion, the curve is steeper below \(z_0\)
  • 0.88 and 2.25: medians measured by Tversky and Kahneman

With these medians, \(v(1)=1\) and \(v(-1)=-2.25\): losing one unit hurts 2.25 times as much as gaining one unit pleases. \(v(10)\approx7.586\) and \(v(-10)\approx-17.068\). The exact shape varies from person to person; what matters are three traits: a reference point, concavity in gains and loss aversion.

HALOs: DPO was already “human”

The paper defines a class of losses, HALOs (human-aware losses, Def. 3.4). The ingredients are the implicit reward \(r_\theta(x,y)=l(y)\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\), with a normalizer \(l\), a reference point distribution \(Q(Y'\mid x)\) and a non-decreasing value function \(v_{x,y}\). The human value of \((x,y)\) is \(v_{x,y}\big(r_\theta(x,y)-\mathbb E_Q[r_\theta(x,Y')]\big)\) (Eq. 5). A loss is a HALO if it can be written as \(\mathbb E_{x,y\sim\mathcal D}\big[a_{x,y}\,v_{x,y}(\cdot)\big]+C_{\mathcal D}\) with \(a_{x,y}\neq 0\) (Eq. 6), where \(l\), \(a\), \(v\) and \(C\) cannot depend on the policy or the reference; only \(Q\) can.

Theorem 3.5 says that DPO and PPO-Clip are HALOs; conditional SFT (CSFT) and SLiC are not. In the experiments, HALOs match or beat non-HALOs at every scale tested (Pythia 1.4B to 12B, Llama 7B to 30B), with a significant difference (p < 0.05) only from 13B up. And a curious result motivated the method: an offline PPO with dummy +1/−1 rewards matches DPO at almost every scale (except Llama-30B). The right inductive bias seems to matter more than the learned reward.

The KTO loss

KTO makes three changes to the Kahneman–Tversky form. (1) It replaces the power \(\alpha\), which is numerically unstable, with a logistic, which is still concave in gains and convex in losses; a \(\beta\) controls saturation. (2) It splits loss aversion into two weights, \(\lambda_D\) for desirable examples and \(\lambda_U\) for undesirable ones. (3) It uses the policy's KL as the reference point: the human judges \(y\) against all possible outputs, so \(Q=\pi_\theta\) and \(z_0=\mathrm{KL}\big(\pi_\theta(y'\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y'\mid x)\big)\). The result is Eq. 8:

\[ \mathcal L_{\text{KTO}}=\mathbb E_{x,y\sim\mathcal D}\big[\lambda_y-v(x,y)\big],\qquad v(x,y)=\begin{cases}\lambda_D\,\sigma\big(\beta\,(r_\theta(x,y)-z_0)\big) & \text{if } y \text{ is desirable}\\[2pt] \lambda_U\,\sigma\big(\beta\,(z_0-r_\theta(x,y))\big) & \text{if } y \text{ is undesirable}\end{cases} \]
  • \(r_\theta(x,y)=\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\): our \(h\), without \(\beta\)
  • \(z_0\): the reference point, an estimate of the KL; no gradient
  • \(\lambda_D, \lambda_U\): weights for desirable and undesirable examples
  • \(\lambda_y\): \(\lambda_D\) or \(\lambda_U\) depending on the label; it only makes the loss non-negative
  • \(\beta\): saturation of the logistic (an effect similar to DPO's \(\beta\))

Notice: there are no pairs at all. Each example is a binary label, good or bad, and the “contrast” is against \(z_0\), not against another response. The paper's intuition for the reference point is elegant. If the model tries to raise the reward of a desirable example by brute force, raising the probability of everything, the KL rises too, the reference point rises with it and nothing is gained. The model is forced to learn what makes that output good. Since \(z_0\) receives no gradient, it only controls where the logistic saturates.

The amber window is Proposition 4.1 in chart form. For a desirable example, the gradient is \(-\lambda_D\,\beta\,\sigma(\cdot)\,(1-\sigma(\cdot))\,\nabla_\theta r_\theta\) (derivation from the research note). If an example's adjusted reward goes to \(\pm\infty\), its contribution to the gradient tends to zero. Examples that are too easy, or too hard (perhaps mislabeled), are ignored. That buys robustness to noise and, as a price, a risk of underfitting on complex distributions, which the paper suggests mitigating with a smaller \(\beta\) and more epochs. Compare with DPO, whose weight \(\sigma(-\beta\Delta h)\) closes on one side only: an extremely “inverted” pair keeps pulling at full strength.

KTO in practice: z₀, thumbs up/down and the balance

Estimating z₀ without sampling

The reference point is an expected KL, which would require sampling responses from the policy at every step, and that is slow. The paper's trick is to use mismatched pairs within the microbatch. In a microbatch of \(m\) examples \((x_i, y_i)\), pair each prompt with the response of the next example, \(\{(x_1,y_2),(x_2,y_3),\dots,(x_m,y_1)\}\):

\[ \hat z_0=\max\Big(0,\ \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_j\mid x_i)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_j\mid x_i)}\Big),\qquad j=(i \bmod m)+1 \]
  • \(y_j\): the response of another example in the microbatch, evaluated on prompt \(x_i\)
  • \(\max(0,\cdot)\): the clamp, because a KL can't be negative
  • cost: one extra forward (policy and reference) on the mismatched pairs

Why responses from other prompts? Because the \(y_i\) themselves were chosen precisely for being canonically good or bad, and their rewards have magnitudes that are not at all representative of the policy. The estimator is biased: the clamp at zero adds upward bias and reduces variance. There is one case where you can skip the estimate and use \(\hat z_0=0\): if KTO comes after an SFT on the same desirable data and the SFT model is the reference, the estimated KL quickly tends to ~0. Without SFT, or with SFT on other data, you have to estimate it.

In TRL (KTOTrainer, stable since v1.8.0), the KL is the mean, aggregated across devices, of \(\log\pi_\theta-\log\pi_{\text{ref}}\) on the mismatched completions, with .clamp(min=0) and no gradient. The mismatched completions are built by shifting the batch by +1, which requires train_sampling_strategy="sequential" (the KTOConfig default) and a per-device batch greater than 1: with 1, the “KL” becomes the example's own reward. The documentation recommends a per-step batch of at least 4.

Binary data and imbalance

KTO only needs one desirable/undesirable label per example. Preference pairs become binary by assuming \(y_w\) is desirable and \(y_l\) undesirable, an assumption the paper itself calls naive; the one-y-per-x variant uses only one of the responses from each pair, eliminating any pairing. The real gain shows up with production data, which usually has far more “bad” than “good”, or the other way around. For this the paper gives a rule of thumb (Eq. 9), with \(n_D\) desirable and \(n_U\) undesirable examples:

\[ \frac{\lambda_D\,n_D}{\lambda_U\,n_U}\in\Big[1,\ \frac{3}{2}\Big] \]
  • the paper's example, ratio 1:10 (desirable:undesirable): \(\lambda_U=1\) and \(\lambda_D\in[10,\ 15]\)
  • default: \(\lambda_D=\lambda_U=1\); the TRL documentation suggests the range \([1,\ 4/3]\)
  • exception: when avoiding the worst matters more (toxicity), \(\lambda_Dn_D<\lambda_Un_U\) may be better

The previous figure's “balance” mode lets you set up this adjustment yourself. The rule implies that, once the imbalance is corrected, sensitivity to gains pays off more than sensitivity to losses on most benchmarks, a curious result for a method named in honor of loss aversion.

What the paper reports

KTO handles extreme imbalance, matching DPO with up to 90% fewer desirable examples. With good enough pretraining, you can skip SFT and go straight to KTO; with DPO, SFT is always needed for the best result. On Zephyr-β-SFT trained on UltraFeedback (Table 2), KTO (\(\beta=0.1\), \(\lambda_D=1\)) scored 53.5 on GSM8k, versus 40.0 for DPO and 39.0 for SFT; the one-y-per-x variant scored 50.0. The ablations tell the method's story: without \(z_0\), 49.5; with a concave value \(v=\log\sigma\), 42.5; with a risk-neutral value (\(v(\cdot)=\cdot\)), 42.0 on GSM8k, and BBH collapses to 6.1. The saturation is not decoration.

Two theoretical results complete the picture. Theorem 4.2 shows that maximizing preference likelihood does not identify the “prospect” utility: there are rewards in the same equivalence class, with the same optimal policy, and different value distributions. And Theorem 4.3 deals with contradictory preferences: if \(y_a\succ y_b\) in a proportion \(p\in(0.5,\ 1)\) and the opposite in the rest, and if \(p^{1/\beta}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_a\mid x)<(1-p)^{1/\beta}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_b\mid x)\), DPO's optimal policy gives more probability to the minority response \(y_b\), while KTO's update with \(\lambda_D=\lambda_U\) increases that of the majority one. KTO has a better worst case under noise and intransitivity. The paper's recommendation (§4.5): binary feedback, especially imbalanced, calls for KTO; clean, transitive preferences favor DPO, because KTO can underfit; lots of noise or intransitivity favors KTO, and public datasets like SHP, OASST and even the synthetic UltraFeedback usually have this noise.

Hyperparameters

The learning rate is the most sensitive hyperparameter. KTO's optimum is usually 2× to 10× DPO's, because the adjusted reward is smaller in magnitude, and the default recommended in v5 is 5e-6 with AdamW, with or without prior SFT, with an already instruction-tuned model and with LoRA. The diagnostic is useful: if the implicit rewards of desirable and undesirable examples drop together, the LR is too aggressive; ideally only the undesirable ones drop. (The paper's comparison experiments used DPO's LR with RMSProp, to be fair; the practical recommendation is different.) The microbatch needs at least 2 examples to estimate \(z_0\); the experiments use an effective batch of 32 and the recommendation ranges from 8 to 128. For \(\beta\): 0.1 is the default; \([0.01,\ 0.10)\) if the model has already been tuned on the same data; \((0.10,\ 0.50]\) if the reference is already very good, such as an instruct model.

SimPO: no reference, with a mean and a margin

SimPO, by Meng, Xia and Chen (Princeton; NeurIPS 2024), starts from two complaints about DPO's implicit reward. The first is practical: it requires \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) throughout training, which costs memory and compute. The second is conceptual: it does not match the metric that guides generation. In decoding, what counts is (approximately) the mean log-likelihood of the text under the policy, with no reference at all. The authors measured that, after DPO, only ~50% of training triples give the preferred response a higher mean log-prob than the rejected one. The model “gets” the pair right by the implicit reward and gets it wrong by the generation metric. The solution is to use the generation metric itself as the reward:

\[ r_{\text{SimPO}}(x,y)=\frac{\beta}{|y|}\log\pi_\theta(y\mid x)=\frac{\beta}{|y|}\sum_{i=1}^{|y|}\log\pi_\theta(y_i\mid x,y_{<i}) \]
  • \(\frac{1}{|y|}\log\pi_\theta\): mean per-token log-prob (length normalization)
  • \(\beta\): reward scale (much larger than in DPO, because the mean is ~\(|y|\) times smaller than the sum)
  • there is no \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) anywhere

Why the mean? The sum punishes long responses, because each token adds a negative term. If the preferred response is longer, optimizing the sum forces the model to inflate the probability of long sequences, which raises the risk of degeneration. The ablation without normalization (“w/o LN”, Table 5) shows the effect: the Spearman correlation between mean log-prob and length goes from 0.34 (SimPO) to 0.82, and AlpacaEval 2 LC on Mistral-Base drops from 21.5 to 11.9.

The margin γ: home-field advantage

The second ingredient is a target margin. Bradley–Terry gains a “home-field advantage” \(\gamma>0\): \(p(y_w\succ y_l\mid x)=\sigma\big(r(x,y_w)-r(x,y_l)-\gamma\big)\). It isn't enough for the preferred response to be better; it must be better by at least \(\gamma\). The loss is (SimPO, Eq. 6):

\[ \mathcal L_{\text{SimPO}}=-\mathbb E_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim\mathcal D}\Big[\log\sigma\Big(\frac{\beta}{|y_w|}\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)-\frac{\beta}{|y_l|}\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)-\gamma\Big)\Big] \]
  • \(\gamma\): target margin, in reward units; usually given as the ratio \(\gamma/\beta\)
  • \(|y_w|, |y_l|\): lengths in tokens
  • with \(\gamma=0\) and without the division by \(|y|\), it becomes CPO's preference loss

Quality improves with \(\gamma\) up to a point and then gets worse; zeroing \(\gamma\) cost 4.7 LC points on Mistral-Base (16.8 versus 21.5). IPO also has a “target margin” (\(1/(2\tau)\)), but its full objective did worse in this study. The gradient (Appendix F) shows the two mechanical differences from DPO:

\[ \nabla_\theta\mathcal L_{\text{SimPO}}=-\beta\,\mathbb E\Big[s_\theta\Big(\frac{1}{|y_w|}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)-\frac{1}{|y_l|}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_l\mid x)\Big)\Big],\qquad s_\theta=\sigma\Big(\frac{\beta}{|y_l|}\log\pi_\theta(y_l)-\frac{\beta}{|y_w|}\log\pi_\theta(y_w)+\gamma\Big) \]
  • \(s_\theta\): the pair's weight, high when the policy gives \(y_l\) more mean log-prob than \(y_w\) (plus the margin); it doesn't involve the reference
  • \(1/|y|\): each response's gradient is divided by its length; in DPO, long sequences get larger gradients and “dominate” training

In the figure, with \(\beta=2\) and \(\gamma=1\), the sum gives \(u=2\cdot(-25+17)-1=-17\) and a loss ≈ 17: the sum “thinks” the preferred response is much worse, and fixing that would require inflating the probability of the long sequence. The mean gives \(r_w=-2.5\), \(r_l=-3.4\), \(u=-0.1\), loss 0.7444 and weight 0.525: the pair is almost right, it just needs to open the margin. More points from the research note's table: with \(\beta=2.5\) and \(\gamma=0.3\), \(u=+0.825\) and loss 0.3634; with \(\beta=10\) and \(\gamma=5\), \(u=-0.5\) and loss 0.9741.

DPO already had a margin, just a variable one

SimPO's Appendix I rewrites DPO in a revealing way. Expanding the log-ratio:

\[ \mathcal L_{\text{DPO}}=-\log\sigma\big(\beta\log\pi_\theta(y_w)-\beta\log\pi_\theta(y_l)-\gamma_{\text{ref}}\big),\qquad \gamma_{\text{ref}}=\beta\log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w)-\beta\log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l) \]
  • \(\gamma_{\text{ref}}\): a per-pair margin, set by the reference
  • on our pair, \(\gamma_{\text{ref}}=0.1\cdot(-26+15)=-1.1\) (our calculation): a negative margin, which “forgives” the preferred response for being long

In other words, DPO “is” a reference-free loss with a margin that varies from pair to pair. SimPO replaces that margin with the constant \(\gamma\), and the sum with the mean. In SimPO's Table 15, applying only normalization to DPO helps on Mistral-Base but not on Mistral-Instruct, and applying only \(\gamma\) to DPO doesn't help. There is also the middle ground, Tülu 3's length-normalized DPO, which keeps the reference and divides each log-ratio by the length: \(-\log\sigma\big(\frac{\beta}{|y_w|}h_w-\frac{\beta}{|y_l|}h_l\big)\). It was the best in Tülu 3, with \(\beta=5\), compared with SimPO and DPO. On our pair, with \(\beta=5\), the logit is \(5\cdot0.25=1.25\) and the loss 0.2519.

No KL, so handle with care

SimPO has no explicit KL. The authors attribute its stability to a small LR, diverse preference data and the LLM's intrinsic robustness, and they measure a low empirical KL (Fig. 5a). But there is a documented warning (Appendix J, Table 16): continuing to train Llama-3-8B-Instruct with LR 1e-6 gave AlpacaEval 2 LC 53.7, with GSM falling from 78.5 to 57.4 and MMLU from 61.7 to 54.9, a case of catastrophic forgetting. With an LR of 4e-7 to 5e-7, the chat gains were smaller and GSM and MMLU were preserved. On Gemma-2-9B-it the LR barely mattered: in the released checkpoint (LR 8e-7), GSM went from 87.4 to 88.0 and MMLU from 72.7 to 72.2, with AlpacaEval 2 LC 72.4 and Arena-Hard 59.1. The repository README also notes that SimPO v0.2 (Llama-3) “struggles” with structured outputs such as JSON, and attributes this to forgetting combined with the high LR. In return, the efficiency gain is real: on Llama-3-Base 8B on 8×H100 (Fig. 5c), DPO took 73 minutes with a 77 GB peak and SimPO, 60 minutes with 69 GB.

SimPO in TRL: mind the name

The “real” SimPO is the CPOTrainer from trl.experimental.cpo with loss_type="simpo" and cpo_alpha=0.0 (zeroing CPO's NLL term). The default simpo_gamma is 0.5. The DPOTrainer has a loss_type "sigmoid_norm", which the documentation associates with SimPO, but which keeps the reference: in practice it is length-normalized DPO, Tülu 3 style, not SimPO.

The rest of the family: ORPO, CPO, R-DPO, β-DPO and DPOP

With the “score + contrast + anchor” mold in hand, the other variants become easy to read: each one is DPO with one piece swapped. The map below lays out the family tree; click a method (or pick it from the menu) to see its loss, and filter by the type of swap.

ORPO: preference and SFT in a single stage

ORPO (Odds Ratio Preference Optimization; Hong, Lee and Thorne, 2024) does SFT and preference in a single step and without a reference. It scores each response by the per-token mean, \(\log P_\theta(y\mid x)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_t\log P_\theta(y_t\mid x,y_{<t})\), so that \(P_\theta\) is the geometric mean of the token probabilities, and compares the odds of the two responses (Eq. 3 to 7):

\[ \mathcal L_{\text{ORPO}}=\mathbb E\big[\mathcal L_{\text{SFT}}+\lambda\,\mathcal L_{\text{OR}}\big],\qquad \mathcal L_{\text{OR}}=-\log\sigma\Big(\log\frac{\text{odds}_\theta(y_w\mid x)}{\text{odds}_\theta(y_l\mid x)}\Big),\qquad \text{odds}_\theta(y\mid x)=\frac{P_\theta(y\mid x)}{1-P_\theta(y\mid x)} \]
  • \(\mathcal L_{\text{SFT}}\): the (per-token) NLL of the preferred response, i.e. SFT itself
  • \(\lambda\): weight of the preference term (default 0.1; ablation 0.1 / 0.5 / 1.0)
  • \(P_\theta\): geometric mean of the token probabilities

The gradient of the odds term is \(\delta(d)\,h(d)\) (Eq. 8 to 10), with \(\delta(d)=\big[1+\frac{\text{odds}(y_w)}{\text{odds}(y_l)}\big]^{-1}\), a penalty that vanishes when the pair is already right, and \(h(d)=\frac{\nabla\log P_\theta(y_w)}{1-P_\theta(y_w)}-\frac{\nabla\log P_\theta(y_l)}{1-P_\theta(y_l)}\). The factor \(1/(1-P)\) is ~1 when \(P\) is small and grows as \(P\) approaches 1 (our reading of Eq. 10; the paper's text says the factor amplifies the gradient when \(P\) is low, but the math shows the opposite: \(1/(1-P)\approx1\) for small \(P\) and it blows up as \(P\to1\)): a rejected response that the model already finds very likely gets a stronger push down. Why odds and not the probability ratio? According to the authors (§7.1), the log probability ratio leads to a more extreme discrimination against the rejected response; in joint SFT, a softer contrast preserves domain adaptation. With \(\lambda=0.1\), the log-probs of chosen and rejected responses stay close and those of the rejected ones don't even drop; \(\lambda=1.0\) hurt extraction, math and reasoning on MT-Bench and improved the open-ended categories. Without a reference, ORPO does half the forwards of DPO. The reported training uses a peak LR of 8e-6 for 10 epochs (OPT, Phi-2, Llama-2), picking the checkpoint with the lowest validation loss. In TRL (trl.experimental.orpo), beta is the paper's \(\lambda\). On our pair: \(P_w=e^{-1.25}=0.2865\), \(P_l=e^{-1.70}=0.1827\), odds 0.4016 and 0.2235, log of the ratio 0.5859, \(\mathcal L_{\text{OR}}=0.4425\), \(\mathcal L_{\text{SFT}}=1.25\) and a total of 1.2943.

CPO: the uniform reference

CPO (Contrastive Preference Optimization; Xu et al., 2024) was born in machine translation (ALMA-R), where human “gold” translations are sometimes worse than those of strong models, and plain SFT would copy that ceiling. The approximation is to take \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) as uniform: the reference terms cancel out and what remains is \(\mathcal L(\pi_\theta;U)=-\mathbb E\big[\log\sigma\big(\beta\log\pi_\theta(y_w)-\beta\log\pi_\theta(y_l)\big)\big]\) (Eq. 3). Theorem 1 shows that, if the reference were the “ideal” policy of the preferred responses, DPO's loss (plus a constant) would be upper-bounded by this uniform version. A behavior cloning constraint, \(\mathbb E[\mathrm{KL}(\pi_w\|\pi_\theta)]<\epsilon\), becomes, through the Lagrangian (with multiplier 1), an NLL term on the preferred responses: \(\mathcal L_{\text{CPO}}=\mathcal L(\pi_\theta,U)-\mathbb E[\log\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)]\). Watch out for length: without normalization, the preference uses summed log-probs. On our pair, \(\mathcal L_{\text{prefer}}=-\log\sigma(0.1\cdot(-25+17))=1.1711\): CPO sees the pair as “wrong” and pushes hard. In the SimPO paper (Table 10), CPO generates responses ~50% longer than SimPO on Arena-Hard, which favors CPO on that benchmark, since Arena-Hard does not penalize length. In TRL: CPOTrainer, cpo_alpha=1.0 (BC weight), beta=0.1.

R-DPO: charging for length

Park, Rafailov, Ermon and Finn (2024) measured the size of the problem: on the HH and TL;DR datasets there is a small preference bias toward long responses, and unregularized DPO produces responses ~2× longer, outside the dataset's distribution. On HH with \(\beta=0.1\), this already happens in the first 10% of the epoch. The fix, R-DPO, puts length into the RL objective: \(\max\ \mathbb E[r(x,y)-\alpha|y|]-\beta\,\mathbb D_{\text{KL}}\) (Eq. 6). The optimum is \(\pi^*\propto\pi_{\text{ref}}\,e^{(r-\alpha|y|)/\beta}\), so \(r=\beta\log\frac{\pi^*}{\pi_{\text{ref}}}+\beta\log Z+\alpha|y|\), and the same DPO trick gives (Eq. 9):

\[ \mathcal L_{\text{R-DPO}}=-\mathbb E\Big[\log\sigma\Big(\beta\,\Delta h+\alpha\,\big(|y_w|-|y_l|\big)\Big)\Big] \]
  • \(\alpha\): price per token of length (0.01 on HH and 0.05 on TL;DR in the paper's Fig. 2)
  • when \(y_w\) is longer, the positive term already “explains” part of the preference and the implicit margin needs to be smaller (interpretation)

The paper reports up to 20% improvement in win rate when controlling for length; the sweeps use \(\alpha\in\{0,\ 0.005,\ 0.01\}\) on HH and \(\{0,\ 0.2,\ 0.5\}\) on TL;DR, with \(\beta\in\{0.05,\ 0.1,\ 0.5\}\), and the scales depend on \(\beta\) and on the typical length. On our pair, with \(\beta=0.1\) and \(\alpha=0.01\): logit \(0.3+0.01\cdot10=0.4\) and loss 0.5130. An important caveat comes from the DAA over-optimization analysis: correcting for length shifts the KL–win rate frontier, but does not eliminate over-optimization.

β-DPO: a β that adapts to the batch

β-DPO (Wu et al., NeurIPS 2024) measures each pair's discrepancy, \(M_i=r(y_w^{(i)})-r(y_l^{(i)})\), with the implicit reward computed using a \(\beta_0\). The per-instance version, \(\beta_i=[1+\alpha(M_i-M_0)]\beta_0\) (Eq. 5), can destabilize training; the one actually used is per batch, \(\beta_{\text{batch}}=\big[1+\alpha\big(\mathbb E_{i\in\text{batch}}[M_i]-M_0\big)\big]\beta_0\) (Eq. 6), with \(M_0\) updated by a moving average (Eq. 7). There is also a filter that samples \(|\text{batch}|\times\rho\) examples with a Gaussian weight around \(M_0\) (Eq. 8 and 9; \(\rho=0.8\) by default). The principle: “easy” pairs, with a large gap, call for a larger \(\beta\) (caution against noise); close, high-quality pairs call for a smaller \(\beta\) (an assertive update). Values: \(\beta_0=0.1\), optimal \(\alpha\) typically between 0.6 and 1.0. With \(\alpha=0.6\) and \(M_0=0.2\), a batch with \(\bar M=0\) gives \(\beta=0.088\) and one with \(\bar M=1\) gives 0.148.

DPOP: no knocking down the preferred response

Remember the likelihood displacement from the previous chapter: since DPO's loss only sees the margin, \(\log\pi_\theta(y_w)\) can fall together with \(\log\pi_\theta(y_l)\), as long as \(y_l\) falls more, and the mass moves to other responses. Razin et al. (ICLR 2025) show an alarming case: in an alignment run meant to increase refusals on unsafe prompts, Llama-3-8B-Instruct's refusal rate on SORRY-Bench dropped from 74.4% to 33.4%. DPOP (DPO-Positive; Pal et al., 2024, the Smaug paper) attacks this directly:

\[ \mathcal L_{\text{DPOP}}=-\mathbb E\Big[\log\sigma\Big(\beta\Big(\Delta h-\lambda\max\Big(0,\ \log\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w\mid x)}{\pi_\theta(y_w\mid x)}\Big)\Big)\Big)\Big] \]
  • the penalty is zero while \(\pi_\theta(y_w)\ge\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w)\) and grows when the preferred response drops below the reference
  • the paper's values: \(\beta=0.3\), \(\lambda=50\) (ablation 5, 50, 500), LR 5e-5, AdamW, 1,000 steps

On MetaMath, with low edit-distance pairs, DPO and SLiC “fail catastrophically”, IPO does not improve and only DPOP improves. On our pair, if \(\log\pi_\theta(y_w)\) drops to −27 (\(h_w=-1\)), DPO with \(\beta=0.3\) only sees a smaller margin (logit \(0.3\cdot(-1+2)=0.3\), loss 0.5544), with no way to tell this case apart from a rejected response that went up; DPOP's penalty, on the other hand, is 1, and its logit goes to \(0.3\cdot(1-50)=-14.7\): loss 14.7. DPOP “screams” when the preferred response drops.

The same pair, seven losses

Time to put everything on the same workbench. The calculator below evaluates seven losses on the toy pair, with the reference fixed (−26 and −15, lengths 20 and 10) and the policy free: you control \(\log\pi_\theta(y_w)\) and \(\log\pi_\theta(y_l)\). For each method it shows the loss and the push that gradient descent gives each log-prob, \(-\partial\mathcal L/\partial\log\pi_\theta(y)\): positive raises the response, negative lowers it. On the plane, each point is a policy state; the arrows give the direction of each method's push, and the particle follows the flow of the chosen method.

In the “after a few steps” state (−25 and −17), the table reproduces the research note: DPO 0.5544; IPO 4.0 (the pair has already passed the target 1, and IPO pulls \(y_w\) down and \(y_l\) up); KTO 0.4627; SimPO 0.7444; ORPO 1.2943; R-DPO 0.5130; CPO 1.1711. A few contrasts, which hold for any \(\log\pi\) you choose:

  • DPO, R-DPO and CPO push along the diagonal (\(+1, -1\)): only the difference matters. R-DPO merely shifts the point where the weight saturates.
  • SimPO pushes the short response harder. The push is \(\propto(1/|y_w|,\ -1/|y_l|)\), so \(y_l\), with 10 tokens, gets twice the push of \(y_w\), with 20. The \(u=0\) boundary has slope \(|y_l|/|y_w|=1/2\) on the plane, versus 1 for DPO's lines.
  • IPO has a finish line. The particle stops at the target \(\Delta h=1/(2\tau)\); DPO keeps walking along the diagonal forever.
  • KTO treats each response on its own, with windows that close at the ends, and ORPO pulls \(y_w\) up even when the pair is already ordered, because of the NLL.

Try the “displacement” preset: both responses drop (−28 and −21). The margin grows (\(\Delta h=4\)) and DPO's loss decreases to 0.5130, even with the preferred response 2 nats below the reference. It is likelihood displacement in miniature. SimPO, having no reference, has no way of even knowing that something dropped; ORPO keeps pulling \(y_w\) up through the NLL. Putting the tables together gives the research note's message: when the preferred response is longer, methods without a reference and without normalization (CPO, SLiC) see a “wrong” pair and push hard; DPO discounts length through the reference; SimPO and normalized DPO remove it through the mean; R-DPO discounts it explicitly.

A decision guide, our own interpretation of the recommendations in the papers themselves:

  1. Have pairs and an SFT? Start with DPO. If the labels are deterministic or few, or if over-optimization shows up, go with IPO. If verbosity bothers you, R-DPO or length-normalized DPO.
  2. Have “good/bad” labels or a strong imbalance? KTO, with \(\lambda_D, \lambda_U\) tuned by Eq. 9.
  3. Short on memory, no room for a reference? SimPO, with a conservative LR, or ORPO, if you haven't done SFT yet.
  4. Is the preferred response's likelihood dropping and behavior getting worse? Filter pairs (the CHES score from Razin et al.), use DPOP or add an NLL on the preferred responses.
  5. Can you generate and judge during training? Go online/iterative: it is the biggest structural leap (in OAIF, the online version beat the offline one in ~66% of human evaluations, averaged over DPO, IPO and SLiC).

The memory math explains the popularity of reference-free methods. For an 8B model, full fine-tuning needs ~128 GB just for states, the entire unified memory of a DGX Spark (128 GB), before activations; the reference adds ~16 GB. With LoRA, the base in BF16 takes ~16 GB and serves as both policy and reference, and SimPO's advantage shrinks to compute (2 forwards instead of 4). The full math, with activations, gradient checkpointing and ZeRO, is in chapter 51.

Configuring in TRL and MLX

In TRL (1.14 on PyPI; the main read during research is 1.15.0.dev0), the three main variants change only a few lines compared with a DPO script:

# IPO: the same DPOTrainer, a different loss. beta = τ, and the log-ratio is a per-token MEAN.
args = DPOConfig(loss_type=["ipo"], beta=0.5)        # sweep τ; don't copy τ from another implementation

# KTO: dataset {prompt, completion, label}; pairs become chosen→True, rejected→False
from trl import KTOConfig, KTOTrainer
args = KTOConfig(beta=0.1, desirable_weight=1.0, undesirable_weight=1.0,   # λ_D, λ_U (Eq. 9)
                 per_device_train_batch_size=4,      # > 1: the KL uses completions from OTHER examples
                 gradient_accumulation_steps=4,
                 learning_rate=5e-6,                 # the paper's recommendation; TRL's default is 1e-6
                 max_length=2048)                    # train_sampling_strategy="sequential" is the default

# Real SimPO: CPOTrainer without the NLL term
from trl.experimental.cpo import CPOConfig, CPOTrainer
args = CPOConfig(loss_type="simpo", cpo_alpha=0.0, beta=2.5, simpo_gamma=1.375,
                 learning_rate=1e-6, num_train_epochs=1)   # high LR = risk of forgetting

On the Mac, plain mlx-lm doesn't do preference optimization. mlx-lm-lora 3.1.3 has --train-mode dpo, cpo and orpo, among others, and --dpo-cpo-loss-type \(\in\) {sigmoid, hinge, ipo, dpop}: IPO and DPOP are there, but there is no KTO or SimPO. For binary data on the Mac, one way out is to convert the labels into pairs (best positive × worst negative per prompt) and use DPO. Chapter 63 builds these recipes end to end.

What to monitor. For the methods with a reference, TRL's metrics are the implicit rewards: rewards/chosen, rewards/rejected, rewards/margins and rewards/accuracies, plus logps/chosen (if it keeps falling, that's displacement). In KTO, track the estimated kl (it should stay positive and stable) and check that only the undesirable rewards drop. In all of them: the mean generation length and the win rate per checkpoint. In the DAA analysis, the best point showed up at only ~25% of an epoch when the KL budget is loose; looking only at the end of training hides the over-optimization hump.

Bridge to Module 1

Serving doesn't change. IPO, KTO and SimPO produce weights with the same architecture as the starting model; if training used LoRA, the adapter can be merged before quantization, and the vLLM serving recipe stays the same (chapter 46). Online needs fast inference. TRL's OnlineDPOTrainer (trl.experimental.online_dpo) accepts loss_type sigmoid or ipo and generates responses with use_vllm, in colocate or server mode: everything Module 1 taught about the KV cache and continuous batching turns into training speed. Aligning is not editing. The GLM abliteration (chapter 19) removes a refusal direction without any training; likelihood displacement shows the reverse path, a DPO designed to increase refusals that cut them from 74.4% to 33.4%. One possible reading (our interpretation): alignment behaviors live in directions of the residual stream, and preference optimization moves them, not always on purpose.

All the methods in this chapter start from fixed pairs or labels and a supervised loss. The next chapter opens up the other side of the family: the path of regression (AWR, ReST, RAFT: an SFT on samples weighted by the reward, with weight \(\exp(A/\beta)\) in AWR, or filtered by it, with a threshold in ReST and best-of-\(K\) in RAFT, the limiting case of this weighting; the exponential weight leads to the same “tilted” optimum as DPO) and the path of contrast (InfoNCE, SLiC, CPL), where you will discover that DPO is exactly an InfoNCE with a single negative.
