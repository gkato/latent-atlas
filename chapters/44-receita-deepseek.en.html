Someone hands you two DGX Sparks, a QSFP cable and a name: DeepSeek-V4-Flash-Vision-Exp: ~157 GiB of weights (experts in MXFP4, the rest in FP8), 43 layers, 256 experts, compressed KV, a built-in DSpark drafter and a vision tower. The goal: serve it on vLLM with a 1-million-token ceiling per request, six simultaneous conversations and reliable tool calling. This chapter rebuilds Mia's recipe in the order you would build it (budget, network, image, hotfixes, flags, verification) and stitches each piece to its concept. At the end, a case study follows its successor, DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash, through five deployments, from two Sparks to DeepSeek's own production engine.

Three lanes before the first command

The word lane has two meanings in the repository, and mixing them up is the main source of settings that “do nothing”:

  • Runtime lane: which vLLM code actually runs in the container. There are three: A · Anemll (default), B · Stage-C overlay (historical) and C · official-main (experiment).
  • Service profile: the shape of the service (context × slots × nodes). There are three: 1M/6 on TP=2 (default), TP=3 with 16 slots and Stage-C 200K/16.

On top of that runs the timeline of checkpoints (DSpark preview → 0731 → Vision-Exp), and each swap changed a number in the recipe, such as the speculation k (5 → 6). In the figure, each lane is a subway line: pick one and follow the train all the way to vllm serve.

Notice the asymmetry: in lane A, the production one, DSpark comes ready-made inside the image and the repository only touches it through hotfixes; in lane B, the repository holds the entire code, which is why it is the readable reference implementation, even though it is not the one you will bring up (overlay vs hotfix: Recipe engineering; model runners: vLLM architecture).

Classic trap

VLLM_DSPARK_*, VLLM_DSV4_*, VLLM_USE_B12X_WO_PROJECTION and DSPARK_SLOT_CLAMP are only registered by the overlay's envs.py (lane B). In the Anemll image they only produce Unknown vLLM environment variable and do nothing. Putting VLLM_DSPARK_GPU_REJECTED_CONTEXT_MASK=1 in lane A's .env and “measuring the effect” is measuring noise.

Step 0 — The budget: bytes that fit, bytes that move

Every recipe starts with two back-of-the-envelope calculations, capacity and bandwidth (Journey map), and for DeepSeek they decide almost everything.

Capacity: where each GiB lives

Each Spark exposes ~121.7 GiB of MemTotal, shared between CPU and GPU (Unified memory). On TP=2, each rank loads 79.17 GiB of weights. The checkpoint (~157 GiB) does not fit in a 121.7 GiB node, so TP=2 is the minimum. vLLM gets the --gpu-memory-utilization fraction of that memory. Out of it come the weights and the peak of activations, graphs and workspaces measured in the profile run (PagedAttention & KV), and the rest becomes the KV cache pool:

\[ \text{KV}(u) \;\approx\; u\,M \;-\; W \;-\; A \qquad\qquad N_{\text{tok}}(u) \;\approx\; N_0\,\frac{\text{KV}(u)}{\text{KV}_0} \]
  • \(u\): --gpu-memory-utilization (default 0.835)
  • \(M\): node memory (~121.7 GiB)
  • \(W\): weights per rank (79.17 GiB on TP=2)
  • \(A\): activations, graphs and workspaces (≈5.4 GiB, derived from the reference boot)
  • \(\text{KV}_0, N_0\): measured point, 17.04 GiB and 2,331,430 tokens at \(u=0.835\)

Hence the recipe's rule of thumb: each +0.01 of utilization ≈ +1.2 GiB of KV ≈ +165K tokens. The catch is that, in unified memory, every GiB vLLM takes comes out of the operating system. Of the ~20 GiB outside vLLM, the OS, the page cache, the IPC shared memory, the CUDA/NCCL contexts and the workspaces allocated outside the budget (prefill chunk, autotune) eat almost everything: at 0.83, the host's MemAvailable sits at 3.8–5.2 GB after boot (without root, the repository did not attribute each share). Play with the balance.

Notice two things. First, --max-model-len 1048576 is a per-request ceiling, not a reservation. Think of a restaurant: the ceiling sets the maximum size of a single table, and the pool is the dining room's capacity. Six parties of 200K (1.2 M) get seated, six of 1M (6 M) never do, and the overflow waits at the door. The boot's Maximum concurrency … 2.22x only says that about two 1M tables fit together. Second, raising u buys little extra pool and charges host RAM for it: slim down the host first (no graphical session, earlyoom disabled).

Deep dive: why a 1M context fits

DeepSeek-V4 stores one 512-dim latent vector per token (MLA with K = V, MLA & compact KV), compresses blocks of 4 or 128 tokens in alternating layers (C4A/C128A, Sparse attention) and writes each entry in 584 B with NoPE in FP8 (Quantized KV cache). At long context this comes to ≈3.85 KB/token plus ~3.4 MB of fixed SWA windows per sequence, replicated on both ranks.

Bandwidth: the cost of a decode step

In decode at c=1, each step rereads the active weights: the routed experts touched (≤42 distinct out of 256 per layer), FP8 attention, drafter, bf16 lm_head and the Markov head. The recipe's cost model adds up to ≈16.5–17 GB per rank per step, or 68–74 ms at 230–250 GB/s effective: “c=1 decode on TP=2 is ~80% weight streaming”. Hence the recipe's three levers: more tokens per weight read (DSpark), more sequences per read (6 slots) and less CPU overhead between kernels (regular CUDA graphs). Section 8 turns this calculation into a tok/s prediction.

Steps 1–2 — Two nodes, one network and a pinned image

The topology is as simple as it gets: one tensor parallel rank per node (Parallelism). The head (rank 0) runs the API on :8888, the EngineCore with the scheduler and the TCPStore on MASTER_ADDR:25000; the worker (rank 1) runs headless, GPU and NCCL only. Between the two, a QSFP cable plugged straight into the ConnectX-7s, with RoCE v2 (NCCL & RDMA). The figure shows the traffic of one decode step (43 all-reduces of wo_b + 43 of MoE + drafter, embeddings, logits and Markov ≈ 108 collectives) and each rank's slice of the 8 groups × 8 heads.

Notice that the collectives are many and small. In the α-β model they are dominated by latency, which is why doubling the bandwidth barely changes decode: with MTU 9000 and two HCAs, end-to-end throughput went from 1,311 to 1,410 tok/s (+7.6%), and decode did not change materially. Two network details are mandatory (GID and SSH) and one is recommended (the two PFs):

  1. RoCEv2 GID resolved, not pinned. The GID index changes per node, per HCA and after a reboot. With NCCL_IB_GID_AUTO=1, the launcher validates in sysfs that each HCA has a RoCE v2 GID matching the interface's IPv4, fails closed if it doesn't, and leaves NCCL_IB_GID_INDEX undefined (if empty, it gets unset: NCCL would read empty as index 0).
  2. Passwordless SSH head → worker and the same image on both nodes: everything is driven from the head.
  3. Recommended: both PCIe functions of the port. On the GB10, the QSFP's 200 Gb/s shows up as two virtual NICs (PCIe Gen5 ×4, ~126 Gb/s each), and a single HCA in NCCL_IB_HCA uses half the port. Use the exact match, =rocep1s0f1,roceP2p1s0f1, and WORKER_NCCL_* on the worker; even so, the single-PF A/B tied on decode (Sep 2).

The pinned image and the container

The Anemll image is referenced by digest, not just by tag. The tag is the label on the box, which anyone can stick on another box; the digest is the numbered seal, which only matches those exact bytes. Since every hotfix is source-exact against vLLM 752a3a504, on a different build the anchors don't match and the boot fails closed; in the worst case, a patch matches different code by chance. Weights: fixed revision, offline mode and optional NFS

prepare-dspark-model-cache.sh --official downloads Vision-Exp at the pinned revision 86f746b3… (DSPARK_REVISION) inside a container of the image itself, writes refs/main and checks every shard in the index (Checkpoints). In service, HF_HUB_OFFLINE=1 is in effect: a missing revision becomes an error, not a silent download of ~157 GiB (≈169 GB). There is no Jinja chat template: messages, tools and reasoning live in encoding/encoding_dsv4.py, which must exist on both nodes. By default the worker keeps a second copy of the ~157 GiB; with DSPARK_WORKER_HF_NFS=1, it mounts the head's cache over read-only NFSv4 and keeps only the JIT caches local.

The launcher, step by step

start-deepseek-v4-flash-dspark.sh has 2,154 lines, and many of them guard against failures documented in the CHANGELOG. Follow the stepper and switch the scenario to see the two deviations it handles on purpose.

The “already running” scenario is a supervision contract (0 ok, 1 failure, 2 invalid config, 3 already running). After a reboot, unless-stopped restores the ranks; if start exited with 1, a systemd unit with retry would spiral into a restart storm. That is why it exits with 3 (SuccessExitStatus=3), and on the worker a remote probe with sentinel 42 tells “rank exists” apart from “SSH failed” (255). The “patch mismatch” scenario shows the preflight: the sensitive opt-ins (and #117, always) run in --check in a throwaway container (docker compose run --rm --no-deps), worker then head, before any rank comes up, and the pair is never left half-patched.

Inside the container: the fail-closed hotfix chain

The Anemll 0.1.1 image predates the Vision-Exp checkpoint and has bugs that show up with 1M contexts, speculation and agents, plus a few generic IPC ones. The repository fixes them inside the container, at boot, before exec vllm serve (restarting compose in the middle of the load brought down the TCPStore, #38). The patchers are mounted read-only from patches/, and the compose command: is a single bash -lc in which every step ends in || exit 1. That is what fail-closed means: if a fix fails, the rank does not come up, because serving without it is worse than not serving.

Analogy: the assembly line with inspectors

The part (the vLLM installation in the container) goes through ~14 stations. At each one, an inspector checks the part against the exact blueprint (SHA-256 or anchor) before tightening the bolt. If the part doesn't match, the inspector doesn't improvise: they stop the whole line. The line is the same on both ranks, in the same order, and only at the end does the part become a product (exec vllm serve).

In the figure, the little vLLM box travels through the stations in the real order of the command:; each little dot is a patch (filled if applied, hollow if its gate is off). Switch the preset and click simulate drift.

Anatomy of a source-exact patch

The conveyor belt is trustworthy because of the pattern every modern patcher follows, source-exact. The most didactic case is hotfix-vllm-dspark-block-k.py, which adds self.method != "dspark" to a rule in vllm/config/speculative.py. It pins the identity of the entire file: stock 3f1abd1c… (56,845 B) → patched 7fffe035… (56,987 B). Follow the six gates and switch the target's state.

Three properties come out of this design. Idempotence: running it twice is the same as running it once, because the second encounter recognizes the patched pin. Atomicity: the post-image is built and compiled in memory and published by os.replace after fsync, so nobody ever reads half a file. Self-verification: --check and --status inspect without writing.

# conceptual skeleton (summary, not the actual repo file)
state = sha256(target)               # 1. inspect (and the vLLM version)
if state == PIN_PATCHED: exit(0)     # 2. idempotent: already applied
if state != PIN_STOCK:  fail()       #    fail-closed: unknown source -> exit 1
new = apply(read(target))            # 3. region 1×, no marker, compile()
assert sha256(new) == PIN_PATCHED    # 4. the post-image is pinned too
publish_atomic(new)                  # 5. tmp + fsync + os.replace
assert sha256(target) == PIN_PATCHED # 6. re-verify

Step 7 — The vllm serve command, flag by flag

At the end of the conveyor belt, the entrypoint runs vllm serve deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V4-Flash-Vision-Exp --revision 86f746b3… with about 40 flags. Some JSON arguments (--speculative-config, --default-chat-template-kwargs, --limit-mm-per-prompt) are assembled inside the container, because values from a .env go through source in bash and would lose their quotes. nvfp4_ds_mla is the most instructive case of “the name lies”. In the first view, each 64-dim block of the latent becomes 64 FP8 bytes plus a 1-byte scale, and the scales of all the tokens sit together at the end of the page; in the second, the 584 B per token become the allocator's contract.

There is no 4-bit KV today: the name brings neither memory savings nor quality risk. In lane B, Stage A taught vLLM to accept the dtype, Stage B tested a 416 B row inherited from GLM-5.2 and Stage C went back to 584 B to keep page sizes equal; a truly FP4 KV (292 B/row) is still only on the drawing board. And the physical ≈3.85 KB/token sits below the boot's ≈7.8 KB/token because the latter adds up all four KV groups.

k=6 looks arbitrary until you look at how DSpark drafts (DSpark). It receives the block [anchor, noise, noise, noise, noise]: the anchor is the last accepted token, and the noise is noise_token_id 128799. With the features from the target's layers 40–42, the block attends non-causally to itself and to the context window and produces 5 drafts in a single forward; a rank-256 Markov head ties each position to the token chosen before it. With 5 trained positions (dspark_block_size), k < 5 would truncate the block. The catch is that lane A treats DSpark as an MTP module with n_predict = num_nextn_predict_layers = 3 and requires, if k > 3, k % 3 = 0. It's a bus that only leaves with seats filled in multiples of 3: the 6th seat goes to a noise passenger. The smallest valid k is 6, and the input gains a 6th position (a 5th noise token) that was never trained. Play with k and the slots:

\[ R = N_{\text{seqs}}\,(k+1) \qquad\qquad S_{\text{cap}} = 8\left\lceil \frac{R}{8} \right\rceil \qquad\qquad \text{captured sizes} = \{\,j\,(k+1)\ :\ j(k+1) \le S_{\text{cap}}\,\} \]
  • \(R\): verification rows per step (1 anchor + k drafts per request)
  • \(S_{\text{cap}}\): --max-cudagraph-capture-size rounded up to a multiple of 8
  • \(k+1\): uniform_decode_query_len, the stride between captured sizes

With block-k (DSPARK_ENABLE_DSPARK_BLOCK_K=1), the divisibility rule no longer applies to method=dspark, because the stages form a non-causal backbone and are not reused step by step. So k=5 (the trained block) is valid: identical per-position acceptance (0.88/0.74/0.53/0.36/0.24), a cheaper step, +2–10% in single-stream, and the capture of 6×(5+1) = 36 becomes 40. The 64-row warning is real too: nothing above that has been established as clean with respect to the stochastic hangs of #141 (the default of 48 is consistent with the hypothesis, without proving it safe), and at the mild end the stream gets truncated without a finish_reason.

Options: TP=3, Stage-C 200K/16, vision and agent mode

Each option below changes the shape of the default service (2 nodes, 1M/6) and comes with a measured price.

Three Sparks (TP=3)

With ./start-tp3.sh and the WORKER2_* variables, the launcher exports DSPARK_TP3=1, TP_SIZE=3 and NNODES=3, and the conveyor applies patches/tp3/apply_tp3_patch.py. 64 heads and 8 o_groups don't divide by 3 (TP=3 & TP=4): stock refuses or, worse, silently computes 8 // 3 == 2. The patch adds a padding group (8 → 9 groups, 64 → 72 heads) and takes the intermediate dim from 2048 to 2112 and the vocabulary from 129,280 to 129,408. The pads have zero weights and attn_sink = −∞ (a sink of 0 would add 1 to the softmax denominator and dampen the real heads), and every rank logs DSv4 TP pad: heads 64 -> 72 for TP=3. Gloo, TCPStore and bootstrap go over the LAN (TP3_BOOTSTRAP_IFNAME=enP7s7); NCCL uses both CX ports (NCCL_IB_MERGE_NICS=0).

The gain is capacity, not speed: ≈5.0 M tokens of pool (4.79× at 1M), 16 slots and ≈198 tok/s aggregate over 16 streams. Per-stream decode changes little (+4–13%), and prefill gets worse (TTFT 91.5 s versus 74.9 s at 128K), because MLA replicates the latent KV and the indexer on every rank and the 3-node all-reduce costs more.

Stage-C 200K/16 (historical)

The largest aggregate in the 200K/16 shape (315 tok/s static at C16) came from lane B. On lane A, high admission (MAX_NUM_SEQS=32, MAX_NUM_BATCHED_TOKENS=12288) measured 325 tok/s aggregate at c=32, but that is only performance data, with no guarantee against #141; and the 0731 regular-graphs probe reached 340.5 tok/s of aggregate decode at C6. Steps 8–9 — Verify, measure and tune

“It came up” is not “it works”. Verification comes in layers, from cheapest to most expensive:

  1. API identity. curl -fsS http://127.0.0.1:8888/v1/models → "id": "deepseek-v4-flash-vision-exp", "max_model_len": 1048576.
  2. Boot lines, on both ranks. Available KV cache memory: ~17 GiB, GPU KV cache size: ~2.3M tokens, Maximum concurrency … ~2.2x and Capturing CUDA graphs (FULL) 6/6, without “Truncating max_cudagraph_capture_size”. The [dspark-…] patched lines should appear only for the enabled opt-ins, with no EngineDead and no NCCL WARN.
  3. Smoke, status and logs. The smoke test fires 6 concurrent requests at temperature 0. ./status-…sh and ./logs-…sh (TAIL=160) exit with 1 if a probe fails. Inconsistency: status falls back to a Stage-C image if DSPARK_VLLM_IMAGE is not set.
  4. Quality probes. 17×23 = 391 and “capital of Japan = Tokyo”. With garbling or leaking XML: test directly on :8888 at temperature: 0, check the digest and the snapshot (with the encoder) on both nodes, and only then blame the harness.
  5. Audit. bash scripts/run-audit.sh --model deepseek-v4-flash-vision-exp. Expected (Aug 16, best seen): C1 73–76 tok/s, C6 168–180, acceptance ~68–75% (pos0 ~0.93 → pos4 ~0.33), RULER-lite 8/8 at 8K/32K, tools 7/7, #55 always finish=length, garble CLEAN up to ~900K.
  6. Responses API in strict mode. scripts/verify-responses-api-live.py checks SSE, stateful continuation with a tool, strict JSON schema, reasoning and disconnection; a 404 on the continuation is a configuration failure, not “passed without state”.

Predict before you measure

Now we close the calculation from section 2. The time of a step is the time to read the bytes plus the collectives, and each step delivers 1 bonus token plus the accepted drafts:

\[ t_{\text{step}} \approx \frac{B_{\text{rank}}}{\beta_{\text{eff}}} + N_{\text{col}}\,\tau_{\text{col}} \qquad\qquad \text{tok/s} \approx \frac{1 + \mathbb{E}[\text{accepted}]}{t_{\text{step}}}, \quad \mathbb{E}[\text{accepted}] = \sum_{i=1}^{k} a_i \approx \bar{a}\,k \]
  • \(B_{\text{rank}}\): bytes per rank per step (≈16.6 GB at c=1, k=6)
  • \(\beta_{\text{eff}}\): effective memory bandwidth (230–250 GB/s)
  • \(N_{\text{col}}\,\tau_{\text{col}}\): ≈108 collectives × 30–50 µs
  • \(a_i\): fraction of steps with position \(i\) accepted. It never grows with \(i\) (position \(i\) only counts if the previous ones did); with a constant chance α, \(a_i = \alpha^i\)
  • \(\bar{a}\): reported mean acceptance (fraction of the k drafts)

The simple model gets it right. With the acceptance measured on code (73.3%), it predicts ~76 tok/s versus 76.0 measured; with prose (25.7%), ~36 versus 37.3; with the audit's (~68–75%), ~75 versus C1 73–76. The lesson changes how you tune: the biggest decode lever left is neither kernels nor network, it is acceptance, as September's own A/Bs say about the gap between natural prose (~25%) and the audit (~70%). And k larger than 6 doesn't pay off: positions 5 and 6 accept only 0.28/0.21 in the best probe and make the step more expensive.

Why noise and dates matter

The c=1 noise in this lane is ±10 tok/s depending on the prompt (47.7–76.5 measured), and the same profile measured 15–25% less on Sep 2 than on Aug 14 without any knob changing. That is why the recipe demands one variable per boot, a same-day baseline, ≥8 trials with the median, and boot gates (FULL 6/6, no “Truncating”, expected KV), and rejects any knob that costs ≥0.5 GB of MemAvailable or increases swap. The recipe, one sentence per decision

TP=2 because the weights don't fit on one node. Image pinned by digest because the patches are source-exact. A fail-closed conveyor because half a fix is worse than none. nvfp4_ds_mla + #22 because the layout is FP8 and only the dispatch needed to change. --block-size 256 to equalize the hybrid pages. k=6 because block 5 and n_predict 3 leave no other option without block-k. Capture rounded to 8 so that c=6 stays in the graph. Chunk 1024 + the #27 cap so that decode never starves. 1M as a ceiling, not a reservation, so that the pool limits concurrency.

Across the three recipes

The KV of uses fp8_ds_mla with 656 B per token and layer (512 B of FP8 NoPE, 16 B of scales and 128 B of a zeroed RoPE block, which brings the geometry to 576 dims); here stores 584 B, because V4 has 448 NoPE dims + 64 RoPE and 1-byte UE8M0 scales. And breakable graphs are the same enemy in : on the v0.30 lane they caused 10–18% variance between identical runs and were turned off with the same VLLM_USE_BREAKABLE_CUDAGRAPH=0.

With this, you have rebuilt the trail's three recipes (GLM, Qwen and this one). What's left is learning to measure rigorously, in Benchmarking, and then applying everything to a freshly released model, in Recipe from scratch.

Case study: DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash — one model, five deployments

Everything above serves V4-Flash on two Sparks with vLLM. Its successor, DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash-1017, came with something rare: five public deployments of the same checkpoint, read for this section on 2026-10-30. Four are Mia's AI Lab recipes for 2, 3 and 4 DGX Sparks (two engines, three fabrics); the fifth is DeepInfer, the engine DeepSeek uses in production. Same weights, five answers: it is the best material in the trail for telling what belongs to the model from what belongs to the hardware. Every number below comes with its source and its conditions; where it is our own arithmetic, it says computed.

What V4.1-Flash-1017 changes

None of the repositories lists the changes against V4-Flash, so this list puts side by side what this chapter documented for V4-Flash and what the V4.1 repositories describe:

  • A third memory axis: Engram. Two n-gram tables of ~384 million rows each (~203 GB, FP8 values with their scales in separate tensors), read by two layers on every decode round and prompt chunk (conditional memory, N-gram & MTP). They are lookups, not matmuls, so every Spark recipe keeps them out of the GPU-resident weights: on each node's NVMe, one shard per rank (SGLang: ~63 GiB per node at TP=3, ~48 GiB at TP=4), or on local disk read with O_DIRECT (TensorFold).
  • More experts, a much bigger checkpoint. 384 routed experts (V4-Flash: 256). The native checkpoint is ~510 GB (476 GiB): routed experts 289 GB, Engram 203 GB, the DSpark draft 7.9 GB and everything else 10.5 GB [SGLang recipe, docs/fast-load.md]. V4-Flash-Vision-Exp was ~157 GiB.
  • The same attention geometry. 64 heads, 8 o_groups and a 129,280-token vocabulary, exactly what made TP=3 hard earlier in this chapter, plus 128 draft experts in the DSpark block, which don't divide by 3 either.
  • Tiny KV. According to the SGLang recipe, only four kv_source layers store KV: in FP8, 1,670.75 B per token per rank (MLA latent, replicated across TP), so 1M tokens take 1.67 GB, plus a fixed 0.34 GB sliding-window pool. The 2-Spark TensorFold recipe stores it in compressed FP4 (about 0.9 GiB per rank per 1,048,576 positions).
  • What stays. The 1,048,576-token window, DSpark draft blocks shipped in the checkpoint (blocks of 5 tokens, verified exactly) and a vision tower; video only as sampled frames (4-Spark TensorFold). The reasoning effort is now a number from 1 to 100 placed in the prompt (the publisher maps low/high/max to 50/75/100).

What an AI researcher should extract

1. Quantize to fit fewer nodes, or keep the native weights on more. Two Sparks only fit the model at 2.9 bits per weight (196 GiB), and the quantization's model card publishes no perplexity or KL against the original: its quality cost is unmeasured. Four Sparks run DeepSeek's weights as published and check the native kernels against DeepSeek's reference arithmetic. The surprise is speed: on code, greedy, thinking off and one stream, the pair measured 118.1 tok/s and the four-node ring 117.4 (measured separately, so read it as a tie, not a ranking). Interpretation: decode is set by the bytes each node reads per step (~98 versus ~80 GiB of weights per node) and by the collectives, not by the size of the whole model. What the extra nodes buy is everything else: 16 streams instead of 4, the full window with a 2M-position pool, about twice the prefill rate and no quantization error to audit.

2. Padding at TP=3, exact shards at TP=4. The 64 heads, the 8 groups, the vocabulary and the 128 draft experts don't divide by 3 (TP=3 & TP=4). SGLang pads heads 64 → 96 and groups 8 → 12, because 32 local heads is the smallest size its SM120 sparse-MLA kernels instantiate, so rank 2's attention shard is entirely padding (head padding). The V4-Flash recipe in this chapter padded only to 72 heads: the pad size is set by the kernel, not by the model. The padding also had a hidden cost: the all-zero blocks had never-written scales holding denormals, the MXFP8 re-encoding rejected them and 86 layers fell back to Triton until an adapter set those scales to 1.0. At TP=4 each rank gets exactly 16 heads and 2 groups, and the repository measures four nodes faster than three at every concurrency, despite one more hop per collective (prose at one stream: 87.7 versus 51.0 or 37.9 tok/s).

3. The memory left over decides whether 1M fits. The KV is not the problem: 1M tokens of FP8 latent take 1.67 GB per rank. What decides is what the weights leave behind. At TP=3, ~101 GiB of weights per rank leave ~6 GB free while serving, and the recipe configures 262,144 tokens with long prompts bound by that headroom. At TP=4, ~77 GiB leave ~40 GB, the profile keeps ~24 GiB per rank outside the static pool for the prefill transient, and the 1,011,084-token needle passed. That transient is the indexer: about 14 B × chunk × prefix, so ~15 GB for 1,024-token chunks at 1M context and ~60 GB for 4,096-token chunks, which is why the 4-Spark profile scores the chunk in bounded row slices (DSV41_INDEXER_CHUNKED=1). On two Sparks the same arithmetic sets the practical window: with ~98 GiB of weights, a needle in a 479,012-token prompt was found 3 of 3 times with the head at 2.67 GiB free, 0.17 GiB above the watchdog's floor, so the recipe calls 524,288 the limit for a head that runs other software.

4. Switch or ring with four nodes. A Spark has two ConnectX-7 ports: three nodes form a full triangle, four don't. Either you add a RoCE switch or you cable a switchless ring, and then rank placement becomes a design decision. TensorFold places the 2×2 grid's nodes 0, 1, 3, 2 around the ring so that every row partner pair and every column partner pair has its own cable; only the all-gathers reach the node across the ring, through a relayed hop (its two neighbors relay half each). SGLang needs a patched NCCL (sparkring, with NCCL_SWITCHLESS_RING_ONLY=1): environment variables on stock NCCL are not enough. The 4× TensorFold numbers were measured on the ring, and its README notes that a switch removes the relayed hop. A small detail with a large effect: the idle RDMA proxies used to spin ~300% CPU per node and now park on a futex once every rank agrees (0.21–0.32%, patch 0034).

5. Generic or specialized engine. SGLang is a general engine bent with import hooks (sitecustomize.py) and gated adapters: it brings a mature server, but at TP=3 it needed padding repairs and allocator workarounds (with expandable_segments:True, every prefill of more than 64 query tokens returned NaN logits). The native TensorFold engine is a family written for this model: exact (drafted replies equal undrafted ones, checked by token-hash replay), but every fix is one more patch in a series of 33 or 36. DeepInfer is specialization taken to the top, written by the model's authors with their own kernels (FlashMLA, DeepGEMM, DeepEP). Yet the release is offline inference for SM100 and Ascend 950, and the repository itself lists what is missing for a complete service: a KV cache system, prefill/decode (and vision encoder) disaggregation, and load balancing across data-parallel replicas.

Deep dive: determinism is a property of the deployment

The same class of bug shows up in all three engines: a reduction whose order depends on the shape of the batch. In 2× TensorFold, cuBLAS picks its kernel and split-K from the GEMM's shape, so the mixed EAQ v1.1 pack runs its plain linears over a fixed 16 rows (TF_DS_PLAIN_ROWS, patch 0026); without it, drafts-on and drafts-off replies matched on only 4 of 8 prompts. In SGLang, the fused MoE finalize (atomic bf16 adds) is off at TP=3, and TP=4 replaces it with a fixed-order sum. DeepInfer turns the property into a switch: DEEPINFER_BATCH_INVARIANT=1 for batch-invariant kernels, plus a fixed seq_seed for deterministic sampling. On a cluster this matters even more: reproducible evaluations, RL rollouts that match the trainer's numbers (RL fundamentals) and regressions you can bisect.

DeepInfer publishes its performance as a Pareto frontier, not as single numbers: decode on 64 GPUs (EP64·DP64), each point a batch of 8 to 256 requests per data-parallel rank; prefill on 4 GPUs (EP4·DP4), each point a chunk of 4,096 to 65,536 tokens per rank. Read the fine print: the decode curve multiplies the measured steps per second by τ = 1 + 3 × 0.75 = 3.25, an acceptance assumed by the plotting script, not measured in the sweep. The Spark tables can be read the same way, with each concurrency level as one point, and that is what the figure's second view does.

From Sparks to production: the same decisions on H200 and NVIDIA AI Enterprise

Each Spark trade-off has a direct counterpart in a datacenter; what changes is which side of it hurts.

  • Fit. An HGX H200 node has 8 × 141 GB = 1,128 GB of HBM3e (public spec). The native ~510 GB checkpoint fits in one node with ~600 GB to spare (computed), more if Engram's 203 GB stay outside HBM, so quantization stops being a condition for fitting and becomes a cost and throughput decision. One caveat: Hopper (SM90) has no FP4 tensor cores, so the MXFP4 experts run through weight-only kernels (they save memory, not math); Blackwell (SM100, DeepInfer's target, and the GB10's SM121) has them.
  • Engram. On a Spark, host memory is GPU memory, so the tables go to NVMe. On an HGX node host DRAM is separate from HBM: the tables can stay in host memory or on local NVMe and be fetched row by row, the access pattern to design for (interpretation).
  • TP inside the NVLink domain. Pick TP degrees that divide 64 heads and 8 groups (2, 4 or 8) and keep them inside the node, where NVSwitch makes the many small decode all-reduces cheap. The TP=3 lesson becomes: never let the node count dictate a degree that doesn't divide the model.
  • KV and long context. The MLA latent is replicated on every TP rank (1,670.75 B/token on each). On a cluster you avoid that with attention DP: each GPU keeps only its own requests' KV, and the experts are spread with EP across GPUs and nodes (DeepInfer: EP64·DP64), with the redundant experts of EPLB (384+64) balancing the load. For a single very long prompt, context parallelism splits the sequence (DeepInfer's balanced CP; Parallelism).
  • Fabric. Inside a node there is no switch-or-ring question; across nodes it comes back as the InfiniBand or RoCE fabric, and the Spark lesson holds: map each collective to the link it uses most, TP on NVLink and the EP all-to-all on the largest scale-up domain available (DeepInfer measured decode on 64 GPUs inside one).
  • Disaggregation. DeepInfer sizes prefill (4 GPUs) and decode (64 GPUs) separately because they saturate differently: prefill per GPU flattens around 32K–64K tokens per step, while decode per GPU keeps rising with requests as each user slows down. On H200 the same idea is prefill/decode disaggregation, with prefill nodes and decode nodes and the KV handed over between them. NVIDIA AI Enterprise packages a supported stack for it (NIM microservices on engines such as TensorRT-LLM, vLLM and SGLang, and NVIDIA Dynamo for disaggregated serving and KV-aware routing; check the current support matrix for V4.1). The ratio between the pools comes from your traffic (prompt versus output lengths), not from the model (interpretation).

Five deployments, one checkpoint: what moved between them was never the model, only the bytes per node, the shards, the links and the engine. The next chapter, Benchmarking, turns the discipline that separated 51.0 from 37.9 here into a method.
