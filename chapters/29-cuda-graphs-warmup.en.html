Imagine a GPU that finishes a small matrix multiplication in a few microseconds and then sits idle, waiting for the CPU to say what comes next. Now multiply that by ~1,500 to 1,800 launches per step — the estimate from the DeepSeek recipe's audit (the static-reading report every repository ships in docs/CLAUDE/fable5-1-report.md) for a decode step with 6 conversations running in eager mode. This is no minor detail: when it turned out, in the DeepSeek recipe, that the 6-conversation step had no CUDA graph, changing a single calculation in the compose file (42 → 48) was enough to take the aggregate from 122 to 139 tok/s. This chapter explains what it means to record and replay an entire GPU step, why that requires everything to have a fixed shape, and the whole boot ritual — compile, tune, warm up, verify — that the recipes go through before accepting the first request.

The invisible bottleneck: launching a kernel is expensive

In ch. 26 we saw the queue: the CPU does not execute kernels, it launches them onto a CUDA stream and moves on, and the GPU consumes them in order. Here the focus is the cost of that launch and what to do about it. Every call goes through the Python interpreter, the PyTorch dispatcher (which picks the implementation for the dtype and device), the CUDA runtime and the driver: from a few microseconds (driver alone) to a few dozen (Python + PyTorch) per operation. Illustrative order of magnitude, not a measurement from the recipes.

As long as the CPU enqueues faster than the GPU consumes, the queue absorbs the difference and the GPU never goes idle. When the CPU is slower than the kernels, the queue drains and the GPU starts waiting: the step becomes limited by launches (launch-bound). The figure simulates this for three layers of a MoE in decode: on top, eager mode; below, the same work played as a graph.

The launch cost does not depend on the size of the work: launching a 1-token GEMV costs the same as launching a 256-token one. That is why the problem is acute in decode and almost disappears in prefill, where each kernel processes thousands of tokens. With the graph, kernels come out back to back: the CPU only copies the inputs and hits play.

\[ c_i = c_{i-1} + t_{\text{cpu}}, \qquad s_i = \max\big(c_i,\; s_{i-1} + g_{i-1}\big), \qquad T_{\text{eager}} = s_N + g_N \] \[ T_{\text{graph}} \approx t_{\text{replay}} + \sum_{i=1}^{N} \big(g_i + \varepsilon\big) \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \frac{T_{\text{eager}}}{T_{\text{graph}}} \;\text{grows when}\; t_{\text{cpu}} \gg g_i \]
  • \(N\): kernels in the step (thousands in a large LLM)
  • \(t_{\text{cpu}}\): CPU cost of launching one kernel
  • \(c_i\): time at which the CPU finishes launching kernel \(i\)
  • \(g_i\): duration of kernel \(i\) on the GPU
  • \(s_i\): time at which kernel \(i\) starts on the GPU
  • \(t_{\text{replay}}\): CPU cost of copying the inputs and launching the graph
  • \(\varepsilon\): gap between kernels inside the graph (small)

In plain language, the recurrence says: kernel \(i\) starts once both things have happened — the CPU has launched it and the GPU has finished the previous one. If \(t_{\text{cpu}} > g_i\) for most kernels, the \(\max\) is always dominated by the CPU and \(T_{\text{eager}} \approx N \cdot t_{\text{cpu}}\): the fastest GPU in the world won't help.

Analogy

A kitchen (the GPU) and a waiter (the CPU). In eager mode, the waiter brings every order separately: “one norm… now a multiplication… now the attention”. If the dish is ready in 5 seconds and the waiter takes 10 to walk back and forth, the kitchen spends half its time twiddling its thumbs. A CUDA graph is handing over the fixed menu for the entire dinner in one go: the waiter just says “the usual, with these fresh ingredients”.

vLLM V1 already hides part of the CPU cost with async scheduling: the scheduler prepares step N+1 while the GPU executes step N. That hides the scheduling, not the thousands of launches inside the step. That is why the DeepSeek recipe's audit estimated a modest gain (“+3–10%”) from capturing the 6-conversation step (GPU at ~150 ms per step, CPU almost always ahead), and yet the measurement came out at +14% (122 → 139 tok/s; or −12% without the graph).

Record and replay: the anatomy of a CUDA graph

A CUDA graph is a directed graph of GPU operations (kernels, copies, memsets) in which every parameter is already resolved: which kernel, with which grid, reading which addresses, with which scalars. It is born through stream capture: you put the stream into recording mode (cudaStreamBeginCapture), run the forward normally, and everything that would be enqueued is recorded instead of executed. When you finish (cudaStreamEndCapture), the graph is instantiated (cudaGraphInstantiate) into an executable that the driver validates and optimizes once. From then on, cudaGraphLaunch performs the replay of hundreds or thousands of kernels with a single call.

In PyTorch, this shows up as torch.cuda.CUDAGraph and the with torch.cuda.graph(g): context. Three practical details matter. (1) Before capturing, you run the code a few times in eager mode, so that lazy initializations (cuBLAS handles, allocations, autotunes) happen outside the recording. (2) Tensors allocated during capture come from a private pool owned by the graph, so their addresses stay valid on every replay. (3) Inputs and outputs are static buffers: to run with new data, you copy the data into the same tensor, never swap the tensor. Follow the full cycle in the figure, with real numbers in the matrices.

What the recording freezes is, quite literally, everything the CPU decided while it was recording:

  1. Pointers. The graph reads and writes the addresses from capture time; a new tensor, at a different address, is invisible to it.
  2. Shapes, strides and grids. Rows, the stride between rows and blocks per kernel are all fixed. That is why there is one graph per batch size (next section).
  3. Branches taken on the CPU. A Python if evaluated during capture picks one path, and only that path goes onto the tape. On replay there is no Python.
  4. Scalars passed by value. An integer computed on the CPU becomes a recorded constant.
  5. Radio silence with the CPU. Nothing inside the graph can wait for the CPU or hand values back to it: no synchronization, no synchronous CPU→GPU copy, no new allocation.
Deep dive — why the error is silent

A CUDA graph checks nothing on replay: it doesn't know the tensor “moved” or that the request now has 9,000 tokens instead of 900. Break the contract and you don't get an exception — you get wrong tokens, sometimes only every now and then. Several of the recipes' fixes exist precisely because of this kind of intermittent corruption.

Four real traps (and how the recipes avoid them)

Pick a scenario in the figure. Each one is a documented story from the recipes; turn on “apply the fix” to see the solution that was adopted.

  • Branch decided on the CPU — a reminder from ch. 13: in DeepSeek-V4's C4A layers, each compressed KV entry summarizes compress_ratio = 4 tokens and the indexer picks the best topk_tokens = 512; in the C128A layers, the C128 compressor summarizes 128 tokens per entry. The backport hotfix-dsv4-skip-topk-49486.sh skips scoring when max_seq_len / compress_ratio ≤ topk_tokens (up to 2,048 tokens). Taken during capture, the shortcut would be replayed for long prefixes, selecting candidates 0..topk−1 without scoring them; the upstream guard #52492 forbids it under torch.cuda.is_current_stream_capturing(). For the same reason, hotfix-dsv4-skip-empty-c128-48957.sh only skips the C128 compressor outside FULL mode, which cannot branch on CPU metadata at every step.
  • Frozen stride — hotfix-dsv4-adaptive-topk-50004.sh (adaptive top-k width) was removed on 2026-08-22 after the upstream revert (#51318): the builder wrote with the live batch's stride, the FULL graphs read with the capture's stride, and rows ≥ 1 read stale slots — intermittent corruption under MTP + prefix cache + CUDA graphs.
  • CPU→GPU copy — in the EXL3 MoE, pin_exl3_expert_map moves the expert_map to the GPU once (“CUDA graph capture forbids a CPU→GPU copy”), and map_topk_to_local converts global ids into local ones on the device, sending ids that belong to another rank to the sentinel n_local.
  • Allocation during capture — work buffers must exist before recording starts. GLM sizes the E3 scratch by EXL3_FAT_GROUPED_TOPK (MNBT × topk rows), because “growing inside a graph capture is an error”, and the cooperative MoE allocates its scratch between weight loading and capture; DS reserves its buffers through the workspace manager in the dummy run, before lock_workspace() (backport #50298), and the Stage-C overlay's b12x MoE refuses to copy during capture.
In TensorFold — the same contract, different solutions

The engine behind the GLM recipe's second version runs into the same rules. Addresses: the decode KDA sizes its scratch (reserve()) before any capture, because “a graph keeps the addresses it was captured with”, and buffers that have been outgrown are not freed while a graph may still point at them. Capture without touching live state: the batched verification is recorded on slot 0, in the pool's first rows, before any request is admitted, and slot 0's states are zeroed afterwards; the drafter's padding rows write into “trash” rows reserved for that purpose. CPU→GPU copies: each round's metadata goes up through pinned buffers, with an asynchronous copy and an event, so the host never rewrites a buffer that is still in flight. Section 9 puts these pieces together.

Fixed shapes: capture sizes, padding and the 42 → 48 lesson

If each graph freezes the shape, vLLM has to record one graph per batch size. It keeps a list of capture sizes (tokens per step). A batch of \(T\) tokens uses the smallest captured size \(\ge T\), filling the extra rows with padding; a batch larger than the last size runs eager. In the Qwen recipe, for example, padding rows use NULL_BLOCK_ID and are masked even inside the short-conv of the PLE (the model's N-gram memory), so that they don't write state anywhere.

With speculative decoding (fundamentals in ch. 35), each conversation in decode contributes \(k+1\) tokens per step (the last accepted token plus \(k\) drafts to verify), so a uniform decode step with \(c\) conversations has \(c(k+1)\) verify rows. vLLM knows this and, in the function adjust_cudagraph_sizes_for_spec_decode, rounds each size in the list up to a multiple of \(k+1\) and discards whatever exceeds the ceiling.

\[ T(c) = c\,(k+1), \qquad s^{*}(T) = \min\{\, s \in \mathcal{S} \;:\; s \ge T \,\}, \qquad \text{waste} = \frac{s^{*} - T}{s^{*}} \]
  • \(c\): conversations in decode in the step (≤ max_num_seqs)
  • \(k\): speculative tokens per conversation
  • \(\mathcal{S}\): set of captured sizes
  • \(s^{*}\): graph used; if none exists, the step runs eager

Now the DeepSeek recipe's story, step by step. The compose file passed --max-cudagraph-capture-size = MAX_NUM_SEQS × (MTP_NUM_TOKENS+1) = 6 × 7 = 42. vLLM builds the default grid [1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 24, 32, 40]; 42 isn't on it, so the log warns “Truncating max_cudagraph_capture_size to 40”. Rounding to multiples of 7 turns the grid into [7, 14, 21, 28, 35] (40 would become 42 > 40 and is discarded): “Capturing CUDA graphs (FULL): 5/5”. The step with 6 conversations (42 tokens) has no graph and runs eager. The fix was a single expression in the compose file — round the ceiling up to a multiple of 8:

# in docker-compose every dollar sign appears doubled (compose escaping); in plain shell:
--max-cudagraph-capture-size $(( ( ${MAX_NUM_SEQS:-6} * (${MTP_NUM_TOKENS:-6} + 1) + 7 ) / 8 * 8 ))
# 6 × 7 = 42 → 48 → grid [1,2,4,8,…,48] → multiples of 7: [7,14,21,28,35,42]  (FULL 6/6)

Graph modes: NONE, PIECEWISE, FULL — and the “breakable” ones

Not every part of the forward is equally easy to record. Linears, norm, activation and MoE depend only on the number of tokens. Attention depends on per-request metadata — how many tokens each conversation already has, where its KV cache blocks are, who is in prefill and who is in decode — which the model runner builds at every step. Each attention backend declares how much recording it can tolerate (AttentionCGSupport: ALWAYS, UNIFORM_BATCH, UNIFORM_SINGLE_TOKEN_DECODE or NEVER); in a model with several backends the minimum applies, and vLLM downgrades the mode automatically. Hence the cudagraph_mode modes:

ModeWhat gets recordedWho uses it
NONEnothing (debugging)Qwen TP1 with CUDAGRAPH_MODE=NONE
PIECEWISEeverything except attention: the forward is split at the attention operations, the pieces become graphs and attention runs eager between them (requires piecewise compilation)part of the default mode
FULLthe entire forward, including non-uniform batches (if the backend supports it)—
FULL_DECODE_ONLYfull graph only for uniform decode; prefill and mixed steps run without a graph
FULL_AND_PIECEWISEFULL for uniform decode + PIECEWISE for the rest (the default when piecewise compilation is on) default (the boot shows FULL and PIECEWISE) · no --compilation-config: same default, by inference (mixed steps in piecewise)

The piecewise graph is the wildcard: it serves any batch composition, because the pieces depend only on the number of tokens. The FULL graph is the speed champion, but it requires attention metadata in fixed-size persistent buffers — in practice, uniform decode. The dispatcher chooses which graph to play via a BatchDescriptor (num_tokens, num_reqs, uniform, has_lora). The conveyor belt shows what each mode records in a uniform decode and in a mixed step (a prefill chunk + decodes).

Look at the mixed step: with FULL_DECODE_ONLY it loses the whole graph; with FULL_AND_PIECEWISE it falls back to piecewise. That is why mixing prefill into a decode step is expensive: in GLM, a prefill chunk from another request knocked the step off the uniform FULL path and cost seconds, which gave birth to the GLM53_MIXED_PREFILL_CHUNK=fair policy (details in scheduler and batching). And the dispatcher key can have bugs: regression vllm#53030, a BatchDescriptor collision in PIECEWISE graphs, pinned draft acceptance at 1.00 at position 0 — GLM's spec-accept-gate.sh script exists to catch it (it passes if position 0 stays below 0.999 with ≥ 100 drafts).

In TensorFold — there is no mixed step

TensorFold's GLM scheduler alternates iterations: either a prompt chunk (up to 1,024 rows while other streams decode) or a decode round, by a credit (TF_GLM_FILL_SHARE = 0.5: chunk, round, chunk…). A prompt chunk never shares a forward with decode rows. So decode always runs in the graph for its width, and prefill — chunks of up to 2,048 rows, with kernels long enough to hide the launch cost (section 1) — runs eagerly. The problem that the fair policy patched over in vLLM disappears by construction; the cost is that long, fresh prompts arriving together wait their turn (scheduler and batching).

Breakable CUDA graphs: the variant DS and Qwen turn off

Breakable CUDA graphs are an alternative to vLLM's CUDAGraphWrapper: instead of cutting the torch.compile graph at the attention ops, marked functions break the capture at capture time — they close the current segment, run eager on the capture stream, register the callable for replay and open a new segment. The v0.30.0 release note says they now require opt-in. Anemll 0.1.1 (vLLM 0.25.2.dev, which predates that change) turns them on for DeepSeek V4 when VLLM_USE_BREAKABLE_CUDAGRAPH is absent; and the Qwen recipe records that v0.30 itself turns them on for Qwen4Exp, despite the note — there may be per-model auto-enablement (the documentation doesn't say). DS and Qwen don't rely on the default: they pin it to 0. GLM doesn't set the variable, and its boot log (quoted in patch_skip_cudagraph_profile.py) shows the breakable_cudagraph logger: we can't claim the path is turned off there (interpretation).

Vocabulary trap — “capture” doesn't always mean CUDA graph

VLLM_DSV4_DSPARK_DEFER_TARGET_CAPTURE and _EXACT (both 0 in the Stage-C override, lane B) appear in DS's docs/ENVS.md as “Defer target cudagraph capture”, but the overlay code shows that they defer the capture of the target-layer features consumed by the DSpark drafter, recording them in the next layer so as not to break the post/pre fusion of the hyper-connections (mHC) (EXACT uses dspark_hc_post_mean; without it, residual.mean(dim=1)). Drafters go through the dispatcher too. In lane B's readable code (Stage-C overlay), the DSpark proposer wraps the drafter in a CUDAGraphWrapper in PIECEWISE mode with persistent buffers (_draft_input_ids_buffer, _draft_hidden_buffer…) and pads the batch with noise_token_id, zeros and slot 0 (lane A reorganizes this logic into code that isn't in the repo); in Qwen, the audit notes that the MTP drafter passes are also dispatched through the graph dispatcher (see DFlash2 & DSpark).

torch.compile, Inductor and opaque custom ops

CUDA graphs eliminate the cost of launching kernels; torch.compile attacks on another front: launching fewer kernels and generating better ones. It has two halves. TorchDynamo intercepts the Python bytecode of forward and records the tensor operations into an FX graph, together with guards (dtype, device, shape range) that say when that graph remains valid; anything it can't follow causes a graph break. Inductor generates code from the graph, fusing neighboring operations — residual add + norm + activation become a single Triton kernel that reads memory once instead of three times.

In vLLM this is CompilationConfig.mode, passed via --compilation-config:

mode 0 · NONE
no torch.compile: eager PyTorch (CUDA graphs are still possible).
mode 1 · STOCK_TORCH_COMPILE
the standard torch.compile pipeline.
mode 2 · DYNAMO_TRACE_ONCE
a single Dynamo trace, no recompilation.
mode 3 · VLLM_COMPILE
vLLM's Inductor backend: cache, piecewise compilation (split at the attention ops, via splitting_ops such as torch.ops.vllm.unified_attention_with_output), shape specialization and fusion passes. The default in V1. Everything compiles before serving; the cache lives in VLLM_CACHE_ROOT/torch_compile_cache/{hash}/{rank}.

Note the connection: the PIECEWISE mode from the previous section depends on mode 3, because it is the compiler that splits the graph at the attention ops. That is why, in v0.30, uncompiled models fall back to FULL_DECODE_ONLY. The figure shows what happens to a decoder layer as it goes through torch.compile.

Custom ops: how an external call survives the compiler and the capture

Dynamo can't see inside a C++/CUDA extension (such as exllamav3_ext) or inside Python code full of state and side effects. The way out is to register the call as a custom op: the compiler then sees it as a single opaque node that it doesn't open, fuse or reorder. In vLLM, direct_register_custom_op registers the op in the vllm namespace (it becomes torch.ops.vllm.name) with three pieces:

  1. op_func — the real implementation. What actually runs, in eager mode and inside graphs.
  2. fake_impl — the “pretend” implementation. During tracing, the compiler runs the op on fake tensors (shape and dtype only); the fake impl says what the output shape is without computing anything.
  3. mutates_args — what the op writes in place. Declaring mutates_args prevents the compiler from assuming a tensor hasn't changed and from reordering or eliminating the write.

The recipes have both flavors. Qwen's PLE short-conv writes into a preallocated output buffer (hence mutates_args=["output"] and a fake impl that returns nothing):

def qwen3_8_flash_next_ple_short_conv(inputs, output, layer_name) -> None:
    layer = get_forward_context().no_compile_layers[layer_name]
    result = layer._short_conv(inputs)
    output[: result.shape[0]].copy_(result)

def qwen3_8_flash_next_ple_short_conv_fake(inputs, output, layer_name) -> None:
    return

direct_register_custom_op(
    op_name="qwen3_8_flash_next_ple_short_conv",
    op_func=qwen3_8_flash_next_ple_short_conv,
    mutates_args=["output"],
    fake_impl=qwen3_8_flash_next_ple_short_conv_fake,
)

GLM's dense EXL3 linear forward, torch.ops.vllm.dense_exl3_forward, on the other hand, returns a new tensor; its fake impl only needs to get the shape right — x.new_empty((*x.shape[:-1], sum(output_sizes))) — and mutates_args=[]. That way the trellis kernel stays “opaque to dynamo/inductor and capturable in a CUDA graph”. Opaque doesn't mean exempt: on the inside, the op still obeys the capture contract (no sync, fixed shape). vLLM's own attention is a custom op — which is why it serves as the piecewise split point.

Parameter--compilation-config '{"mode":0,"cudagraph_mode":"FULL_DECODE_ONLY"}'

No torch.compile/Inductor (mode 0), and full graphs only for pure decode, where launch overhead matters; the reasons: stability and startup time. The audit suggested trying mode 3 + FULL_AND_PIECEWISE (the custom ops register fake impls and the classes carry @support_torch_compile): estimated gain of 0–8%, +2–5 min of cold start and a cache in ~/.cache/vllm. Not adopted. DS uses the default (mode 3); so does GLM, which doesn't pass --compilation-config — by inference.

In the recipe — the AOT cache has a key

The torch.compile cache is addressed by code and configuration. DS's runtime ablation (hotfix-dsv4-runtime-ablation.py, see abliteration) changes the forward, so it keeps a stamp in VLLM_CACHE_ROOT/.dsv4_ablate_stamp with the version, on/off, λ, layers, direction hash and patch hash; if the stamp changes, it deletes torch_compile_cache before boot. An elegant detail: the _refusal_dir buffer is injected into all layers, even those that don't get the projection, to keep a single torch.compile/AOT signature.

A note on Qwen TP1: the custom op ple_offload_wait made the GPU wait on a flag via stream memory ops (cuStreamWaitValue32), which the GB10 doesn't support; inside the graph the wait waited for nothing, and the worker spun forever. The fix became a host-side handshake (unified memory).

Host-device synchronization: the hot path's handbrake

In ch. 26 we saw the .tolist() bubble: in a host-device synchronization, the CPU stops until the GPU queue drains, reads the value and only then resumes launching, while the GPU sits idle. Here the focus is what this does to capture. Besides .item(), .tolist(), .cpu() and if tensor:, the sneakiest culprits are nonzero(), torch.unique and x[mask], whose output size depends on the data: the CPU needs to know “how many” before allocating the result. Even bincount can stage through the host.

Inside a capture it's worse: nothing executes during recording, so the value the CPU is asking for doesn't exist — CUDA refuses the operation and invalidates the capture. And even if it worked, the value seen during capture would be frozen onto the tape, like the branch in section 2. The figure shows the bubble in a step with several MoE layers; below, the same routing done two ways.

How much does it really cost? Two numbers from the recipes:

GLM · “fat” experts in prefill

The legacy path (experts with more than 128 rows) did one .nonzero().tolist() per layer; in the 3-step profile at 16k tokens, aten::nonzero consumed 1.45 s of CPU (29%). The E3 tier (EXL3_FAT_GROUPED) describes the fat experts in on-device tables (prefix sums, 64-row segments via searchsorted) and covers everything in 3 launches, sync-free and capturable: +37–45% vs E2 in cold prefill (ch. 28).

DS · reasoning budget

The first version of thinking_token_budget (#31) scanned the tokens on the CPU at every step (O(n) in Python × MTP rows): a ~4.7× cliff in decode (~4.5 tok/s). v2 uses two Triton kernels on the GPU, with no per-step D2H copy: at 65K context, 37.2 → 59.3 tok/s.

The recipes keep reusing a small repertoire to get the CPU out of the way — the same one you'll use when porting a new model:

  1. Fixed shape with a sentinel. Instead of filtering (variable size), map whatever doesn't matter to a sentinel value: map_topk_to_local sends non-local ids to n_local.
  2. Counting on the device. scatter_add_ instead of bincount; num_active = -1 (maximum-concurrency grid) instead of an .item() to size the launch.
  3. Tables on the device. Prefix sums and searchsorted on the GPU produce the indices a Python loop would produce (GLM's E3).
  4. Masks on the GPU. In lane B, DSpark masks the rejected rows of the drafter's ring buffer right on the GPU (VLLM_DSPARK_GPU_REJECTED_CONTEXT_MASK: default 0, 1 in the Stage-C override), without bringing rejection counts back to the CPU.
  5. Clamp on the device, log outside the capture. DSpark's slot guard in lane B (DSPARK_SLOT_CLAMP=1) does clamp(0, num_rows−1) on the GPU (capture-safe) and only logs DSPARK_STALE_SLOT_INDEX outside the capture, because logging requires a sync.
  6. If you can't avoid it, one sync instead of N. The EXL3_FAT_SORTED tier does a single counts.tolist() and takes contiguous slices of the sort, instead of one sync per expert.
Modification_Clock · _Late · a single pinned copy (patch 0013)

In TensorFold, every decode round used to have a torch.cuda.synchronize() just for timing, two pageable .cpu() copies of the DFlash2 candidates (“two syncs and two pageable copies”) and the text work — detokenizing, looking for stop strings, writing the stream — between rounds, with the GPU idle. Three changes fixed it. The window's time is now measured with CUDA events, read after the sample has already waited for the GPU (“timing adds no host-device sync”). The candidates went into a single buffer read by one non-blocking pinned copy + event. And _Late hands a round's tokens to the server one round later, when the next window is already queued: the text work runs while the GPU verifies. It is the async-scheduling principle from section 1, applied to the output.

CPU metadata is not a sync

Deciding something on the CPU from what the scheduler already knows (lengths, positions) synchronizes nothing — #48957 computes c128_boundary that way. The only problem is using that decision inside a FULL graph, which would freeze it. A sync is when the information comes from the GPU.

JIT: kernels born on first use — caches and shape warmup

Many of the recipes' kernels don't exist as binaries inside the image: they are compiled on the spot, via JIT. Triton turns the kernel's Python into intermediate IRs, then into PTX and a cubin for sm_121a; TileLang (DS's mHC kernels), CuTeDSL (the b12x MoE), DeepGEMM (via nvcc/NVRTC) and FlashInfer do the same; and the driver itself, when it receives PTX, compiles it and stores the cubin in ~/.nv/ComputeCache. Each compilation takes anywhere from seconds to minutes.

The subtle point is the compilation key. A Triton kernel is recompiled for every distinct combination of tl.constexpr parameters (block sizes, flags such as TOPK_ENABLED/TOPP_ENABLED), dtypes and automatic specializations — by default Triton specializes integers equal to 1 or divisible by 16, and pointers aligned to 16 bytes. To avoid compiling one kernel per input size, kernels round dynamic sizes into buckets, typically the next power of 2:

\[ \texttt{BLOCK\_SIZE}(s) = \min\!\big(256,\ \mathrm{next\_pow2}(s + q)\big), \qquad q = 1 + k \]
  • \(s\): tokens scheduled in the step (measured exactly via /tokenize)
  • \(q\): queries per request (1 + drafts)
  • \(\mathrm{next\_pow2}(x)\): smallest power of 2 \(\ge x\)
  • each BLOCK_SIZE value = one binary in the cache

This is the actual key of DS's _prepare_dflash_inputs_kernel and of the equivalent DFlash2 kernel in GLM. Whatever isn't in the JIT cache compiles on first use — under traffic. And therein lies the danger the next section explains: in tensor parallel (ch. 38), one rank compiling leaves the other waiting in a collective (ch. 39). The recipes tackle this in two complementary ways: persisting the caches (so each shape compiles once per image, not once per boot) and warming up the shapes (so they compile before serving).

Three lessons jump out of the figure. (1) In GLM, with \(q = 8\), the smallest \(s\) (1) already gives 9 → bucket 16: “BLOCK 8 is unreachable”, says the script — there's no point warming it up. (2) GLM's ladder, 1 24 56 120 248, is almost exactly \(s_B = B - 8\): each rung lands on the upper edge of a bucket. (3) DS documents the key as next_pow2(s+6) (“+6 = 1 + num_speculative_tokens” with k=5), but the current lane uses k=6; the audit points out it should be +7. Do the math in the figure: with the ladder 1 6 20 45 100 200, +6 and +7 land in the same buckets {8, …, 256}, because the rungs sit far from the edges (our own calculation). The GLM script warns: “don't copy DSpark's next_pow2(s+6) ladder”.

Autotune, parity across ranks and the clocks that kill processes

Autotuning is JIT's more expensive sibling: for a given shape, the kernel runs with several tactics (tile, split-K, stages), each is timed, and the winner goes into a cache (a JSON file). Two rules follow. It has to happen before capture — timing requires synchronizing, and synchronizing inside a capture is forbidden. It has to be consistent across ranks — because in tensor parallel the ranks move in lockstep, like dance partners.

Modification_dense_exl3_warmup_autotune

Ch. 28 previewed the case. ExLlamaV3's cooperative autotuner tunes the first time it sees a “launch hash” and synchronizes the stream; inside the capture, that hung (py-spy 20260924T151053: capture → shared_experts → custom op → exl3_gemm → tune → cudaStreamSynchronize). Since the hash uses MIN(roundup_pow2(rows), 16) plus the module's dimensions, one warmup forward per module shape × rows {1, 2, 4, 8, 16} is enough — derived from the capture sizes ≤ 144, the threshold above which the forward switches to BF16 reconstruction. It costs seconds, before recording.

FlashInfer tunes too. In DS's default lane (Anemll), --enable-flashinfer-autotune is on, but the stock sparse-MLA warmup only covers tokens=16 (tactic=-1 for everything else), and the 1st request after a restart takes 6–10 s of TTFT because of cold autotune. In the Stage-C overlay (lane B), kernel_warmup.py extends autotune to DSpark's uniform-decode shapes (num_tokens = k+1, seq_lens 512 and 2048) with the consistency trick: only rank 0 tunes, does a broadcast_object of the JSON, and everyone loads the same tactics. The cache lives in VLLM_FLASHINFER_AUTOTUNE_CACHE_DIR (default VLLM_CACHE_ROOT/flashinfer_autotune_cache/<ws>/<hash>/autotune_configs.json). GLM went the opposite way: --no-enable-flashinfer-autotune, because the sparse-MLA and fused MoE autotune “kills rank 0 on SM121” and full autotune runs out of memory (OOM) on unified memory. Qwen lets autotune happen on the first (slow) request and stores the result in ~/.cache/vllm.

The likely reading (an interpretation of the mechanism): it is better for both ranks to tune from scratch, together, than for one to skip and the other to wait.

Parity: every rank must record the same tape

In TP, each rank executes exactly the same sequence of all-reduces (ch. 39), with the same sizes, in the same order — the scheduler broadcasts the same step plan to all of them. An all-reduce only finishes when everyone reaches it. If one rank has a graph for 5 tokens and the other pads to 8, the collectives stop matching and both wait forever. The GLM recipe sums it up: “a rank that captures different CUDA graphs hangs NCCL after PIECEWISE”. That is why the launcher forwards the same GLM53_*/EXL3_* to every container and reapplies the 25 overlays in the same order on both ranks (fail-closed), and why DS runs the --check of the enabled opt-in hotfixes (and always #117) on the worker node and then on the head node, before bringing up any rank, so that a patch that doesn't match the source blocks the boot without leaving the pair half-patched. (These explanations — and the mechanism of diverging collective sizes — are ours; the symptom is the documented one.)

In TensorFold — parity verified, not assumed

At startup, each rank packs its relevant settings (window, prompt grid, copy drafts, hyper-connection split, graph widths, profiling mode…) into a list of integers, all-gathers it and refuses to start if they differ (“the two ranks were started with different settings”) — fail-closed. And parity also applies to code that only measures: a startup profiling routine ran on rank 0 alone, but replayed graphs whose all-gathers need the peer (a hang); and the choice between RDMA and NCCL for an all-gather looked at the buffer's address, which differs between ranks, so one could wait on RDMA while the other waited on NCCL “forever”. Patch 0040 made both behave the same on both ranks: profiling on both, transport chosen by byte count alone.

The clocks

Three deadlines watch over the engine, and mixing them up leads to plenty of wrong diagnoses:

NCCL watchdog
The ProcessGroupNCCL watchdog kills the process if a collective doesn't finish within 600 s; no vLLM variable covers it. DS turns on the flight recorder (TORCH_NCCL_DUMP_ON_TIMEOUT=1, TORCH_NCCL_ENABLE_MONITORING=1) to leave per-rank evidence when the pair dies.
RPC deadline
VLLM_EXECUTE_MODEL_TIMEOUT_SECONDS: the deadline for the execute_model/sample_tokens RPC between the EngineCore and the workers: 300 s in stock vLLM, 1800 s in GLM and DS, because Triton/TileLang JIT in the middle of serving at TP=2 can exceed 300 s without being a real hang (DS #65/#87: it killed the EngineCore and looked like an NCCL hang).
readiness
READY_TIMEOUT: in GLM, 3600 s of waiting for /health, passed on as VLLM_ENGINE_READY_TIMEOUT_S (stock 600): loading and warming up an MoE of this size is slow.

The JIT scenario explains the combination: with the stock 300 s deadline, a 450 s compilation brings down the EngineCore; with 1800 s, it becomes “just” a latency spike. But nothing above 600 s survives, because the NCCL watchdog doesn't depend on vLLM: the long deadline is the seat belt, and persistent caches plus warmup are the real brakes. In Qwen, by the same parity reasoning, the container doesn't even have automatic restart: a rank restarted on its own hangs at NCCL init.

ParameterTF_ROCE_WAIT_S=20 · startup barrier (patch 0052)

TensorFold has a fourth clock. Its one-shot RDMA all-gather (ch. 39) has no NCCL watchdog: the GPU spins waiting for the peer's flag and, once TF_ROCE_WAIT_S passes (default 20 s, from 1 to 3,600), it poisons the runtime. On the first start of an image, each rank compiles its CUDA extensions on its own, and the two could drift “tens of seconds” apart — the first collective died (issue #6). The fix: until the engine is ready, each eager RDMA all-gather first waits for the peer on an NCCL barrier, which has no such limit; gathers captured in a graph never do (and keep the deadline from capture time — one more scalar frozen on the tape).

Update: CUDA graphs in TensorFold, one per window width

The GLM recipe gained a second version : the same EXL3 checkpoint, on the same two Sparks, served by a different engine — TensorFold v0.6.0 with 53 patches by Mia (the engine's ideas are in ch. 48; the full recipe, in ch. 49). The physics of this chapter holds just the same: decode is still launch-bound, and a graph still freezes pointers, shapes, branches and scalars. What changes is the unit that gets recorded. vLLM records “a step with \(T\) tokens” and, with GLM's attention backends, needs the batch to be uniform in order to record attention; TensorFold records “a verify window with \(R\) rows” and lets everything else vary as data on the device. They are two answers to the same contract, and they are worth seeing side by side.

The round: what becomes a graph

TensorFold's decode moves in rounds. In each one: (1) every stream proposes drafts — copies of earlier text first, then DFlash2; (2) one forward verifies the window [pending token, drafts…]; (3) each row is sampled with the request's key (the keyed sampling of ch. 48); (4) drafts are accepted up to the first disagreement; (5) the accepted rows are committed to the KV, the KDA state, the conv and the drafter's context. Step (2) is what becomes a graph, in two families of captures:

single stream
widths 1 to 8 (GRAPH_ROWS: the pending token + up to 7 DFlash2 drafts) and, with COPY_CODE=1, also 16 (TF_GLM_WIDE_GRAPHS=16), for long copies. Each width records one graph per KDA state parity (below) plus one per pool bucket past the dense limit. This is the PARALLEL=1 path.
batched (up to 4 streams)
BatchedVerify.capture() records one graph for every row count, from 1 to 32 (MAX_WINDOW = 32). This is the path of the recipe's default (PARALLEL=4) — even when only one request is active, because the recipe pins TF_GLM_MULTI_LONE=0 (the reason comes right below).

The trick: everything that varies becomes a table on the device

How can a single 12-row graph serve both “4 streams with 3 rows each” and “one stream with 8 and another with 4”, if each stream lives in a different stretch of the KV pool, sits at a different position and keeps its recurrent state in another slot? With the idea behind the E3 tier from section 6, taken to the extreme: every piece of metadata that changes from round to round becomes the contents of a device tensor, and the kernels read it instead of receiving it as a constant. Per row: position, the base of its extent in the pool, segment, whether the row is dense or sparse. Per segment: first row, number of rows, state slot, parity, conv slot, how many pools the last row can see. Before the replay, the host rewrites these tables in place — an asynchronous copy from a pinned buffer, with an event so it never overwrites a buffer still in flight — and hits play. The table's address is frozen on the tape; its contents are not. Hence the line in the code: “one graph a row count covers every mix”.

Compare the three modes in the figure. In vLLM, GLM's attention backends (sparse-MLA and KDA) only accept a FULL graph with a uniform batch; that is why adaptive-k brings every conversation down to the batch's smallest \(k'\). The capture list of the “live best” profile was designed to match \(c\,(k'+1)\) with no padding — the cost of the uniform batch shows up as cut drafts, not as empty spots. In batched TensorFold, each stream keeps the depth chosen by its own policy; the combined window is trimmed only when it exceeds 32 rows (one draft at a time, from the longest window) and lands exactly on a graph. In single-stream mode, a copy proposal of 9 to 15 rows is filled up to 16 by repeating the last draft: padding made of real drafts, verified like the others.

None of this is free. Every kernel that touches per-stream state — the KDA recurrence, sparse latent attention, the indexer's selection, the drafter's attention — needed a segmented version that reads the table and reproduces exactly the loop, tiles and head blocks of the single-stream version, because the engine's contract is that each stream gets the same bits it would get on its own. Whatever may pick a kernel by row count (the drafter's selector via cuBLAS, torch.topk) runs per stream, outside the batched part. That is the price of an engine written for one model family: vLLM accepts any architecture and lets each backend declare how much of itself can be recorded; TensorFold writes every kernel with capture already in mind.

Double parity: the state a draft must not dirty

Linear attention has a problem the KV cache doesn't: the recurrent state is overwritten at every token (ch. 14). If the window updated the state with drafts that were later rejected, it would be contaminated. TensorFold keeps each slot's KDA state in two parities: the window reads the current parity and writes the other one. At commit time, if the stream accepted everything, it is enough to flip the parity; if it accepted less, the state is recomputed from the kept rows only (a replay from per-row scratch). Parity is metadata too: on the batched path it lives in the segment table; on the single-stream path, it picks between the two graphs recorded for each width. This is TensorFold's alternative to keeping num_spec + 1 states, as vLLM does (ch. 35).

What stays frozen — and TensorFold's ways out

Device-side tables solve per-stream shapes and pointers, but the contract from section 2 still stands. Four cases show where it bites in an engine like this:

🏠

The graphs' home

The single-stream graphs recorded slot 0 and the pool's base 0 — the “home” — and the engine only uses them for a state with the same key. TF_GLM_MULTI_LONE=1 moved a lone stream home and gained +0.6–0.9%, but it evicted the prompts saved in the first rows: with three conversations taking turns (~4.5k tokens, 6 turns), 0% of prompts came from the cache, against 98.1% with 0. The recipe has pinned 0 since v1.3.1.

🔁

The collective that counts by itself

The one-shot RDMA all-gather (ch. 39) numbers each exchange. If that number were a kernel argument, the graph would repeat it forever — it is the “scalar passed by value” from section 2. The kernel's last block advances a device-side epoch, and the next replay reads the new number without the host. The figure below compares both versions.

Bucket on the tape, real bound on the device

Past the dense limit (2,051 tokens), sparse decode graphs are captured per pool bucket — a power of 2, at least 1,024 — because exact sizes fragmented memory at 128k. The kernels read on the device how many pools each row really sees and only score and select up to there (patch 0043): the shape comes from the bucket, the work comes from the real bound, with the same tokens chosen.

🔀

Fork and join inside the tape

Prefetching weights into L2 runs on a side stream, forked at each point in the layer and joined before the forward returns — a requirement for the branches of a captured graph to come back together. On its own, it took prose from 48.36 to 49.46 tok/s and code from 59.54 to 61.08 (one request, two boots each), with the same bits.

The collective deserves a closer look, because it combines three items from section 2's list — frozen scalar, no waiting on the CPU, order across ranks. The kernel does everything in one call: each block copies its piece of the local shard; the last block to arrive (an atomic arrival counter) rings a doorbell for the thread that talks to the network card; the NIC writes the shard to the peer and, afterwards, a 4-byte flag with the exchange number, on the same reliable queue (the flag can never become visible before the data); a waiter only reads once the peer's flag holds the number of this exchange; and the shards go to the output in rank order, so the sum that follows has the same bits on both ranks. Two alternating slots (seq & 1) are enough, because a peer is never more than one exchange ahead.

Measuring the cost curve without fooling yourself

Some of TensorFold's decisions use the measured cost of the windows: the joint allocation of rows across streams (TF_GLM_MULTI_DEPTH=joint, optional: the recipe stays on the default policy, where each stream follows its own policy) and the round profiler time windows of every width at startup, by replaying the graphs. Two lessons came out of that. The input matters: with a repeated token, every row routes to the same few experts of the MoE, and a 16-row window was timed at about half its real cost — patch 0048 switched to distinct, fixed ids. And timing is traffic like any other: the profiling routine that ran on rank 0 alone hung the pair (section 8). For a sense of the curve: at the time of patch 0011 (an engine predating several optimizations), verifying 1 to 8 rows cost 29.2 · 35.6 · 42.6 · 48.8 · 53.6 · 58.9 · 64.5 · 66.0 ms — 8 rows for ~2.3× the price of one (our arithmetic), the margin speculation exploits in a bandwidth-bound decode.

How much of this turns into speed? It can't be isolated. In sparkDash (prose, temperature 0), the TensorFold recipe reached 60.4 tok/s with 1 request and 108.8 aggregate with 4, measured with the GPU clocks capped at 2,200 MHz, against 36.1 and 75.3 for the vLLM recipe in a 2026-09-17 run — but with 4-bit dense weights, FP8 KV, RDMA transport, copy drafts, different dates and different settings. It is not an A/B test of CUDA graphs, nor a controlled A/B of engines: read it as an order of magnitude. The engineering lesson is that the second answer exists — and that it only works because the whole engine was written around it.

In the recipes: the timed boot and the decision board

Putting it all together, a recipe's boot is a pipeline: start processes and NCCL → load weights → profile run (measure activations and size the KV cache) → kernel warmups and autotune → compilation (if mode 3) → graph capture → API up (/health) → smoke test → shape warmup and canary → READY. One memory interaction deserves a spotlight: during the profile run, vLLM can estimate how much the graphs will take up and subtract it from the KV pool (VLLM_MEMORY_PROFILER_ESTIMATE_CUDAGRAPHS, on by default) — the full pool calculation is in PagedAttention & KV.

ParameterCG_ESTIMATE → VLLM_MEMORY_PROFILER_ESTIMATE_CUDAGRAPHS · patch_skip_cudagraph_profile.py

Default 1 (upstream). In the GLM kit, “the estimate is 2.43 GiB, while the captured graphs consume −0.19 GiB”: ~2.6 GiB of KV reserved and never used. The optional 0 keeps CUDA graphs on and removes only the deduction. With it, overlay #2, patch_skip_cudagraph_profile.py, cuts another source of waste: profile_cudagraph_memory() ran a test capture and threw the result away; gating it on the flag takes the KV profile from 18 to 7 s (with 1, the overlay stays inert). DS uses VLLM_MEMORY_PROFILER_ESTIMATE_CUDAGRAPHS=0 directly in its compose file.

Weights and initialization dominate. The graphs themselves are cheap (~7 s in Qwen, 11 s of capture + warmup in GLM); what's expensive is recompiling what could have been cached (30 s for the DFlash2 cubin) and measuring what won't be used (11 s for the test capture). A full GLM restart went from 259 s to 122 s. In the TensorFold version , loading ~80 GiB per Spark takes 2 to 6 min (~2.2 min with the worker's weights over NFS), and the first start of each image adds a few minutes of kernel compilation.

Four philosophies, one physics

GLM · everything explicit

  • Capture list built by hand by the launcher, adaptive-k aware.
  • FlashInfer autotune off; ExLlamaV3's autotune warmed up before capture.
  • Triton/TileLang/NV caches on the host; warmup + canary, fatal only for a degenerate engine.
  • Cuts boot waste (test capture skipped with the optional CG_ESTIMATE=0).

Qwen · minimal and stable

  • Mode 0, FULL_DECODE_ONLY, inferred 1–64 captures.
  • Breakable turned off in v0.30.
  • Autotune on the first request; in /tmp in v0.30 so the ranks don't diverge.
  • Compile/performance-mode experiments documented, not adopted.

DS · armored against JIT

  • Capture ceiling rounded to 8 (42 → 48).
  • Breakable pinned to 0 (when absent, Anemll turns it on).
  • All JIT caches on the HF volume; engine and shape warmup, non-fatal.
  • FlashInfer autotune on (in lane B, rank 0 tunes and broadcasts); long deadlines + flight recorder.

GLM·TF · one graph per window

  • Graphs per window width (1–8 and 16 per stream; 1–32 batched), metadata in device-side tables.
  • Decode always in a graph; prefill eager, never in the same forward.
  • Its own kernels via JIT, cached per image hash; the 1st start of each image compiles.
  • Settings compared across ranks at startup (fail-closed); loading in 2–6 min (~2.2 min with weights over NFS).

A playbook for your new model

  1. Start in eager mode. Validate the numerics with --enforce-eager (or cudagraph_mode: NONE); whatever breaks afterwards is the capture's fault.
  2. Turn on graphs and compute the capture sizes. Uniform decode = \(c(k+1)\) for \(c = 1..\)max_num_seqs; round the ceiling; in the log, no “Truncating” and a “FULL n/n” that covers your concurrency.
  3. Find out what your attention backend supports. If it only accepts uniform batches, policies that mix lengths (adaptive-k, prefill in a decode step) must respect that.
  4. Hunt down syncs in the hot path. In the CPU profile (py-spy, the torch profiler), per-layer aten::nonzero, .item() and .tolist() are red flags.
  5. Register extensions as custom ops with a correct fake impl and mutates_args, obeying the capture contract on the inside.
  6. Persist every JIT cache on a volume (Triton, TileLang, CuTeDSL, FlashInfer, NV ComputeCache, VLLM_CACHE_ROOT).
  7. Warm up the shapes. List the dynamic-key kernels, derive the ladder \(s_B = B - q\), cover long prefills and the sampler; add a canary.
  8. Autotune before capture, and consistently (rank 0 tunes and broadcasts, or everyone tunes together from scratch); or turn it off if it destabilizes the hardware.
  9. Same environment on every rank, and deadlines that accommodate legitimate compilations without hiding real hangs.
  10. Measure with A/B. A boot like DS's “G capture42” — change just one thing and compare — turns suspicion into a decision.
