In one decode step of Qwen3.8 served on two DGX Sparks, the two GPUs exchange about 106 messages. Each one is roughly 20 KB, smaller than a phone photo. Size is not the problem: each message costs something like 20–30 µs just to “pick up the phone”, and together they add up to a floor of 2–3 ms per step that does not shrink with batch size or quantization. This chapter is about that phone: the collectives that tensor parallel demands, the NCCL that executes them, the RDMA/RoCE that carries them over the 200 Gb/s cable, and the dozens of environment variables that decide whether everything works, runs slow, or hangs without an error. At the end, the new variant of the GLM recipe shows another design for the same cable: all-gathers instead of all-reduces and a hand-built one-shot RDMA transport for the small messages.

Why two GPUs need to talk

In the parallelism chapter you saw tensor parallel (TP) split each matrix across the GPUs. Now let's look at the math that is left after the split. Take a linear layer \(y = xW\) with \(W\) split by rows (row-parallel). Node A holds the top rows and node B the bottom ones. Each node receives the matching half of \(x\), which already arrives sliced from the preceding column-parallel layer.

\[ \begin{gathered} y \;=\; xW \;=\; \begin{bmatrix} x_A & x_B \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} W_A \\ W_B \end{bmatrix} \\[4pt] y \;=\; \underbrace{x_A W_A}_{\text{node A}} \;+\; \underbrace{x_B W_B}_{\text{node B}} \end{gathered} \]
  • \(x\): the activation of one token (a row vector of dimension \(d_{in}\)); \(x_A, x_B\) are its two halves
  • \(W_A, W_B\): the row blocks of \(W\) that each GPU holds
  • \(y\): the full output (dimension \(d_{out}\)), which both nodes need for the next layer

Neither node has all of \(y\): each one has a partial sum. To move on, both must add up the partials and end up with the same result. That operation is called all-reduce. The figure shows the sequence with toy numbers.

Notice two things. First, what crosses the cable is activations, not weights: the vector \(y\) in BF16, the size of the residual stream. Quantizing the weights to 4 bits does nothing to shrink this traffic. Second, the conversation is mandatory and synchronized: neither node can start the next layer before the all-reduce has finished on both.

How many conversations per step, and how big

The audit of the recipe counted ≈106 collectives per decode step (batch 1, MTP=3): 36 all-reduces in the out_proj of the GDN layers (Gated DeltaNet linear attention, whose output projection is row-parallel; see linear attention), 12 in the attention o_proj, 48 after the MoE, 1 logits all-gather, and 9 in the passes of the MTP drafter (the extra layer that proposes tokens; see MTP and EAGLE). The audit counted ≈108: 43 in wo_b (the output projection of DS-V4 attention, 8192 → 4096, row-parallel; see three models), 43 in the MoE, 6 in the draft, 2 in embeddings, 2 logits all-gathers, and 12 in DSpark's Markov loop, executed serially: the drafter samples its tokens one by one with a Markov head, and each step needs one all-reduce and one all-gather (see DFlash and DSpark). For (45 layers, hidden 4096), the repository only records “one all-reduce per MoE layer” and estimates 45 all-reduces of 8 MiB in a 1024-token prefill step (≈378 MB, 15–20 ms on the bus). Since the attention o_proj (in the 34 KDA layers and the 11 MLA ones) is also row-parallel, we count 2 per layer, ≈90 per decode step (our interpretation, the same as in the parallelism chapter); the repository's 378 MB figure covers only the collective of the MLP/MoE block.

The size of each message changes a lot between phases. In decode it is tiny: on Qwen, 4 tokens (1 verified + 3 drafts) × 2560 × 2 B = 20 KB. In prefill it is huge: an 8192-token chunk gives 8192 × 2560 × 2 B = 42 MB per all-reduce on Qwen, and on DeepSeek a 1024-token chunk gives [1024 × 4096] in BF16 = 8 MiB. This three-orders-of-magnitude gap drives the whole chapter.

In the TensorFold variant: the same two exchanges, a different collective

The second version of the GLM recipe, served by the TensorFold engine (ch. 48), confirms the count of 2 per layer: the code speaks of “the two all-gathers a layer”, one exchange after the attention block and another after the MLP/MoE, or 90 per decode round across the 45 layers, plus the sampling exchanges and rank 0's control message. Only they are not all-reduces. Each rank sends its whole fp32 partial, \([R, 4096]\) for a window of \(R\) rows, and both add the partials in the same order. That is 16 KiB per row, twice the 8 KiB of a BF16 row, in a regime where, as section 3 will show, latency is what counts. The update section does the math.

Why this matters

In decode, the toll depends on the number of messages; in prefill, on the cable's bandwidth. The recipes' NCCL_* variables pick the road (RDMA, not TCP), the address (the RDMA card and the port identifier, which we will meet later as HCA and GID), and steer clear of detours (MTU, the card's PCIe functions, protocols). Getting one of them wrong costs performance or hangs the boot.

The vocabulary of collectives

A collective is a communication operation in which all participants in a group enter together and nobody finishes before everyone has done their part. Each participant is a rank. In the recipes, that means one process per GPU and one GPU per Spark: rank 0 on the head, rank 1 on the worker. The number of ranks is the world size \(p\). Six operations cover almost everything:

Deep dive: all-reduce = reduce-scatter + all-gather

Split the buffer into \(p\) chunks. In the reduce-scatter, rank \(r\) ends up owning the complete sum of chunk \(r\), and each rank sends \(\frac{p-1}{p}n\) bytes. In the all-gather, each owner distributes its summed chunk, with another \(\frac{p-1}{p}n\) bytes. The total, \(2\frac{p-1}{p}n\), is the theoretical minimum for an all-reduce, which is why the ring is “bandwidth-optimal”.

In TensorFold: the identity built by hand

The TensorFold variant's engine never calls all-reduce. It all-gathers the partials and adds them in its own kernel, rank 0 first, so the arithmetic stays in the engine and the library only moves bytes (the why is in ch. 48). In prefill, where bytes matter, it rebuilds the identity above with its own hands: each rank sends the peer only the partial of the peer's rows, with ncclSend/ncclRecv (a reduce-scatter), adds and processes its own rows, and sends back the finished rows (an all-gather), with the compute in the middle. The details are in the update section.

In the recipe: EP that doesn't talk

The recipe passes --enable-expert-parallel --all2all-backend allgather_reducescatter. The audit showed that the all-to-all backend is never invoked (use_all2all_kernels is false with DP=1 and PCP=1). Tokens are replicated by TP, each rank computes its 256 local experts, and the result goes through the normal all-reduce. Here, EP only changes which weights each GPU holds, not the communication (details in parallelism).

Ring, tree, and the alpha-beta model

The ring: everyone passes to their neighbor

Sending everything to a “leader” that sums and sends it back overloads the leader. In the ring, the \(p\) ranks form a circle, the buffer is cut into \(p\) chunks, and at each step every rank sends one chunk to its right-hand neighbor while receiving another from the left: \(p-1\) reduce-scatter steps (the receiver adds) and \(p-1\) all-gather steps (the receiver copies). Everyone works at the same time.

The α-β model: latency versus bandwidth

The cost of a message has two terms: a fixed one, α (latency: waking up the NIC, setting up descriptors, crossing the cable, signaling completion), and a per-byte one, 1/β (bandwidth). This is the α-β model:

\[ \begin{gathered} T(n) \;=\; \alpha + \frac{n}{\beta} \\[6pt] T_{\text{ring}}(n,p) \;=\; 2(p-1)\,\alpha \;+\; 2\,\frac{p-1}{p}\,\frac{n}{\beta} \end{gathered} \]
  • \(n\): bytes in the buffer to reduce (e.g., 20 KB in decode, 8 MiB in a DS prefill chunk)
  • \(\alpha\): latency per communication step (µs), independent of size
  • \(\beta\): effective link bandwidth (GB/s)
  • \(p\): number of ranks; with \(p=2\), \(T_{\text{ring}} = 2\alpha + n/\beta\)

For an isolated message, the two terms are equal at \(n_{1/2} = \alpha\beta\). In the ring with \(p\) ranks, equating \(2(p-1)\alpha\) with \(2\frac{p-1}{p}\frac{n}{\beta}\) gives \(n_{1/2} \approx p\,\alpha\beta\). Below that point the message is latency-bound; above it, bandwidth-bound. With \(p=2\) and the figure's illustrative values (α ≈ 12 µs per step, β ≈ 11 GB/s), \(n_{1/2}\) ≈ 260 KB. Decode at 1 sequence sits well below that: 20 KB on Qwen and 7 tokens × 4096 × 2 B ≈ 57 KB on DS. At 6 sequences (344 KB), the DS collectives already fall into the mixed zone of the illustrative model. With DS's real fixed cost, however (30–50 µs per collective, without GPUDirect), \(n_{1/2}\) ≈ 30–50 µs × 11 GB/s ≈ 330–550 KB, which is why the audit considers them “small and latency-bound”, for which MTU and PF width “barely matter”. Prefill (8–42 MB) sits far above: only bandwidth matters.

The per-step floor

Multiply the cost of one small collective by how many there are and you get a fixed floor per decode step. On Qwen, 106 × 20–30 µs ≈ 2–3 ms/step (6–8% of a ~40 ms step). On DeepSeek, whose audit saw no GPUDirect RDMA (the NIC accessing GPU memory directly, next section), each collective costs 30–50 µs because it goes through a buffer on the host: 108 × 30–50 µs ≈ 3–5 ms/step (5–8% of a 58–65 ms step at 1 sequence; 2–3% of a ~150 ms step at 6). These are analytical estimates from the audits. The floor does not drop with batch: more sequences fatten the messages without increasing their number, and small messages cost almost nothing but α.

This explains DeepSeek's TP=3: with 3 ranks the ring has 4 steps instead of 2 (≈2× the latency, the audit estimates), and ≈13 ms of fixed cost per step wiped out the gain from reading 1/3 fewer weights per rank (TP=3 and TP=4).

Two knobs for the same floor

If the floor is “number of messages × α”, there are two ways to lower it: send fewer messages or shorten each one's α. Batch and quantization touch neither. The variant of the GLM recipe turned the second knob: with its own RDMA transport, a 16 KiB all-gather dropped from 45 µs (NCCL) to 11 µs, measured in the README, and decode rose 6%. The full math is in the update section.

Deep dive: tree, algbw, and busbw

The alternative to the ring is the tree (NCCL uses a “double binary tree”). It trades the \(2(p-1)\) steps for ≈\(2\log_2 p\) and wins on latency when \(p\) is large. With \(p=2\), ring and tree are the same exchange, which is why the Qwen audit only suggested pinning NCCL_ALGO=Ring. When reading nccl-tests output, distinguish algbw = \(n/T\) from busbw = algbw × \(2\frac{p-1}{p}\). The busbw is comparable to the cable's bandwidth; for \(p=2\), the two coincide.

RDMA: the network that writes straight into the other machine's memory

The TCP path and the RDMA shortcut

With TCP, sending a buffer goes through a system call, a copy into the kernel, the TCP/IP stack, the driver, and the NIC; on the other side, an interrupt, the stack, and another copy. The CPU touches every byte. RDMA (Remote Direct Memory Access) skips all of that: the process registers a memory region with the NIC, and from then on node A's NIC writes directly into node B's registered memory, with no kernel on the hot path (kernel bypass), no intermediate copy (zero-copy), and no remote CPU (one-sided).

Verbs: queue pairs, registered memory, and the state machine

The RDMA API is called verbs (libibverbs). Four concepts show up in the recipes' errors:

MR (memory region)
a registered region: the kernel pins the pages (they cannot be swapped out or moved) and returns keys (lkey/rkey) that authorize the NIC. Pinning requires memlock, hence the containers' --ulimit memlock=-1.
QP (queue pair)
the “socket” of RDMA: one send queue and one receive queue, where requests are posted (RDMA WRITE/READ, SEND/RECV)
CQ (completion queue)
where the NIC reports that a request has finished; NCCL polls it
QP states
RESET → INIT → RTR (ready to receive) → RTS (ready to send). The transition to RTR, done with ibv_modify_qp, receives the peer's address (the GID, in the next section). This is where the recipes' ibv_modify_qp … 61 and … 110 Connection timed out errors come from.

InfiniBand, RoCE v1, and RoCE v2

RDMA was born in InfiniBand (IB): a network of its own, with switches, a subnet manager that hands out local addresses (LIDs), and credit-based flow control that never drops packets. RoCE (RDMA over Converged Ethernet) carries the same IB transport headers (the BTH) inside Ethernet frames. In RoCE v1 they go directly over Ethernet, which limits the reach to layer 2 (it does not route). In RoCE v2 they go inside UDP/IP (port 4791): the packet is routable and the RDMA address is now derived from the IP. The Sparks' ConnectX-7 speaks Ethernet, with one IP subnet per cable: the recipes use RoCE v2 (NCCL_IB_ROCE_VERSION_NUM=2). NCCL calls this transport “IB” because it uses verbs in both cases (NCCL_NET=IB).

RoCE assumes an Ethernet that almost never drops packets: when a buffer fills up, the NIC emits pause frames. The DeepSeek audit found 22,084 pause frames and 13,071 rx_out_of_buffer on the worker, probably from loading weights over NFS on the same cable; these pauses are global and hold back NCCL as well.

GPUDirect RDMA and what was observed on the GB10

In datacenter servers, GPUDirect RDMA (GDR) lets the NIC DMA directly into GPU memory, via the nvidia_peermem module or via DMA-BUF. Without one of these paths, the NIC cannot register GPU memory as an MR, and NCCL falls back to an intermediate buffer on the host. In the log, GDR shows up as via NET/IB/0/GDRDMA. On the GB10 stack of a DS recipe contributor (driver 580.173.02), the container reported CU_DEVICE_ATTRIBUTE_DMA_BUF_SUPPORTED=0 and the transport showed up as via NET/IB/x, without /GDRDMA: the collectives went through a buffer on the host. The source itself warns that this holds for that stack and says nothing about GDR on the GB10 in general; other stacks may differ. The Qwen audit found nvidia_peermem missing, but says that, from the outside, you cannot tell whether there is staging through the host: check for GDRDMA/dmabuf in the transport line of the log.

ParametersNCCL_NET_GDR_LEVEL · NCCL_NET_GDR_READ · NCCL_DMABUF_ENABLE

The recipe leaves all three unset (passthrough; commented-out examples: SYS, 1, 0). NCCL_NET_GDR_LEVEL sets the maximum topological distance at which GDR is used (SYS = any), NCCL_NET_GDR_READ also enables GDR on the send side, and NCCL_DMABUF_ENABLE controls registration via DMA-BUF (0 turns off the probing where it misbehaves). No effect was demonstrated on the measured stack. The audit suggested testing NCCL_DMABUF_ENABLE=1 + NCCL_NET_GDR_LEVEL=SYS (not adopted). For the DS audit, the detour through the host helps explain the 30–50 µs per collective.

No GPUDirect, but unified memory

The RDMA transport the TensorFold variant adopted, from the b12x project, starts from the same diagnosis: according to b12x, the DGX Spark stack has no GPUDirect RDMA (neither DMA-BUF nor nvidia_peermem), so NCCL keeps going through the host there. And it turns that into an advantage: since the GB10's GPU is integrated, pinned host memory is addressable by GPU kernels at full bandwidth, and the NIC registers it as an MR with a plain ibv_reg_mr. If the “host buffer” is the same LPDDR5X, the GPU can write its shard straight into the region the NIC will send and read the peer's where the NIC left it, in a single kernel call. The copies remain (the shard is staged on the way out and copied back on the way in); what goes away is the handoff: “no NCCL proxy handoff”, says the code, and nothing on the receive side involves the CPU (the send side uses the transport's own proxy thread, which only posts the WRITEs). See the update section.

ConnectX-7, HCAs, GIDs, and MTU

One port, two PFs: the GB10's “socket-direct”

In RDMA, the card is called an HCA (Host Channel Adapter). In Linux, each function shows up as an RDMA device (rocep1s0f1, under /sys/class/infiniband) and as an Ethernet interface (enp1s0f1np1); ibdev2netdev shows the pair. The names follow the PCI address: p1s0f1 = bus 1, slot 0, function 1; the P2 in roceP2p1s0f1 is PCI domain 0002.

The DeepSeek audit found a peculiar topology: one ConnectX-7 ASIC, two physical QSFP ports, and two PCIe functions (PFs) per port (0000:01:00.x and 0002:01:00.x), all with the same sys_image_guid. Each PF is PCIe Gen5 ×4, ≈15.7 GB/s ≈ 126 Gb/s raw. The DS recipe's .env describes the same arrangement as two controllers of “~100G” each and says that, with a single HCA, the link runs at “half port”: 126 is the raw PCIe rate, ~100 is the practical one. A single PF cannot fill the 200 Gb/s port. This socket-direct arrangement explains the recipes' “dual-HCA”: listing both PFs of the port in NCCL_IB_HCA. (The Qwen audit calls the second PF a “second CX7 link”; based on the DS analysis, it is, in our interpretation, the second PF of the same port.)

There is also crossed cabling: in the GLM kit and in Qwen's live cluster, the cable connects the head's f1 port to the worker's f0. That is why every recipe has per-node variables: HEAD_CX7_IF/WORKER_CX7_IF (GLM), IFACE/WORKER_IFACE (Qwen), NCCL_*/WORKER_NCCL_* (DS).

Choosing the HCA: the grammar of NCCL_IB_HCA

NCCL_IB_HCA accepts a comma-separated list of name[:port]. By default each item is a prefix (roce matches all of them). A leading = asks for an exact match and a leading ^ excludes. NCCL keeps only the first 32 entries, truncates names at 63 bytes, and only considers ACTIVE ports with an Ethernet or IB link (up to MAX_IB_DEVS=32). Qwen uses =rocep1s0f0 (one device, exact); DS recommends =rocep1s0f0,roceP2p1s0f0 (both PFs). And never list a port cabled to another cluster: NCCL hangs at init with no useful message.

With two PFs, two variables decide how NCCL combines them. NCCL_IB_MERGE_NICS (default 1) allows merging compatible ports into one logical NIC. NCCL_CROSS_NIC says whether a channel may use the NIC with index 0 on one node and the one with index 1 on the other: 0 requires alignment, 1 allows crossing, 2 (default) tries to align but accepts crossing. GLM pins each port (MERGE_NICS=0, CROSS_NIC=0 in TP2); DS uses CROSS_NIC=1 and leaves merging at the default. On 3 nodes merging “is wrong”, because each port goes to a different neighbor (TP=3).

The GID: the RDMA address derived from the IP

In RoCE v2, the peer is addressed by a GID (Global Identifier), a 128-bit number in IPv6 format. Each port has a GID table that the kernel fills in from the interface's addresses. Each address produces two entries, one of type RoCE v1 and one of type RoCE v2. An IPv4 address appears in IPv4-mapped format: ::ffff:10.0.22.1, which ends in ffff:0a00:1601 (the ffff:aabb:ccdd suffix that the DS resolver compares). On an interface with only the link-local and one IPv4, the typical table is 0 = fe80 v1, 1 = fe80 v2, 2 = IPv4 v1, and 3 = IPv4 v2. That is where the templates' “3” comes from. Each extra address shifts the table, and the order depends on when the addresses came up: the correct index varies per node and can change after a reboot.

Each recipe handles the GID its own way:

GLM · per-node pin + preflight

  • NCCL_IB_GID_INDEX=3 in the template, with HEAD_GID/WORKER_GID per rank (“this kit needs head=4, worker=3”) and NCCL_IB_ROCE_VERSION_NUM=2.
  • The preflight reads …/ports/1/gids/N on every listed HCA; if it is empty, it prints tables 0–7 with their type and aborts, because “an all-zero entry kills the rank ~60 s later (ibv_modify_qp errno 61)”.

Qwen · fixed pin + hint

  • IB_GID_INDEX=3 becomes NCCL_IB_GID_INDEX. The README says: “if 3 doesn't come up, try 5”. The audit suggests NCCL_IB_ROCE_VERSION_NUM=2.
  • Wrong GID = silent hang at init, ~1 min after startup.

DS · automatic resolver, no pin

  • NCCL_IB_GID_AUTO=1: the launcher mirrors the grammar of NCCL_IB_HCA and the candidates of ncclIbInit and requires, on each member, a RoCE v2 GID with its own IPv4; it fails closed (72 checks in test-nccl-ib-hca-gid-resolve.sh).
  • It leaves NCCL_IB_GID_INDEX unset: NCCL picks per HCA (NCCL_IB_ROCE_VERSION_NUM=2, NCCL_IB_ADDR_FAMILY=AF_INET, IPv4-mapped GIDs). A global pin “could hang init” with dual-HCA, since each PF may have a different index; the manual pin (e.g., 3 / 6, with WORKER_/WORKER2_) is only for AUTO=0.

GLM·TF · found per node, at every start

  • nodes.sh follows the route to the peer (ip route get): it gives the interface and the source IPv4; sysfs gives the HCA; the right entry is the one that reads ::ffff:a.b.c.d and is of type RoCE v2. The index it finds becomes that rank's NCCL_IB_GID_INDEX (“an all-zero or v1 entry makes NCCL fail about a minute in”).
  • The second HCA joins as a rail only if it is up, on the link's subnet, and has the v2 GID at the same index, and only if both ends have the same number of rails (v1.3.2's rule; v1.3.3 also takes the cabled port's PCIe twin by name, and v1.10 pings every second rail before using it: see the update section). If the route to WORKER leaves through a port without RoCE (a LAN address), the script looks on its own for a directly reachable CX7 address on the worker (since v1.3.1, #9); if there is none, it fails early and asks for FABRIC_PEER.
Pitfall: empty is not unset

To NCCL, NCCL_IB_GID_INDEX="" means index 0, the fe80 link-local, which is useless for reaching the peer. A variable that is defined but empty also masks /etc/nccl.conf (loaded with overwrite=0). Since the compose file always defines the key, the DS entrypoint runs unset on empty NCCL variables before exec vllm. NCCL_IB_GID_MATCH_IP (empty in DS; e.g., 10.0.22.1, with a WORKER_ variant) is only needed if the RoCE IP is not the interface's primary IPv4.

MTU, NIC rings, and the second PF

RoCE derives its MTU from Ethernet: with 1500 bytes, active_mtu stays at 1024; 4096 requires Ethernet ≥ 4200, and the recipes use 9000 on both PFs of both nodes. The DS audit also found the NIC rings at 1024/1024 (max 8192) and rx_out_of_buffer on the worker, hence the ethtool -G <port> rx 8192 tx 8192. And the second PFs had IPs in the same /24 as the first ones: since Linux answers ARP for any local address on any interface (“ARP flux”), the RTT through the second PF was 0.70 ms, versus 0.33 ms. The fix is a different subnet, like the 10.0.122.1 ↔ 10.0.122.2 pair from the Qwen audit.

Three networks and the rendezvous: bootstrap, control, and data

The three planes

A pair of Sparks running vLLM uses three logical “networks”, even if they all go over the same cable:

  1. Bootstrap (TCP). The rendezvous of torch.distributed in a TCPStore at MASTER_ADDR:MASTER_PORT. Also the Gloo group (CPU collectives, barriers, metadata) on the GLOO_SOCKET_IFNAME interface, and the NCCL bootstrap, which exchanges the unique id and connection data over sockets on the NCCL_SOCKET_IFNAME interface. TP_SOCKET_IFNAME is the interface for torch's TensorPipe (our interpretation: TensorPipe serves torch RPC; vLLM probably does not depend on it on the hot path).
  2. vLLM control (ZMQ + shared memory). At every step, the head's EngineCore sends the SchedulerOutput to the workers: through shared memory to local readers and through ZMQ (TCP) to the remote worker, at the VLLM_HOST_IP address. Measured RTT: ≈0.33 ms (DS), 0.15–0.4 ms (Qwen). Async scheduling hides this time.
  3. Data (RDMA). The forward pass's all-reduces, through NCCL, via verbs on the NCCL_IB_HCA HCA, with the chosen GID.

This explains a GLM gotcha: “NCCL cannot use the 10.0.0.x aliases; without these pins ncclCommInitRank hangs”. The kits use 10.0.0.1/32 and 10.0.0.2 as loopback aliases routed through the CX7, which carry the rendezvous and ZMQ TCP traffic. But NCCL picks interfaces by name and excludes lo by default: it needs the real interface name (enp1s0f1np1) and the RDMA device (rocep1s0f1). The explanation is ours; the symptom is the recipe's.

In TensorFold, control rides the data wire

Without vLLM, Gloo, ZMQ, and the shared-memory queue disappear. The bootstrap is a TCPStore created by rank 0 on port 29551, through which the ncclUniqueId passes (key tf_nccl_uid); NCCL is loaded through ctypes, with no torch.distributed process groups. Control travels over the data plane: every iteration, rank 0 sends one operations message (an int32 vector) in two all-gathers, the length and then the values, and rank 1 applies the same operations in the same order. When idle, rank 1 waits on a doorbell, a key in the TCPStore itself, not inside an all-gather. TensorFold's RUNBOOK warns that neither the store nor the link is authenticated: keep both on the private cable.

Rendezvous and roles

In multi-node vLLM without Ray, each node runs a container with the same vllm serve, changing only a few arguments: --nnodes 2 (how many nodes; 3 or 4 in TP3/TP4), --node-rank (0 on the head, 1 on the worker), --master-addr/--master-port (rank 0's TCPStore), and --headless, which brings up the worker without an API server or scheduler, just to receive the plan and take part in the collectives. The head runs the API server (:8888), the EngineCore (scheduler), and worker rank 0. The executor is always --distributed-executor-backend mp (multiprocessing, one process per GPU); Ray would require a Ray cluster inside containers, and no recipe uses it. On Qwen, only mp enables async scheduling with MTP and creates the PLE offload process, the N-gram table that can be moved into a CPU process (see N-gram memory and MTP and Inside vLLM).

The values change little between recipes. --master-addr is the head's IP on the CX7 link (10.0.0.1 in the kit; GLM requires it to be assigned locally) and --master-port is 29521 (GLM), 50000 (Qwen), or 25000 (DS), free and identical on both nodes (GLM checks it in the preflight). DS injects NODE_RANK and HEADLESS per node (env -u NODE_RANK -u HEADLESS), so that the same .env serves both, and its worker healthcheck exits 0. VLLM_HOST_IP is the IP of each node advertised to ZMQ (WORKER_VLLM_HOST_IP in DS), so the worker does not bind to the head's IP. WORKER_IP (WORKER_HOST in DS) is only the launcher's SSH/rsync target.

Startup order and restarts

The worker comes up first and waits for the TCPStore; then the head comes up (Qwen waits 15 s). In DS, restarting the head in the middle of loading used to break the handshake (issue #38). A rank restarted on its own hangs at NCCL init, because its old peer is gone: the Qwen audit advises against --restart unless-stopped for 2-node TP and prefers a supervisor that runs stop && start on both. DS does the opposite: its compose file uses restart: unless-stopped by default (DSPARK_RESTART_POLICY, issues #38/#39) to bring back a worker that crashed, while the head does not restart in the middle of loading. It is a trade-off: automatic recovery versus the risk of an orphan rank stuck at init. The passwordless head→worker SSH (BatchMode=yes) belongs to the launcher; NCCL does not use SSH.

On 3 nodes, each CX7 cable has its own /24 and none of them reaches every rank, but Gloo accepts a single interface name. That is why GLM (SOCKET_IFNAME or HEAD_/WORKER_/WORKER2_SOCKET_IFNAME, with the *_HOST_IP on that interface) and DS (TP3_BOOTSTRAP_IFNAME=enP7s7) move Gloo, the NCCL sockets, and the TCPStore to the management LAN, never to lo (bind on 127.0.0.1); the data stays on RDMA (see TP=3 and TP=4).

NCCL inside out: channels, protocols, buffers, and knobs

The communicator

NCCL (NVIDIA Collective Communications Library) implements each collective as a CUDA kernel that also drives the transports: NVLink/P2P and shared memory within the node, and the network between nodes (“IB” verbs or sockets). On the network transport, a proxy thread on the CPU posts the RDMA requests on the kernel's behalf. Before the first collective comes ncclCommInitRank: detect the topology, choose NICs, build rings and trees, allocate buffers, and connect QPs. This is the phase that hangs with a wrong HCA or GID, and the one that NCCL_GIN_ENABLE=0 sped up in DS: comm-init from ~2 min → ~13 s (measured at ~97% memory pressure, bandwidth unchanged). GIN (GPU-initiated networking) is the GPU launching network operations itself; with 0, init follows the CPU-driven path. The variable is not listed on the NCCL 2.32.3 variables page consulted for the inventory, but the effect was measured.

Channels: several conveyor belts in parallel

A channel is an independent instance of the ring (or tree), executed by one CUDA block. The message is split across channels that run in parallel: more channels mean more bandwidth (up to the link's limit), but also more SMs occupied and more buffers. NCCL_MIN_NCHANNELS/NCCL_MAX_NCHANNELS bound the choice. GLM exposes NCCL_NCHANNELS: empty lets NCCL decide; an integer pins MIN=MAX on both ranks (reference “Entrpi 2× Spark: 8”). In TP3, GLM pins 8 even when it is empty, to limit pinned memory.

Protocols: Simple, LL, and LL128

How does the receiver know the data has arrived? In the Simple protocol, the sender writes a large block, issues a memory fence, and only then signals. It uses the full bandwidth, but the synchronization costs latency. In LL (low latency), each 8-byte write carries 4 bytes of data and 4 of flag; the receiver polls the flag, with no fence. Minimal latency, but half of the bytes are flags. LL128 does the same with 128-byte lines (120 of data, 8 of flag, ≈94%), but requires the hardware to deliver 128 bytes atomically and in order, a guarantee typical of NVLink. It also suggested NCCL_IB_QPS_PER_CONNECTION=2..4 with NCCL_IB_SPLIT_DATA_ON_QPS=0. More QPs spread the traffic over several queues (with SPLIT=0, whole messages alternate between QPs instead of being split), which “helps prefill, which is bandwidth-bound, and is neutral for decode”. None of this was adopted; the combined estimate with the other knobs is +3–8% on decode. in TP3 does the opposite: NCCL_PROTO=^LL128 (excludes LL128) to save memory.

What the TensorFold variant measured on these knobs

For the gathers that stay on NCCL, the TensorFold variant's nodes.sh pins only the channels: NCCL_MIN_NCHANNELS=NCCL_MAX_NCHANNELS=4. Measured between the Sparks, a “decode-sized” all-gather takes 32 µs, against 80 µs with the defaults. The comment goes on: “NCCL's other knobs (protocols, buffer sizes, QPs, NCCL_NET=IB and the like) were no better, and some of them kept NCCL on one rail”. They belong to the same family as the ones the Qwen audit suggested without adopting, and the result is one more reminder to measure your own fabric. These 32 µs and the README's 45 µs (16 KiB) were not reconciled: size and configuration may differ.

Buffers and pinned memory

Each channel keeps buffers per peer and per protocol (NCCL_BUFFSIZE for Simple, default 4 MiB; NCCL_LL128_BUFFSIZE for LL128). Without GPUDirect, the network buffers live in pinned host memory, which on the GB10 comes out of the same LPDDR5X pool as the KV cache and the weights. In GLM's TP3, with many peers and channels, the math would reach “512 connections × 9 MiB”; that is why it pins NCCL_BUFFSIZE=1048576, NCCL_LL128_BUFFSIZE=262144, NCCL_PROTO=^LL128, and 8 channels. Our interpretation: this would also explain DS's “dual-HCA without merging cost KV buffer”, since more logical NICs bring more connections and more pinned memory.

Inside the node: the shared-memory bulletin board

Not every wait is a network wait. Inside the head, the EngineCore and worker rank 0 are different processes. At every step, the scheduler's plan goes from one to the other through a shared-memory queue (MessageQueue in shm_broadcast.py): a ring buffer of slots, each with a “written” flag and a “read” flag per reader. The writer only reuses a slot that everyone has read; the reader only reads once the written flag is set; a memory fence makes the flags visible across processes. The remote worker receives the plan over ZMQ.

The delicate part is how the reader waits. The SpinCondition class does busy-waiting (it checks the flag in a loop, with sched_yield) for busy_loop_s after the last message. Then it sleeps on a notification socket (PUB/SUB) until the writer signals. Spinning responds fast but burns CPU; sleeping saves CPU, but waking up costs latency.

With 2 ms or 16 ms, the reader sleeps between steps and only spins right after each message. The recipes' values:

Analogy: the receptionist and the doorbell

The reader is a receptionist waiting for deliveries. With busy-waiting, they stand there watching the door: they answer instantly, but it is tiring. When sleeping, they rest until the doorbell rings, but it takes them a moment to get up. With the 1 s window, deliveries arrive every 50–70 ms, so they never get to sit down, and on the GB10 their “fatigue” (the Grace cores) heats up the same chip as the GPU. The lost wakeup is the doorbell ringing at the exact instant they are sitting down: they don't hear it and doze off with the package at the door. The recheck is a 5 s alarm clock.

The recipes landed on different values: DS chose 2 ms, prioritizing CPU and power; GLM measured 2 ms as −1.68% decode and settled on 16 ms. Setups and workloads differ, and each recipe measured its own, as with any knob in this chapter.

Spin or sleep, in two places

TensorFold faces the same dilemma twice. The proxy thread of its RDMA transport spins while there are exchanges, because inside a CUDA graph the gaps between collectives are sub-millisecond; after 20 million polls without a doorbell (ROCE_IDLE_SPINS), it starts asking for a 20 µs nanosleep between polls. And an idle rank 1 does not even spin: it waits for the TCPStore doorbell. The README's stated result: “an idle server holds no GPU or CPU core”. It is the same trade between fast response and burned CPU as in the figure above, resolved with spinning while work flows and a short sleep when it stops.

When it hangs: watchdog, flight recorder, and diagnostics

A collective is a scheduled meeting: if one rank does not show up, the other waits inside the NCCL kernel, with the GPU busy and no error whatsoever. Documented causes: JIT in the middle of serving (Triton, TileLang, CuTeDSL) holds a rank for minutes; ranks with different configurations capture different graphs and “hang NCCL after PIECEWISE” (GLM); different autotune caches between the nodes led Qwen's TP2 into a deadlock (lane v0.30); a wrong HCA or GID hangs init; a rank restarted alone cannot find its peer. The compilation-related ones are covered in CUDA graphs and warmup.

Two watchmen cap these waits. The ProcessGroupNCCL watchdog kills the process if a collective does not finish within 600 s. VLLM_EXECUTE_MODEL_TIMEOUT_SECONDS (default 300 s) is the deadline of the mp executor's RPC (execute_model/sample_tokens; in DS, the one covered is sample_tokens). GLM and DS raise it to 1800 s, because a legitimate JIT can take longer than 300 s, but this does not extend the 600 s watchdog.

Update: TensorFold's network and the one-shot all-gather

The GLM recipe gained a second version : the same EXL3 checkpoint, on the same two Sparks, linked by the same QSFP cable, served by a different engine, TensorFold v0.6.0 with 53 patches by Mia (the engine's ideas are in ch. 48; the step-by-step recipe, in ch. 49). Everything this chapter taught still holds: the α-β model, RoCE v2, the port's two PFs, the GID that must be the v2 entry with the node's IPv4. What changes are four design decisions, and each one answers a question from this chapter differently: which collective to use, which transport carries the small messages, where control goes, and how the two ranks keep showing up to the same meeting.

All-gather instead of all-reduce: more bytes, one α fewer

TensorFold does no all-reduce. After the attention block and after the MLP/MoE block, each rank holds an fp32 partial \([R, 4096]\), where \(R\) is the number of rows in the verify window (the pending token plus the drafts). An all-gather delivers both partials to both ranks, and each adds them in its own kernel, rank 0 first. The reason is exactness, not networking: with the sum outside the library, NCCL only moves bytes, and swapping the transport (NCCL or the custom RDMA coming next) does not change a single bit of the reply. The network math, however, does change, and it is worth doing it with the model from section 3.

\[ \begin{gathered} T_{\text{AR}} \;=\; 2\alpha + \frac{n_{\text{bf16}}}{\beta}, \qquad n_{\text{bf16}} = R \cdot 4096 \cdot 2\ \text{B} = R \cdot 8\ \text{KiB} \\[6pt] T_{\text{AG}} \;=\; \alpha + \frac{n_{\text{fp32}}}{\beta}, \qquad n_{\text{fp32}} = R \cdot 4096 \cdot 4\ \text{B} = R \cdot 16\ \text{KiB} \end{gathered} \]
  • \(T_{\text{AR}}\): ring all-reduce with \(p = 2\) (two steps; each rank sends \(n\) bytes), like vLLM's, which reduces the output in BF16
  • \(T_{\text{AG}}\): all-gather with \(p = 2\) (one step; each rank sends its fp32 partial)
  • \(R\): window rows; 16 KiB is one row of 4,096 fp32 values
  • \(\alpha, \beta\): per-step latency and effective bandwidth, as in section 3 (a model, not a measurement)

By the model, the all-gather sends twice the bytes (fp32 instead of BF16) but pays a single α. In a decode round, with \(R\) between 1 and 32, the message lands between 16 and 512 KiB, most of the time well below the \(n_{1/2}\) of section 3: α is what counts. In prefill, a 2,048-row chunk gives 32 MiB per rank, and there the extra bytes cost. That is why TensorFold attacks the two ends differently: a minimum-latency transport for the small gathers and, for the large ones, two rails on NCCL and fewer bytes in the hyper-connection glue.

One-shot RDMA: what leaves the path

In section 4 you saw the GB10 detour: without GPUDirect, NCCL copies into a host buffer and the NIC does RDMA from there. The patch's code notes that, for decode's small messages, NCCL spends most of its time in fixed latency (~30–50 µs). The recipe's patch 0006 brings in the b12x project's “RoCEnante” transport (the C proxy, unchanged; the GPU side rewritten in CUDA C++ with the same stages and the same memory orderings) and starts from a simple observation: on the GB10, “host” and “device” are the same LPDDR5X. Host pinned memory is addressable by GPU kernels at full bandwidth, and the NIC registers it as an MR with a plain ibv_reg_mr. So the GPU can write its shard straight into the region the NIC will send and read the peer's in the place where the NIC left it. An all-gather becomes one kernel call in five beats:

  1. Stage. Each block copies its share of the local shard into the send slot send[seq & 1], in the pinned region.
  2. Ring the doorbell. The last block to finish (an atomic arrival counter) publishes the size, issues a system-scope fence, and writes ctrl.seq. A proxy thread in C, using libibverbs, spins on that address and wakes up.
  3. Write into the peer. For each HCA, the proxy posts two chained requests on the same reliable QP: an RDMA WRITE with the payload stripe that belongs to that HCA and, right behind it, a 4-byte WRITE (inline) with the exchange number into that stripe's flag.
  4. Wait on the flag, not the CQ. On the receiver, one GPU thread per (peer, HCA) reads the flag with system-scope acquire loads until it sees this exchange's number. The receiver's CPU takes no part: “nothing on the receive path involves the host”, says the code.
  5. Copy in rank order and advance the epoch. Each block assembles the output (its own shard, from the input; the peer's, from the slot the NIC wrote), and the last one advances a device-side epoch, so that a CUDA graph can replay the exchange without the host (ch. 29).

Three verbs ideas hold the protocol up. The first is the one-sided RDMA WRITE: the sender writes into a registered address of the peer, with the rkey exchanged at startup, and the receiver does not even know, except by looking at memory. The second is the RC QP ordering: since the flag goes on the same reliable queue, after the data, it “cannot become visible before its payload”, in the proxy's words. It is the role the fence plays in NCCL's Simple protocol and the per-word flag plays in LL (section 7), here with one 4-byte flag per stripe and everything else useful data (the comparison is ours). The third is slot ping-pong: the kernel for exchange \(N\) finishes when the peers' shards arrive, without waiting for its own send. That is why exchange \(N+1\) uses the other slot, and two are enough, because a peer is never more than one exchange ahead. And the connection itself (the QP descriptors, the rkeys, the GIDs) is exchanged through the NCCL communicator at startup: one rendezvous resting on the other.

The gain, with its conditions alongside

For a 16 KiB all-gather (one row), the README measures 11 µs against NCCL's 45 µs. With COMM=roce, decode rose 6%: prose from 44.2 to 46.9 tok/s and code from 48.7 to 51.6 (on an intermediate build; the per-patch gains were measured at different points and do not add up to the final 60.4 tok/s, which come from sparkDash with the GPU clocks capped at 2,200 MHz). The back-of-the-envelope math uses the floor from section 3:

\[ \Delta t_{\text{round}} \;\approx\; N_{\text{ag}}\,\big(t_{\text{NCCL}} - t_{\text{shot}}\big) \;=\; 90 \times (45 - 11)\ \mu\text{s} \;\approx\; 3\ \text{ms} \]
  • \(N_{\text{ag}}\): partial all-gathers per round (2 per layer × 45 layers)
  • \(t_{\text{NCCL}}, t_{\text{shot}}\): 45 and 11 µs per 16 KiB all-gather (README)
  • against rounds on the order of 50 ms (the third-party project cited just below measured 53 ms per round at 1 stream, on its own engine), that is a few percent, consistent with the measured 6% (our math, which ignores each window's real size)

What goes through the shot is decided by the byte count alone: up to TF_ROCE_MAX_KB (256 in TensorFold, 512 in the recipe) goes over the custom RDMA, and the rest over NCCL, “with the same output layout (rank order), the same bits”. The 512 KiB are exactly the full batched window, 32 rows × 16 KiB: raising it from 256 moved the 17- to 32-row windows of concurrent requests onto the shot (code at 4 streams +1.4%; prose +0.5–1%). The yardstick is always the window, 16 KiB per row: anyone who widens a stream's window with TF_GLM_MAX_ROWS (up to 64) goes past 512 KiB above 32 rows, and those gathers go back to NCCL.

Deep dive: with a fast wire, misalignment is what remains

With the transport at ~11 µs, what is left of a collective's wait is no longer the cable. A third-party project, jayleaton's glm53-tensorfold-spark (TensorFold 0.3.4 with another 77 patches, a different checkpoint), measured 10.8 µs of pure transport per exchange and attributed the rest of the wait to misalignment between the ranks: identical kernels with 9–17% jitter (p5–p95), adding up to 1.3–1.5 ms per round with 1 stream and 2.2–3.0 ms with 4. A collective only finishes when the last rank arrives, so the slowest one sets the round. Hence two practices. Capping the GPU clocks for measurements (the recipe measures with a 2,200 MHz cap) removes boost variation between the ranks, though it does not prevent throttling below the cap (the research's interpretation). And filling the wait with useful work: prefetching weights into L2 on a side stream during each layer's two all-gathers (TF_GLM_L2PF, the recipe's patch 0046, adapted from one of jayleaton's) took, on its own, prose from 48.36 to 49.46 tok/s and code from 59.54 to 61.08 (the recipe's measurement, 1 request, two boots each), with the same bits.

Two rails and a GID found per node

The shot's proxy splits each payload into stripes, one per HCA (up to two), and each stripe gets its own flag: the receiver only reads the slot after seeing all of them. The two HCAs are the two PCIe Gen5 ×4 functions from section 5 (“each Spark's single cabled QSFP port is exposed as two PCIe Gen5 x4 functions”, in b12x's words). For the large gathers, which stay on NCCL, nodes.sh passes both HCAs in NCCL_IB_HCA when both are up and addressed on the link's subnet, with the RoCE v2 entry at the same GID index, forming two rails (that is v1.3.2's rule; v1.3.3 also pairs the cabled port's PCIe twin by name, #30 by webzone, so twins on different subnets, as in NVIDIA's two-Spark playbook, become rails too; and since v1.10, #66, start.sh first sends a full-size unfragmentable ping from each second rail's own address to the peer's matching rail, both ways, and drops a rail that does not answer with a warning, because an unaddressed twin, or one on another subnet or MTU, used to fail the first all-gather with vendor_err 0x81). The all-gather of a prompt chunk, 32 MB, drops from 3.5 ms on one rail to 1.9 ms on two (~1.8×), or ≈9 versus ≈17 GB/s effective (our math, bytes ÷ time), consistent with “one PF ≈ half a port”. NCCL_RAILS=1 forces a single rail.

The GID follows the rule from section 5, resolved at every start and per node (the section 5 comparison shows the steps); the index it finds serves both NCCL and the custom transport. Notice a clash of conventions: the DS audit recommended putting the second PF on another subnet, because of ARP flux (section 5), while TensorFold finds its rails precisely through the link's subnet. In v1.3.2, with the second PF on another subnet, the rails function in nodes.sh does not see it, and TensorFold comes up on a single rail, with no error (our reading of the code); #30 and the ping check removed that trap. With both on the same subnet, you are back in the setup where DS measured ARP flux. Neither source measured the other's choice: anyone alternating the two recipes on the same pair of Sparks should check each side's HCAs (the Link: … RoCE … line from start.sh), the RTT, and the counters under both configurations.

Control over the wire itself, and a doorbell to sleep on

Compare with the three planes of section 6. A TensorFold pair has two processes, one per Spark, and no Gloo, ZMQ, or shared-memory queue. The bootstrap is a TCPStore created by rank 0 on port 29551 (600 s timeout), through which the ncclUniqueId passes; NCCL is loaded through ctypes, and the calls are few: all-gather, the glue's point-to-point exchange, and a barrier. Control travels over the data plane: every iteration, rank 0 sends one operations message (ADMIT, ROUND, FINISH…) as an int32 vector, in two all-gathers (the length, then the values), and rank 1 applies the same operations in the same order. At startup, the ranks also all-gather the list of their settings and refuse to come up if it differs. And an idle rank 1 does not wait inside an all-gather, which would hold the GPU: it waits on a doorbell, a key in the TCPStore itself, which rank 0 rings when work arrives (a patch by Mia that went upstream in v0.6.0).

Meetings that went wrong

Section 9 treated the collective as an appointment. A custom transport adds two clauses: the two ranks must go to the same door, and whoever arrives first must wait long enough. Two of the recipe's patches tell one story each: ranks that went to different doors, and ranks that arrived tens of seconds apart.

The transport chosen by address (patch 0040). The first version sent through the shot any gather that fit within the limit and whose buffers were 16-byte aligned. But alignment depends on the address, and the address is local to each process. In asynchronous mode, rank 0 sent the control message from a view that started 4 bytes after the beginning of the allocation (buf[1:1+n]), while rank 1 received into a buffer of its own, aligned: one rank on NCCL, the other on RDMA, “forever”. The fix becomes a design rule: the choice depends on the byte count alone, which every rank shares, and a misaligned buffer first goes through an aligned copy. The same patch found a relative: the startup profiling routine ran only on rank 0, yet it replayed graphs whose all-gathers need the other rank. It now runs on both and joins the list of compared settings. It also created the application watchdog: if a rank-0 iteration (or rank 1's wait for a message) exceeds TF_GLM_MULTI_WATCHDOG_S (300 s), the Python stacks of every thread go to the log.

The first start (patch 0052, issue #6). The wait on the flag has a limit: 20 million polls of ~1 µs, about 20 s. Running out “poisons” the runtime: later launches do nothing until the host raises the error. On an image's first start, each rank compiles its CUDA extensions on its own, and the two could drift tens of seconds apart, more than the limit, so the first all-gather died. The fix: until the engine is built, each shot gather made outside a graph first waits for the peer in an NCCL barrier, which waits without that limit; gathers captured in a graph never do. The shot's kernel now compiles during setup, the limit became TF_ROCE_WAIT_S (20 s by default in the patch; from 1 to 3,600), and a failure prints the proxy's counters (doorbell, posted, completed, last; per HCA, completed and bytes). It is the same plot as the “One rank compiles, the other waits” figure in section 9, with a different clock. The clock moved once more: in v1.6 (issue #54) the recipe set TF_ROCE_WAIT_S=300, because on long prompts a peer that was merely late (the failing rank's own writes had all completed) outlasted the 20 s and took both Sparks down; now a late peer costs a slow round, and a rank that is really gone is noticed after 300 s, like the stack watchdog.

Prefill: the hyper-connection glue over point-to-point exchanges

In prefill the problem flips: the message is large and β is what weighs. Without a split, at each exchange point both ranks all-gather the fp32 partials \([R, D]\) and both run the hyper-connection glue (hc_post and the next hc_pre, over 4 streams × 4,096 columns) on all \(R\) rows: duplicated work. With SPLIT=1, rank \(r\) becomes the owner of rows \([rH, rH+H)\), with \(H = R/2\). At each exchange point: (1) it sends the peer only the partial of the peer's rows, in fp32, with ncclSend/ncclRecv between ncclGroupStart and ncclGroupEnd (in the same group, both directions move together; outside one, two ranks that send before receiving can deadlock); (2) it adds the two partials of its own rows, rank 0 first, with the same kernel; (3) it runs the glue only on them; (4) it swaps the normalized rows with the peer, in BF16, and both end up with the next block's whole input. It is section 2's identity, all-reduce = reduce-scatter + all-gather, built by hand with the compute in the middle: the same design as Megatron's sequence parallel, applied only to the glue (the analogy is the research's).

\[ \underbrace{4D}_{\text{fp32 all-gather}} \;\longrightarrow\; \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}\cdot 4D}_{\text{partial of the peer's rows}} \;+\; \underbrace{\tfrac{1}{2}\cdot 2D}_{\text{normalized rows (BF16)}} \;=\; 3D \]
  • bytes each rank sends per chunk row, at each exchange point; \(D = 4096\)
  • 16 KiB → 12 KiB per row (−25%), and the glue is halved on each rank (our math, without the drafter's taps, which are swapped like the normalized rows)

The exchanges also run on a second CUDA stream, in pieces (TF_GLM_PREFILL_OVERLAP=2, 2 pieces): each piece of the peer's partial starts traveling as soon as it is ready, a piece's glue waits only for its own rows, and the main stream gets ahead on the next block's “front” (input projections, shared expert) on its own rows while the others are on the wire, always with the bits of the unsplit path. Measured by the recipe: prefill of a 50k prompt from ~1,270 to ~1,730 tok/s (together with two prompt-kernel patches, 0009 and 0020), and a 149k one from 92.8 to 90.7 s with level-2 overlap; decode rounds pay ~1.5%. TF_GLM_HC_EXCHANGE=gather sends the same bytes through NCCL's all-gather, for fabrics where NCCL's point-to-point path is slow or misbehaves; chunks with fewer than 64 rows do not split.

Network checklist for your recipe

  1. Map the fabric on each node. Write down the interface, HCA (ibdev2netdev), link IP, and whether the cabling is crossed. Use per-node variables from the start.
  2. Prepare the host. MTU 9000 on both PFs, 8192 rings, second PF on a distinct subnet (in v1.3.2 only a twin on the link's subnet became a rail; since v1.3.3 the twin is also paired by name, and since v1.10 it is pinged before use), passwordless head→worker SSH, same image (same digest) on both nodes.
  3. Validate the GIDs. Find the RoCE v2 entry with each node's ::ffff:<ip>. Prefer a resolver that fails closed over a fixed index.
  4. Measure the fabric without vLLM. ib_write_bw and nccl-tests all_reduce_perf, with NCCL_DEBUG=INFO. Require NET/IB and write down the 8–32 KB latency.
  5. Set up the container. --network host --ipc host, a large /dev/shm, memlock=-1, /dev/infiniband, GPUs.
  6. Pin the NCCL environment. NCCL_NET=IB, NCCL_IB_DISABLE=0, HCA/ifname/GID per node, NVLS=0. Normalize empty variables.
  7. Start in order. The --headless worker first, then the head. Same arguments and same patches on both ranks.
  8. Instrument the hangs. Flight recorder, VLLM_EXECUTE_MODEL_TIMEOUT_SECONDS above the longest JIT, persisted JIT caches, and shape warmup.
  9. Only then tune. Dual-HCA, protocols, channels, QPs. One variable at a time, in A/B/A, because the effect on decode tends to be small and on prefill, large.
