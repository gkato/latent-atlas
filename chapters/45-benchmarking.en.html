The stopwatch of a request

A streaming response arrives over SSE (Server-Sent Events: an open HTTP connection over which the server pushes data: {…} lines). It is a sequence of events in time, and every serving metric is arithmetic on their timestamps. Call \(t_0\) the send, \(t_1\) the arrival of the first token, \(t_N\) that of the last one and \(N\) the number of generated tokens:

\[ \text{TTFT} = t_1 - t_0 \qquad \text{TPOT} = \frac{t_N - t_1}{N-1} \qquad \text{E2EL} = t_N - t_0 \] \[ \text{decode tok/s} = \frac{N-1}{t_N - t_1} = \frac{1}{\text{TPOT}} \qquad\qquad \text{prefill tok/s} = \frac{P}{\text{TTFT}} \]
  • \(t_0\): the instant the client sends the request
  • \(t_1\): arrival of the first token (what counts as “first” must be written down in the script)
  • \(t_N\): arrival of the last token
  • \(N\): generated tokens, read from usage.completion_tokens
  • \(P\): prompt tokens, from usage.prompt_tokens
  • ITL: interval between two consecutive stream events

TTFT (time to first token) adds up queueing, prefill and the first sampling; with the server idle and one request at a time, it measures prefill, and only then is prefill tok/s meaningful. TPOT (time per output token) is the reading experience during decode; its inverse is the “decode tok/s” that the recipes publish. ITL (inter-token latency) measures the interval between stream events; with speculation, each event may carry several tokens and ITL becomes “time per step”. E2EL is the end-to-end latency.

Note the \(N-1\): the first token belongs to TTFT, and between \(t_1\) and \(t_N\) there are only \(N-1\) intervals (the fencepost problem). Qwen's decodebench.py ((ctok−1)/window) and GLM's bench_decode.py use this form; DeepSeek's bench-miaai.py divides the whole output_tokens by the window. With 128 tokens the difference is 0.8%, small, but it's the first lesson: “almost identical” formulas don't produce comparable numbers. In the figure, step through and turn on speculation.

Three things jump out of the figure. When the prompt grows, E2EL grows almost entirely through TTFT: a “wall-clock tok/s” (\(N/\text{E2EL}\)) mixes the two phases, which is why GLM's bench_decode.py reports tok_s and delivered_tok_s side by side. With speculation, events arrive in bursts: GLM records ~2.3 tokens per chunk with DFlash2, and the DeepSeek audit measured that counting delta.content events undercounts the output by ~4.5×. Count via usage (with stream_options.include_usage).

Who is the “first token”?

Each script anchors \(t_1\) on a different event: Qwen's longctx.py on the first delta of any kind (including the empty role chunk, which marks the end of prefill); bench-miaai.py on content or reasoning; GLM's cold prefill on the first content token. With reasoning on, the first content only comes out after the <think>, and “TTFT” starts measuring the thinking. DeepSeek's bench-miaai.py (thinking: false) and GLM's scripts (enable_thinking: false) turn thinking off; benchmark-0731.py and the Qwen benches run with the server's setting, which is why longctx.py also looks for the needle in reasoning. Trap: the client has a stopwatch too

Pinning the output length: ignore_eos and min_tokens

To compare decode, the arms must generate the same number of tokens. If one stops earlier, the window and the tail (where acceptance usually drops) change, and the average changes without anything having gotten faster. In GLM's cooperative experiment, the same clamp_range prompt ended with 185, 178 and 324 tokens across three arms, and the report recommended preferring the structured and prose prompts.

Request parametersignore_eos: true · min_tokens = max_tokens

Together, with min_tokens = max_tokens, they pin the output. bench/decodebench.py uses --decode 600, --contexts 1000,600000, --temps 0.0,0.8 and four tasks (prose, code, entropy, copy). Deep dive: TPOT, ITL and speculation

In vllm bench serve, TPOT = \((\text{E2EL} - \text{TTFT})/(N-1)\) per request, and ITL is the list of intervals between chunks. Without speculation the two coincide. With it, a step that accepts 3 drafts emits 4 tokens in one chunk: ITL ≈ step duration, TPOT ≈ step ÷ tokens per step. A high p99 ITL with a low TPOT is not a contradiction; it's the signature of long steps that deliver many tokens.

One user or everyone: per stream, aggregate and the ladder

“How fast do I read the answer?” and “how many tokens per second does the machine deliver to everyone?” are different questions: per-stream tok/s and aggregate tok/s. With more concurrency they move in opposite directions. The reason, seen in the prefill and decode chapter and in the GPU chapter: decode is bandwidth-bound, and at each step the engine rereads the active weights in almost the same way with 1 or 8 sequences in the batch. More sequences split the same fixed cost:

\[ t_{\text{step}}(B) \approx t_0 + \delta\,B \qquad \text{per stream} = \frac{\tau}{t_{\text{step}}(B)} \qquad \text{aggregate} \approx B_{\text{eff}} \cdot \frac{\tau}{t_{\text{step}}(B_{\text{eff}})}, \quad B_{\text{eff}} = \min(C, S) \]
  • \(t_0\): fixed step cost (reading weights, collectives, CPU)
  • \(\delta\): marginal cost per sequence (KV, extra experts, activations)
  • \(\tau\): tokens emitted per step per sequence (1 without speculation)
  • \(C\): concurrent streams sent by the benchmark
  • \(S\): engine slots (--max-num-seqs)
  • \(B_{\text{eff}}\): sequences actually in the batch

As long as \(\delta B \ll t_0\), doubling the streams nearly doubles the aggregate. When \(\delta B\) starts to weigh in, the aggregate stops rising in the same proportion: that's the knee. When \(C\) exceeds \(S\), the excess streams wait in the queue, the aggregate plateaus and TTFT explodes. Play with the figure to feel the three phases.

With these parameters the model predicts 52 tok/s at ×1 and 27 per stream (215 aggregate) at ×8; Qwen day-0 measured 54.4 and 26.7 (207.0). It isn't exact physics, it's a ruler for reading ladders. The treacherous case is prefill: while a long prompt is processed in chunks (chunked prefill), each step carries the chunk along with the decodes and everyone waits. In DeepSeek's Aug 14 matrix, c=4 and c=6 with prompts of 32K or more sat on a floor of ~8 tok/s per stream, which the README attributes to one decode token per serialized prefill step.

KV capacity is a metric too

Concurrency and context compete for the same KV cache, so pool capacity is the third axis. The boot prints GPU KV cache size: N tokens and Maximum concurrency for L tokens per request: X× (PagedAttention chapter), and X× is just N ÷ L: “2.22×” in DeepSeek (2,331,430 tokens at 1M) means ~2 full 1M requests at the same time; in Qwen, 3,652,200 tokens with fp8 KV give 13.93× at 262K; in GLM, 1,233,779 tokens, 1.45× at 850K. When it runs out of KV, vLLM doesn't OOM: it preempts and redoes the prefill later, which is why the ladders record num_preemptions_total (0 to 2 per cell at ×16, and at ×8 with 50K, in GLM's Sep 1 ladder). And in GLM's hybrid model, “GPU KV cache size” adds up blocks from all groups: 643 IDs are only ~57,344 tokens of reusable conversation. Pool capacity is not prefix cache capacity.

In the TensorFold lane the pool isn't even fixed. The engine starts from MemAvailable at startup, subtracts a reserve (14.5 GiB in the recipe) and uses up to 12.5 GiB per Spark beyond the window: 2,922,496 tokens at the measured start, but anywhere from ~2.1 to ~2.9 million depending on what was free at that boot. The same .env on two boots can give two pools, and with them a different number of kept prompts and of long conversations at once. Record /health's pool_tokens at every boot, next to the number you publish.

Cold prefill: measuring without the cache cheating

Prefix caching is one of the best serving optimizations and the worst enemy of anyone measuring prefill: if the prompt starts the same as one already seen, the KV blocks are reused. In GLM, ~8K took 8.53 s cold and 1.30 s on the follow-up (7,168 of 8,004 tokens from the cache); 814,571 tokens, 633.7 s cold and 2.748 s repeated. In Qwen, 600K took 336 s cold and 2.87–8.82 s warm. A cold prefill takes these shortcuts out of the measurement.

Recall the mechanics from the prefix caching chapter: the prompt is cut into blocks, and each block's hash depends on its content and on the previous block's hash. A hit on block \(i\) requires blocks 0 to \(i\) to be identical. Hence the chapter's most important practical rule:

\[ h_0 = H(\text{salt} \,\|\, x_{0:b}), \qquad h_i = H(h_{i-1} \,\|\, x_{ib:(i+1)b}) \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \text{hits} = b \cdot \#\{\text{consecutive leading blocks with } h_j = h_j^{\text{cache}}\} \]
  • \(H\): block hash function
  • \(x_{ib:(i+1)b}\): the \(b\) tokens of block \(i\)
  • \(b\): page size that counts for a hit (3,584 in GLM, 1,600 in Qwen)
  • salt: a unique nonce you put at the start of the prompt, or the cache_salt field
  • if \(h_0 \ne h_0^{\text{cache}}\), the count is zero: no block is reused

A salt at the start changes \(h_0\), and the change cascades down the chain: zero hits. At the end, it changes only the last block. Compare the three positions and the reset in the figure.

Notice that the salt at the end barely changes anything: the cache keeps cheating. The API's cache_salt goes into the first block's hash and creates a namespace: different salts don't share prefixes, but they still write to the cache.

The recipes' cold prefill protocol

  1. Unique nonce at the start. Qwen's longctx.py opens with Run identifier {uuid4} -- session log begins. and GLM's _run_cold_prefill.py with COLD-PREFILL salt={uuid4} …. Beware of deterministic nonces: the one in bench-miaai.py (p{len}-c{c}-{rep}-r{i}) is unique within a run, but repeats across runs with the same --prompt/--concurrency. Use uuid4 or a session id.
  2. Reset when available. GLM's bench_prefix_cache.py calls POST /reset_prefix_cache before each cold turn. The route is opt-in (GLM53_EXPOSE_CACHE_RESET=1, patch patch_cache_reset.py, used in an isolated kit), so the script also salts every chat.
  3. Confirm, don't assume. Read cached_tokens from usage (it should be 0) or the delta of vllm:prefix_cache_hits. DeepSeek discarded a 3.0 s TTFT at 131K: rep 0 of --repeat 2 sent p131072-c1-0-r0, the same prompt that ab-measure.sh's --long had just sent. It was cache, not prefill. By the same mechanism, the 5 extra trials in ab-boot.sh repeat 3 c=1 prompts from ab-measure.sh.
  4. Calibrate with the tokenizer. Qwen's filler gives ~25 tokens per line in /tokenize. In DeepSeek, ~1.56 tokens per word: the 1.3 tokens-per-word heuristic (EVAL.md writes “words/token”, but the “under-pads ~1.2x” only adds up this way) made a “524,288-token” sweep send ~815K tokens, and above 700K requested tokens the server's correct 400 (1M ceiling) was counted as false garbling.
  5. Minimal output, one at a time, thinking off. GLM uses max_tokens=8, temperature 0, stream + include_usage and TTFT at the first content token. Concurrency puts queueing inside TTFT.

Speculation under the microscope: /metrics and per-position acceptance

With speculative decoding, tok/s depends on two things measured separately: how long a step lasts and how many tokens it emits. The second comes from draft acceptance, which the engine counts in Prometheus counters at /metrics.

The format is text, name{labels} value. _total counters only go up since boot and add up all clients; histograms come as _bucket{le="…"}, _sum and _count. Dividing totals describes the server's history; delta measurement describes your experiment (the figure just below shows the difference):

\[ \bar{\alpha} = \frac{\Delta\,\text{accepted}}{\Delta\,\text{draft\_tokens}} \qquad \bar{A} = \frac{\Delta\,\text{accepted}}{\Delta\,\text{drafts}} \qquad a_i = \frac{\Delta\,\text{accepted\_per\_pos}[i]}{\Delta\,\text{drafts}} \qquad \frac{\text{tokens}}{\text{step}} = 1 + \bar{A} = 1 + \sum_{i} a_i \] \[ \text{ms/step} = 1000 \cdot \frac{\Delta\,\text{inter\_token\_latency\_seconds\_sum}}{\Delta\,\text{inter\_token\_latency\_seconds\_count}} \qquad\qquad \text{tok/s} \approx \frac{\text{tokens/step}}{\text{ms/step}/1000} \]
  • \(\Delta x\): value after − value before the workload
  • drafts: number of proposals (one per request per step)
  • draft_tokens: proposed drafts (\(k\) per proposal)
  • \(\bar{\alpha}\): acceptance rate (fraction of drafts accepted, \(= \bar{A}/k\))
  • \(a_i\): fraction of proposals in which position \(i\) (and the previous ones) was accepted
  • the “1 +” is the bonus token that the target always emits

Qwen's bench/mtp_accept.py spells out the rule in its docstring: cumulative acceptance mixes every request the server has ever seen, so “always measure a delta around the workload” (--save before.json, benchmark, --since before.json). bench/sweep.py does the same at each rung and warns that it requires an idle server, otherwise the numbers include other people's traffic.

Parsing traps the recipes have already paid for

1. Each counter has a sibling _created series with a Unix timestamp; DeepSeek's spec-acceptance.py anchors on _total so it doesn't add it in. 2. The position="i" label may come last, and a comma-based parse lost the whole curve. 3. Values may come in scientific notation; GLM's spec-accept-gate.sh rejects unreadable or infinite values, or values above \(2^{53}\) (where awk loses precision). 4. Two label sets on the same endpoint have no defensible aggregate: the gate refuses to pick a denominator. 5. A restart zeroes the counters; negative delta = the engine restarted midway.

The per-position curve is the drafter's electrocardiogram

The average rate hides the shape. The curve \(a_1, \ldots, a_k\) shows where the draft loses the thread and decides how much it's worth speculating further out. Since position \(i\) is only accepted if the previous ones are, a healthy curve decays monotonically. If each draft is accepted independently with probability \(p\), then \(a_i = p^i\) and \(\mathbb{E}[\text{tokens/step}] = (1-p^{k+1})/(1-p)\). Don't confuse \(p\) with the rate \(\bar{\alpha}\) from /metrics: in this model, \(\bar{\alpha} = (p - p^{k+1})/(k(1-p))\), which is smaller than \(p\) (with \(p\) = 0.8 and \(k\) = 6, \(\bar{\alpha} \approx\) 0.49). Real curves usually fall faster at the end (the “suffix decay” from the DFlash and DSpark chapter).

Look at GLM with the same DFlash2 (k=7): the structured prompt (“count from 1 to 200”) sustains 0.98…0.83 all the way to the seventh position, while prose (explaining a hash map) falls from 0.75 to 0.06. Same server, 6.71 versus 2.39 drafts accepted per step, 65.1 versus 27.1 tok/s. (The curves are per-position medians, which is why \(\sum a_i\) = 6.41 and 2.33 doesn't exactly reproduce the per-run medians.) The method: noise, A/B/A and one variable per boot

Every measurement is signal plus noise, and the recipes cataloged their sources with rare candor. They are what decide how many repetitions and which experimental design to use:

  • The drawn prompt (and sampling). In DeepSeek, c=1 varies ±10 tok/s (47.7–76.3 within one boot, the A′ of Sep 2). The repository attributes the variation to the randomly drawn cold prompt, which changes acceptance; since the c=1 prompts differ only by the nonce string and the output is sampled at T=0.6, part of the noise comes from sampling itself (interpretation). Three trials aren't enough: ab-boot.sh does 8, and the math just below says why.
  • Clock and temperature. The GPU raises its clock (boost) while there's thermal and power headroom and drops it (throttling) when it heats up or hits the cap: a short burst measures boost, a long session, the sustained clock. In DeepSeek's decode, the fable5-1 report saw the SM at a steady 2,190 MHz (max 3,003, application clock 2,418), ~26 W and no power cap during the measurement, but 29,297 s of SW power cap accumulated since boot. At TP=2 the slowest rank dictates the step, because the collective waits for it. GLM's tests/bench_clock_lock.sh designs an A/B/A around this: the GB10 has no application clocks (“Supported Clocks: N/A”), so the only lever is nvidia-smi -lgc MIN,MAX on both ranks, with clocks.sm, power.draw and temperature.gpu logged at 1 Hz on both nodes. The hypothesis: capping boost avoids thermal oscillation and raises sustained decode. No result was published.
  • The boot. The head's KV varied from 15.96 to 17.12 GiB between boots with the same configuration (±0.6 GiB of profiler noise).
  • Drift. Page cache, residual swap, other people's processes and temperature change over the session. In one DeepSeek A/B, the baseline dropped to 2.55 GB of MemAvailable because the head was carrying ~0.9 GB more of operator processes (the agent session and several node processes).
  • The knob that increases variance. In Qwen with “breakable” CUDA graphs, the same cell (prose ×4) measured 118/149/138/109 across identical runs; without them, ~2%.
  • Isolated cells. In the vLLM 0.30 lane, single runs at medium concurrency varied ±20%. The Sep 16 rerun sums it up: 2.0–5.0% deviation, individual cells don't reproduce, the mean does.

Summarizing a distribution: median, percentiles, range

With few repetitions and long tails, the mean is hostage to outliers (the cold request, a swap pause). The median is the recipes' standard summary (“median of 5×400 tokens” in GLM, “of ≥ 8 trials” in DeepSeek). For latency, what hurts is the tail: report percentiles (TTFT p95 leaves out only the worst 5%). The noise band (the baseline's min–max) is the ruler: a knob only wins if its trials land outside it. DeepSeek's sp-indexer won because both 128K trials (76.0 and 74.4 s) came in below both baseline trials (79.0 and 81.2 s), outside the 2 s spread; B12X_W4A16_TC_DECODE=1 lost with 7 of 8 trials below the minimum (median ≈46 versus ≈55, −15%).

How many trials? An order-of-magnitude calculation

With Gaussian noise of standard deviation \(\sigma\), the standard error of the mean of \(n\) trials is \(\sigma/\sqrt{n}\), and that of the median is \(\approx 1.25\,\sigma/\sqrt{n}\). The difference between two arms adds both uncertainties (a factor of \(\sqrt{2}\)). For an effect \(\delta\) to sit ~2 standard errors away from zero (≈ 95% confidence that it isn't noise), you need \(n \approx 2\,(2 \cdot 1.25\,\sigma/\delta)^2 = 12.5\,(\sigma/\delta)^2\) trials per arm. With \(\sigma\) ≈ 5 tok/s (illustrative, consistent with DeepSeek's ±10 band), 8 trials pin the median to ±2.2 tok/s, and only differences above ~6 tok/s (≈ 11% of 55) rise above the noise. The −15% of B12X_W4A16_TC_DECODE (\(\delta\) ≈ 8 tok/s) needs ~5 trials; a 3% gain (\(\delta\) ≈ 1.7) would need ~115. That's why DeepSeek's A/B only sees large effects at c=1. The “resolution” readout in the simulator below does this math with your sliders.

A/B/A: measuring drift together with the effect

The naive design measures the baseline (A) and then the change (B); if the machine drifted between the two, the drift becomes “effect”. The A/B/A design measures the baseline again at the end and gains two things: the effect against the mean of the baselines, and the measured drift.

\[ \widehat{\Delta} = \frac{\tilde{B}}{(\tilde{A}_1 + \tilde{A}_2)/2} - 1 \qquad\qquad \widehat{\text{drift}} = \frac{\tilde{A}_2}{\tilde{A}_1} - 1 \]
  • \(\tilde{A}_1, \tilde{B}, \tilde{A}_2\): medians of each arm, in the order they were measured
  • \(\widehat{\Delta}\): estimated effect of the change
  • if \(|\widehat{\text{drift}}|\) is comparable to \(|\widehat{\Delta}|\), the conclusion doesn't hold

GLM published a perfect example when testing the drafter's compact pages (Sep 21) as OFF/ON/OFF. Structured: 78.589 / 78.380 / 76.390 → \(\widehat{\Delta}\) = 78.380 ÷ 77.490 − 1 = +1.15%. Code: 53.493 / 52.048 / 49.361 → +1.21%. Prose: 33.188 / 31.131 / 31.200 → −3.30% (on a holdout, −0.93%). But the code baseline dropped 7.7% from A1 to A2: a naive A/B would have concluded −2.7%, a B/A, +5.4%. The published conclusion was “no universal decode speedup”. The simulator shows how often each design gets it wrong.

With −6% drift and zero effect, the naive A/B “finds” a loss in half the sessions; A/B/A gets it wrong far less often. With high noise and 3 trials, both get it wrong a lot: the way out is more trials, not a smarter design. Turn on the cold outlier and compare mean and median: that's why the median and a discarded warmup go hand in hand.

The rules, written down before measuring

  1. One variable per boot, against a baseline from the same day. Numbers from another date are context, not an arm (GLM labels them “different day, not a same-window bracket”).
  2. Decision rule frozen before the first trial (GLM's “frozen gates”). The recipe-from-scratch tutorial uses: accept if the target improves by ≥ 3% on both sides of the A/B/A and no secondary metric gets worse by more than 2% and the quality canary stays within the noise.
  3. Boot gates. Confirm the code path in both ranks' logs: Capturing CUDA graphs (FULL) 6/6, no “Truncating”, the patched lines for the enabled knobs, no EngineDead or NCCL WARN. A failed boot measures something else.
  4. Memory gate. On unified memory, a knob that eats host RAM is a regression even if it speeds things up. DeepSeek rejects any knob that costs ≥ 0.5 GB of MemAvailable or increases swap; GLM used ≥ 2 GiB of MemAvailable and ≤ 256 MiB of new swap-out.
  5. Interleaved order. sweep.py alternates the order of the rungs at each repetition (1, 2, 4, then 4, 2, 1) so that drift doesn't line up with concurrency.
  6. Confidence interval when you can. GLM's adoption of dense FP8 came out as “ms/cycle −4.67% [−5.11, −4.06]”, a paired bootstrap with 10 runs and 5/5 workloads. An interval that doesn't cross zero = an effect distinguishable from noise.
  7. Complete record. Commit, digest, effective .env, date, boot logs and honest labels: GLM's adoption report marks each claim as Measured, Code-verified or Interpretation. Negative results go into the CHANGELOG too.

Predict before you measure: the step cost model

Measuring tells you how much; a model tells you how much it should be, and the difference is where the work lives. The step cost model from the Qwen and DeepSeek review reports starts from the central observation about decode, that each step rereads the active bytes, and adds the network collectives:

\[ t_{\text{step}} \;\gtrsim\; \frac{B_{\text{step}}}{\beta_{\text{eff}}} + N_{\text{col}}\,\ell_{\text{col}} + t_{\text{cpu}} \qquad\qquad \text{tok/s per stream} \approx \frac{1 + \bar{A}}{t_{\text{step}}} \]
  • \(B_{\text{step}}\): bytes read per GPU per step (active weights, verification and draft lm_head, state)
  • \(\beta_{\text{eff}}\): effective bandwidth (the GB10 has ~273 GB/s theoretical; 230–250 is realistic)
  • \(N_{\text{col}}\): collectives per step (TP all-reduces, logits gathers)
  • \(\ell_{\text{col}}\): latency of one small collective over RoCE (tens of µs)
  • \(1+\bar{A}\): tokens emitted per step (bonus + accepted drafts)

The \(\gtrsim\) is deliberate: the model is a time floor (a tok/s ceiling), because real kernels don't reach peak bandwidth and there are synchronizations and CPU work it ignores. Build the budget in the figure and compare it with what the recipes measured later.

DeepSeek: the model adds up with the right acceptance. ≈16.5–17 GB per rank (≈12 GB of experts: up to 42 distinct out of 256 per layer × 6.7 MB × 43 layers), at 230–250 GB/s, give 68–74 ms, plus ≈108 collectives at 30–50 µs (3–5 ms). The report divides “~4.15 accepted tokens/step” by that time, arrives at 56–61 tok/s, below the 62–83 measured, and attributes the slack to a bandwidth “a bit better than assumed”. Despite the “accepted” label, the 4.15 already includes the bonus: it is 1 + the sum of the per-position curve from the TP=3 probe (0.92 + 0.74 + 0.60 + 0.41 + 0.28 + 0.21 = 3.16), the same convention as the report's “≈3.9 tokens per step” (\(1 + 6 \times 0.484\)). The published 62–83 tok/s come from prompts with higher acceptance (code 73.3% → \(1 + 6 \times 0.733 = 5.4\) tokens/step), not from a forgotten bonus (interpretation). The engine counters later measured 14–15 steps/s (≈67–71 ms), code at 76.0 tok/s with 73.3% acceptance and prose at 37.3 with 25.7%. Check: 5.4 tokens/step × 14.5 steps/s ≈ 78; \(1 + 6 \times 0.257 = 2.5\) × 14.5 ≈ 37. One awkward detail: the measured step (67–71 ms) falls below the 71–79 ms “floor” with collectives. The budget used the maximum of 42 distinct experts per layer (the expected value is ≈ 39, ~0.9 GB less; our math) and a conservative bandwidth. A floor is only a floor if its inputs are bounds. c=1 decode is ~80% weight reading, and the lever that remains is acceptance.

Qwen: the coincidence that hid two errors. The report added up ≈9.2 GiB per GPU per step (the lm_head, read 4× for one verification and three drafts, is the largest item, at 26%): 40–42 ms at 235–245 GB/s and, with “~2.2 tokens/step”, 53–56 tok/s, “exactly the 55.8 measured in the README”. First error: 2.2 is the README's accepted/step (823/1131 × 3 = 2.18), without the bonus; with it, that's 3.18 tokens/step, and the floor would predict 76–80 tok/s. Second error: the README marks that 55.8 as captured with MTP off and different weights; with MTP=3 and bf16 KV, it measured 52.1. The real step is then ~61 ms (3.18 ÷ 52.1), which matches the 59.3–60.7 ms from sweep.py with the full vocabulary at S=1 (Sep 16 rerun): ~45–50% above the floor. Interpretation: the byte budget leaves out ~15–20 ms per step of below-bandwidth kernels, launches and synchronizations. That's why ms/step and tokens/step are measured separately: the product can match by accident. Where the model does help is with relative effects: applying the byte model to the lm_head slice (our math, interpretation), cutting the draft vocabulary to 47k predicts −15% on the step, and the rerun measured 59.3 → 51.9 ms (prose, S=1), −12.5%.

The identity that closes any table

If the report gives tok/s, ms/step and accepted/step, check \(\text{tok/s} \approx (1 + \text{accepted/step}) / (\text{ms/step}/1000)\). In GLM's cooperative experiment (Sep 16, structured ×1, stock): \((1 + 6.863)/0.10842 = 72.5\) versus 72.03 published; with the C1 overlay, \(7.712/0.09918 = 77.8\) versus 77.18; on stock prose, \(3.314/0.09672 = 34.3\) versus 34.09. If it doesn't add up, some definition changed. At ×2, for example, GLM's “ms/step” is 1000 · decode_s / drafts with the drafts from both requests, and it drops to ~58 ms without the engine step having gotten any faster (interpretation).

The model also tells you what doesn't help. If 80% of the step is reading weights, an attention twice as fast at short context barely shows up; if Qwen's ~106 collectives cost 2–3 ms and don't shrink with the batch, changing the network won't multiply decode. Four levers remain: fewer bytes per step (dense FP8: 9.2 → 5.7 GiB, a floor of ~25 ms, analytical and never measured on the GPU; a reduced draft vocabulary), more accepted tokens, more sequences per step and a lower fixed floor. Prefill is a different calculation: an 8,192-token Qwen chunk touches every expert (≈34 GB per GPU, ~140 ms) and does 96 all-reduces of 42 MB (~150–180 ms on a 200 GbE link); in GLM, the 947 tok/s before E2 were ~34 TFLOPS, ≈14% of the GB10's dense BF16 peak, with the MoE bandwidth-bound.

Degenerate input: the MoE discounts what you time

The TensorFold lane doesn't estimate the cost of a verify window: it measures it. At startup it times windows of every width, and the joint allocation of drafts across streams (TF_GLM_MULTI_DEPTH=joint) and the round profile use that curve. The first version timed a window of one repeated token. In an MoE, identical rows route to the same few experts, and every distinct expert is weights that must be read: a 16-row window came out at about half the cost of 16 rows of real text. Patch 0048 switched to distinct, fixed ids (drawn with seed 1234). In this section's language, the repeated token zeroes the part of \(\delta\) that comes from extra experts, and the measured “floor” sits below the real one. The real curve is sublinear: at the time of the simulator in section 8, verifying 1 to 8 rows cost from 29.2 to 66.0 ms, 8 rows for ~2.3× the price of one (our arithmetic). That margin is what speculation exploits in a bandwidth-bound decode, and the same trap applies to any MoE microbenchmark: synthetic input has to touch as many experts as the real one.

When the number doesn't add up: profiling and diagnosis

The benchmark says it got slow, the model says it shouldn't have; what answers “where did the time go?” is profiling. The recipes use a ladder of tools, from the cheapest to the most invasive:

1 · logs and /metrics
Free and always on: chosen backend, captured graphs, counters by delta.
2 · engine stopwatches
Per-stage timers in vLLM; cheap, but the CPU waits for the GPU to finish each stage (the observer effect).
3 · torch profiler / nsys
A trace of CPU and GPU kernels (the torch profiler is the path integrated into vLLM); expensive, few iterations.
4 · py-spy
The Python stack of a live or hung process, without restarting it: the tool for hangs.
5 · NCCL dumps
The flight recorder dumps the last collectives on timeout.

STAGE_TIMING measures the stages of the DSpark proposer (context_prepare, prefill_main, graph_prepare, draft, postprocess, total), ITER_TIMING the whole model runner iteration, TARGET_TIMING the target's forward (mHC, attn, ffn, dspark_capture, hc_head). *_LOG_EVERY=20 prints the average every 20 steps. All default to 0 for the reason noted in the code: the CPU waits for the GPU at the end of each stage, which is fine for diagnosis and never for benchmarking.

The observer effect on the GPU

CUDA kernels are launched asynchronously: the CPU enqueues and moves on, and with async scheduling it is already preparing the next step. DSpark's timers measure each stage with CUDA events (torch.cuda.Event before and after) and call end.synchronize() at the end: the time is measured on the GPU, but the CPU sits idle until the stage finishes, without enqueuing the next one, and the CPU/GPU overlap is lost. The code's own log warns: “synchronizes CUDA work and is intended for diagnostics, not speed-gate benchmarks”. Profilers like the torch profiler and nsys don't synchronize at every stage, but they have a cost too; that's why they capture few iterations and stay out of A/B arms.

Acceptance diagnosticsDSparkDiagnostics · DSparkPosition0Diagnostics (Stage-C overlay)

Accumulators in the DSpark proposer: a histogram of scheduled lengths, the prune rate (draft_token_prune_rate), mean confidence and survival per position (\(s_i = \prod_{j \le i} c_j\), the predicted probability that position \(i\) makes it through alive) and position 0's hit rate against the target's argmax (VLLM_DSPARK_POSITION0_DIAGNOSTICS). They show where acceptance drops (0.89 → 0.15 across positions) and whether the confidence head is calibrated, which decides k and hardware-aware scheduling (DSpark chapter).

VariablesTF_GLM_MULTI_PROFILE=N · TF_GLM_MULTI_WATCHDOG_S=300

The TensorFold lane's round profile (patch 0035), off by default. Every N rounds, rank 0 logs the average of each stage: plan, message, propose, verify, sample, accept, commit, taps, emit, plus the remainder, the verify's GPU time, rows and tokens per round and the share of time spent on prompt chunks. The stages are host wall time between marks, and the GPU time comes from CUDA events read after the sampler has already waited for the GPU anyway: the profile adds no synchronization, unlike the DSpark timers above. The watchdog (patch 0040) is a built-in py-spy: if an iteration exceeds 300 s, it dumps the Python stacks, because with two ranks in lockstep a hang is silent.

Container flag--cap-add SYS_PTRACE (TP1 only)

py-spy reads another process's Python stack and needs the SYS_PTRACE capability; without it, py-spy dump in the container gives “Permission denied”. The single-Spark lane (start-tp1.sh) adds it because of a deadlock after graph capture (recorded suspect: ple_offload_wait, a stream wait recorded by the capture without being executed, hangs on the first replay). The dump shows which line each thread stopped at, without killing the process.

For network hangs there's NCCL's flight recorder (communication chapter): DeepSeek's compose turns on TORCH_NCCL_DUMP_ON_TIMEOUT=1 and TORCH_NCCL_ENABLE_MONITORING=1 (with TORCH_FR_BUFFER_SIZE=2000), and a hung rank produces a dump of the last collectives instead of silence; the Qwen report recommends the same, plus VLLM_EXECUTE_MODEL_TIMEOUT_SECONDS. For the hang that /health doesn't see, GLM's UVM livelock document watches /metrics: running or waiting requests > 0 with prompt_tokens_total + generation_tokens_total frozen for 10 consecutive readings (one every 60 s). Summing prompt and generation avoids false alarms during long prefills.

Trap: NCCL fell back to TCP and nobody said a word

With the wrong HCA (the RDMA adapter chosen in NCCL_IB_HCA) or the wrong GID index (the entry in the port's RoCE address table), NCCL may use TCP sockets instead of verbs, and the ~100 small collectives per step become much more expensive. Check once with NCCL_DEBUG=INFO: the transport should say NET/IB, not NET/Socket. In production the recipes use WARN, and DeepSeek's A/B noted that, because of this, it couldn't confirm the device from the logs. Microbenchmarks mislead too: nccl-tests measured +64% busbw with dual-HCA, but the end-to-end gain of the pair (MTU 9000 plus two HCAs) was +7.6%, and decode didn't change.

Update: measuring an exact engine, TensorFold

The GLM recipe got a second version : the same EXL3 checkpoint, on the same two Sparks, served by another engine, TensorFold v0.6.0 with 53 of Mia's patches (the engine's ideas are in ch. 48; the full recipe, in ch. 49). For whoever measures, the news is not just the number. TensorFold signs an exactness contract: the reply with drafts is, bit for bit, the one from serial decoding; a request served alongside three others is the same as served alone; a prompt resumed from a kept state gives the same reply as one computed from scratch. What holds the contract up is keyed sampling: the draw at each position is a function of (seed, absolute position, token id), and, without a seed, the seed comes from a hash of the prompt. With that, three tools from this chapter change shape: a knob's correctness becomes a hash, the noise between trials of the same prompt loses its content part, and draft policies can be simulated exactly, without a GPU.

The conditions that travel with the number

The lane's published numbers come with a line of conditions, and each item pins down a variable from this chapter. sparkDash through the OpenAI API, from another machine on the network: the stopwatch sits at the client, as in section 1. Lab protocol with temperature 0 and thinking off: greedy, no sampling. Default configuration: 4 streams, a 1,048,576-token window, FP8 KV, 4-bit dense weights, DFlash2 plus copy drafts, vision on. And GPU clocks capped at 2,200 MHz (the README's word): an upper limit, not a fixed frequency. The clock is the new item. The cap cuts off boost and the oscillation that comes with it, the part of section 5's clock noise that changes from one run to the next; throttling below the cap is still possible. In DeepSeek's decode, on the same GB10, the SM sat steady at 2,190 MHz with a 3,003 maximum, so a 2,200 cap keeps the clock nearly constant in a session with no power capping (interpretation). This weighs more at TP=2, where the slowest rank sets the pace of the round: a third-party project on TensorFold (jayleaton's) measured 1.3 to 3.0 ms of waiting per round from misalignment between the ranks alone. The README doesn't say how the cap was applied; on the GB10 the available lever is nvidia-smi -lgc MIN,MAX (with MIN = MAX the clock is locked; with a low MIN, it is only capped), the same as in GLM vLLM's bench_clock_lock.sh, which never published a result (interpretation). Numbers without the cap may come out different, neither better nor worse in principle.

Speed and bits in the same gate

On an engine that preserves the distribution rather than the sequence (vLLM's rejection sampling, ch. 35), the batch, the kernel path and speculation can change bits, and knowing whether a knob changed the reply takes statistical evaluation (ch. 25). In TensorFold, each knob is declared in one of two groups, and the first goes through an exactness gate:

same bits
COMM=roce (+6% decode), SPLIT=1, TF_GLM_L2PF, TF_GLM_EXL3_LOADS, the draft policy, copy drafts, multi-prefill. The reply has to come out identical; if it does, the A/B only looks at the clock.
lossy, declared
DENSE=q4 and KV=fp8, plus KDA_CHUNKED=1, close to the serial kernel but not with the same bits. Here quality evaluation comes back: GSM8K 98.0% (250 problems) and HumanEval 97.6% (164) with FP8 KV and q4, and regression #18, in which short French coding prompts lost the end of turn in 12 of 48 replies with q4, against 1 of 48 with bf16 or fp8.

The published checks are that gate: in v1.3, “reference shas, drafted == serial 6/6, concurrency 22/22”, plus images, videos and tool calls; in the README, 11 of 11 cases identical whether served 4 at a time or alone, staggered, and 11 of 11 in a burst. The serial reference is a request field ("draft": false). For the protocol, this flips a priority: on a “same bits” knob, a speed gain with a different hash is not a gain, it's a bug. The gate is binary, cheap enough to run at every boot and doesn't depend on noise. The caveat comes from the contract itself: exactness is against the same engine, the same weights and the same settings. It doesn't say the q4 reply is the bf16 checkpoint's; the reference for that is DENSE=bf16 KV=bf16 KDA_CHUNKED=0, with a smaller window.

Content noise and timing noise

On DeepSeek at T=0.6, the same prompt design gave 48 to 76 tok/s within one boot (section 5), partly because each trial samples a different reply, and different replies accept drafts differently. Separate the sources. The content decides which tokens come out and, therefore, how many drafts are accepted and how many rounds the reply takes. The timing decides how much each round costs: clock, network, host. Greedy already pins down almost all of the content; the key extends that to sampling with temperature, because the same request gives the same reply. What's left is timing, which the clock cap shrinks. One practical caveat: without a seed, the key comes from the prompt's hash, so the nonce that section 3 tells you to put at the start changes the prompt and the key, and each cold trial becomes a different reply again. To isolate timing, measure decode by repeating the identical prompt (the prefill becomes a replay of the kept state and drops out of the measurement) and the cold prefill separately, with a nonce (interpretation). The figure draws eight trials of the same prompt in the four regimes.

This helps read a practice of the recipe that, by the arithmetic in section 5, would look daring: the knob A/Bs are “two boots each” (sometimes “one boot each”), and some published effects are 0.5–1%. By the formula \(n \approx 12.5\,(\sigma/\delta)^2\), two trials per arm only resolve \(\delta\) if \(\sigma \lesssim 0.4\,\delta\): for a 1% effect, noise of at most ~0.4% between boots (our arithmetic). That's plausible with fixed content and a capped clock; it's impossible with DeepSeek's ±10 tok/s of prompt noise. But the repository publishes neither intervals nor the between-boot noise, and notes the design next to each number. Read “the effect was this size over two boots”, not “the effect is”.

Per-patch numbers don't add up

The recipe's knob table has “before → after” pairs measured at different points of development: the starting one-request prose is 38.9 tok/s in the q4 A/B, 44.2 in the RoCE one, 47.2 in the draft-policy one, 48.36 in the L2 prefetch one. Each pair measures an incremental effect in that build, with intermediate workloads and configurations different from sparkDash's. Multiplying the gains doesn't rebuild the published 60.4 tok/s, and none of them is an A/B against the vLLM recipe. It's rule 1 from section 5 (same-day baseline) applied to a changelog.

Simulate before measuring

Section 6 predicted decode with a bytes model. TensorFold takes the idea to the limit with an offline simulator of the drafter's stop rules (patch 0011). With TF_GLM_DRAFT_DUMP=<dir>, the request decodes one token per round, the serial reply, as a teacher-forced record: at each position, it saves what the DFlash2 block would have proposed there (candidates, logits, selector edges, the keyed Gumbel draws) and the token the target committed. Since the draw depends only on (seed, position, token), a round that starts at position \(i\) and proposes a chain keeps exactly the drafts equal to the tokens committed at \(i+1, i+2, \ldots\) up to the first mismatch, plus one sampled token. Any policy can be replayed in numpy, on the CPU, and a table of measured costs turns rounds into time:

\[ t_{\text{round}}(k) = V(k+1) + B\,[\text{drafts}] + o \qquad\qquad \text{tok/s} = \frac{\sum_r (a_r + 1)}{\sum_r t_{\text{round}}(k_r)} \]
  • \(k_r\): drafts proposed in round \(r\); the window verifies \(k_r+1\) rows, the pending token and the drafts
  • \(V(R)\): measured cost of verifying \(R\) rows: 29.2 · 35.6 · 42.6 · 48.8 · 53.6 · 58.9 · 64.5 · 66.0 ms for \(R\) = 1…8 (the table built into patch 0011, measured on the engine of that time)
  • \(B\): one DFlash2 block, 3.08 ms, paid when the policy drafts
  • \(o\): per-round overhead (sampling, commit, host); 0 by default in the simulator
  • \(a_r\): drafts accepted in round \(r\); the “+1” is the token sampled at the first mismatch

The simulator ships the earlier rules (f, fixed depth; fc, the production one, which stops when the product of the chain's confidences drops below \(p\)), the new ones (nc, with noise-aware confidences, which the engine calls fnc; cost, the \(k\) that maximizes \(\mathbb{E}[\text{tokens}\mid k]/t_{\text{round}}(k)\)) and an oracle, which picks the depths in hindsight by dynamic programming: a ceiling no causal rule reaches. It also scores the acceptance predictors with calibration and ranking metrics: log loss, Brier and AUC. That's where the \(\beta\) = 2.0 of the noise-aware confidences came from: fitted by log loss on 9 dumps from Sep 30 (7 of sampled prose, 2 of greedy code), the same value on all of them. Only then came the live measurement, fnc7:0.3 against fc5:0.3: prose 47.2 → 50.6 tok/s and code 51.7 → 55.5, two boots each, with the same replies under any policy. Play with the simulator below: the dump is synthetic, the cost table is the real one.

Three things show up right away. A large fixed depth loses to a small one when acceptance drops, because each extra row costs 1.5 to 7 ms of verification even when rejected. At the same threshold, the rule whose confidence is better calibrated (lower log loss) usually gets closer to the oracle; the best threshold, though, shifts with the predictor and with the dump, and sweeping thresholds costs milliseconds. And the oracle is out of reach: it knows the future. The method's gain is an engineering one: dozens of policies and thresholds evaluated in seconds of CPU over dumps recorded once, with the boot reserved for confirming the winner. The code itself warns that the replay is exact “up to the drafter's numerics” (in the dump, DFlash2's context comes from one-row verifies; live, from wider windows). And the cost table dates from patch 0011: the decode patches that came after changed those times (interpretation). That's why live confirmation remains mandatory.

Why this doesn't come for free with rejection sampling

In rejection sampling, accepting draft \(j\) depends on a draw \(u\) made on the spot against \(\min(1, p/q)\), and \(q\) is the distribution of that policy's chain. The log of one run doesn't say what a different chain would have accepted: you can estimate it in expectation, with the per-position curve from section 4, but the sequence changes along with the policy. With keyed sampling, the committed sequence doesn't depend on the policy, and counterfactual evaluation becomes a deterministic replay (interpretation).

Comparing recipes with caution

The three recipes run on the same hardware (2× GB10, TP=2), and GLM even has two lanes, vLLM and TensorFold, with the same checkpoint. It's tempting to put the numbers side by side and declare a winner. The figure shows why that misleads: each number comes with its own date, prompt category, max context, engine and definition.

What you can say safely is that, on the same hardware, the prompt category moves decode more than most knobs (GLM from 34 to 72 tok/s between prose and structured; DeepSeek from 38.6 to ~55; in the TensorFold lane, from 60.4 to 114.7). Qwen's prefill (~2,960 tok/s at 32K) is about double that of the other two (~1,430), consistent with 36 of its 48 layers being linear attention and the full-attention ones being sparse. The KV pool depends on the max context and the dtype as much as on the model. You can't say that one recipe is “faster” without pinning down prompt, context, concurrency, date and definition. Nor that an engine is: GLM's 36.1 → 60.4 tok/s of prose between vLLM and TensorFold mixes engine, 4-bit dense weights, FP8 KV, network transport and a clock cap (section 8).
