In the Qwen recipe, a single flag took decode from 24.5 to 52.1 tokens per second without swapping a single weight and without changing the distribution of the generated text. In GLM, the DFlash2 drafter gets an average of 6.71 out of 7 guesses right per step on a counting task (the “structured” prompt of bench_decode.py: “count from 1 to 200”). The trick is called speculative decoding. A cheap model drafts several tokens, the big model verifies all of them at once, and a cleverly designed random draw guarantees that the final text follows exactly the distribution the big model would have produced on its own. In this chapter you'll see why this works (the physics of decode), why nothing is lost (the math of rejection sampling), how much it pays off (acceptance and tokens per step) and what changes inside the engine. By the end, you'll know how to pick the method, the k and the sampling mode for a new recipe. And since the GLM recipe now also runs on another engine, TensorFold, you'll see a second way to verify, by equality with a keyed sample, which preserves not just the distribution but the token sequence itself.

Decode's almost-free lunch

Recall chapter 10. In decode, each step generates one token per sequence and, to do so, reads all of the model's active weights from memory. In each linear layer the computation is a matrix-vector multiplication, the GEMV: an activation vector \(x\) of dimension \(d\) times a weight matrix \(W\) of \(d_{\text{out}}\times d\). Each weight brought in from memory takes part in one multiplication and one addition and is then thrown away.

Now stack \(M\) vectors into a matrix \(X\) of \(M\times d\). The product \(XW^\top\) reads the same matrix \(W\) only once and reuses it \(M\) times. The bytes read don't change; only the arithmetic grows. As long as that arithmetic fits within the read time, processing 8 rows costs practically the same as processing 1. It's the same reason batching makes decode cheaper (chapter 32). The difference is that here the extra rows don't come from other users: they are guesses about the same user's future.

\[ t_{\text{step}}(M) \;\approx\; \max\!\Big(\underbrace{\frac{B_W(M)}{\mathrm{BW}}}_{\text{read the weights}},\ \underbrace{\frac{2\,N_a\,M}{F}}_{\text{do the math}}\Big) + t_{\text{fixed}}, \qquad I(M) = \frac{2\,N_a\,M}{B_W(M)} \]
  • \(t_{\text{step}}\): duration of one target forward with \(M\) rows
  • \(M\): rows (positions) processed in the step; with speculation, \(1+k\) per sequence
  • \(B_W(M)\): weight bytes read; constant in a dense model, growing in an MoE
  • \(\mathrm{BW}\): effective memory bandwidth (the recipes use ~230 GB/s per GB10)
  • \(N_a\): active parameters per token
  • \(F\): peak FLOP/s (~125 dense BF16 TFLOPS on the GB10)
  • \(I(M)\): arithmetic intensity, FLOPs per byte read
  • \(t_{\text{fixed}}\): kernel launches, collectives between nodes, sampler

The step only stops being memory-bound when \(I(M)\) reaches \(F/\mathrm{BW}\), the roofline “knee”, which on the GB10 sits between ~450 and ~550 FLOP/B depending on whether you use the nominal or the effective bandwidth. With BF16 weights (2 bytes per parameter), \(I(M) \approx M\): only with hundreds of rows does the arithmetic take over. Verifying 4, 7 or 8 positions is nowhere near that. The figure shows the step from the inside: the weight matrix is read from memory once per step, and the activation rows (the characters) take turns using it.

Notice three things. In the dense BF16 model, the amber curve stays flat from 1 up to hundreds of rows. With 4-bit weights, the step gets 4× cheaper, but the knee arrives sooner, because each byte now carries more arithmetic. In the MoE, the byte curve climbs from the very start: each row picks 8 of GLM's 288 experts, and different rows call different experts. The expected number of distinct experts per layer, assuming uniform and independent choices (the same approximation used in the inventory), is

\[ D(M) = E\Big[1 - \big(1 - \tfrac{1}{E}\big)^{M\cdot k_r}\Big] \qquad D_{\text{GLM}}(1) \approx 7.9 \qquad D_{\text{GLM}}(8) \approx 57 \]
  • \(E\): routed experts per layer (288 in GLM, 256 in DeepSeek, 512 in Qwen)
  • \(k_r\): experts per token (8 in GLM, 6 in DeepSeek, 10 in Qwen)
  • \(M\,k_r\): expert “slots” requested by the whole step

With 8 rows, GLM reads about 2.3× more weight bytes than with 1 (back-of-the-envelope math, weights only). Speculation still pays off, because ~7.7 tokens come out per step on the counting task, but it pays off less than in a dense model. That's why, in an MoE, low acceptance is punished harder.

Measured in the TensorFold lane

The version of the GLM recipe on TensorFold ships a table of verification costs measured on the two Sparks, built into the draft simulator of patch 0011 (on an engine that predates the later optimizations): 29.2 ms for a 1-row window, 35.6 ms for 2, 48.8 ms for 4 and 66.0 ms for 8. Eight rows cost 2.26× one, in the same range as the ~2.3× in bytes from the back-of-the-envelope estimate above (our comparison: the measured time also includes costs that don't grow with the rows, such as the collectives between the Sparks), and far less than 8×. That margin is what speculation exploits, and section 8 uses the whole table.

Analogy: the bus that leaves no matter what

Decode is a bus that makes the whole trip (reading all the weights) at every stop, carrying a single passenger. Taking 8 passengers barely burns any more diesel. Speculative decoding fills the bus with likely passengers, the drafts, and on arrival the driver, who is the target model, checks who had a ticket. Those who didn't have one get off, no harm done, and the trip cost practically the same.

Why this matters

It's the main decode lever in all three recipes. Qwen with MTP=3 went from 24.5 to 52.1 tok/s (batch 1, greedy, 2.13×). DeepSeek with DSpark k=6 emits ~3.9 tokens per step (TP=3 probe, 48.4% acceptance). GLM with DFlash2 k=7 emits 7.71 tokens per step on the “structured” prompt (count from 1 to 200), versus 3.39 on prose. The gain varies this much because it depends on how often the draft is right, and that's what the rest of the chapter is about.

The loop: draft, verify, accept

Call the big model we want to serve the target, with distribution \(p\), and call a cheap proposer the drafter, with distribution \(q\). A speculative step has four beats:

  1. Propose. The drafter guesses \(k\) tokens \(d_1,\dots,d_k\) from the confirmed text. \(k\) is the num_speculative_tokens of --speculative-config.
  2. Verify. The target runs one forward over \(k+1\) positions: the last confirmed token, which hasn't gone through the target yet, and the \(k\) drafts. With the causal mask, position \(i\) only sees what comes before it. The target then returns \(k+1\) distributions \(p_1,\dots,p_{k+1}\), the same ones it would have computed generating those tokens one at a time.
  3. Accept. From left to right, each draft goes through a test, which is the subject of the next section. At the first failure, the target samples a correction token and the round ends. If they all pass, the target also samples a bonus token from \(p_{k+1}\), which was already computed.
  4. Tidy up. The KV of the rejected positions is discarded (the scheduler had already reserved num_lookahead_tokens = \(k\) slots for them), the drafter updates its own state and the next round starts from the last emitted token.

Each step emits between 1 and \(k+1\) tokens, and never zero: even if the first guess fails, the correction token is a legitimate token from the target. In the worst case, speculation wastes the cost of the draft and of the extra verification rows. Follow a complete round in the figure, with \(k=4\) and a toy sentence.

Look at step 3: the target doesn't “read the drafts one by one”. It processes the five positions together, as it would in a short prefill, and each position produces the next-token distribution conditioned on the previous drafts. That's why, when a draft is rejected, the distributions of the positions after it are no longer valid: they were computed over a prefix that never happened.

Going deeper: “lossless” down to the last decimal place?

Mathematically, one forward with \(k+1\) rows yields the same distributions as \(k+1\) single-row forwards. Numerically, not exactly. Kernels pick different summation orders and code paths for \(M=8\) and for \(M=1\) (a different CUDA graph, a different GEMM tile), and floating-point addition is not associative. Logits can differ in the last bits, and in a near-tie that flips the argmax. vLLM's guarantee is that greedy with speculation should match greedy without speculation up to numerical precision. In practice, compare outputs at \(T=0\) and read “lossless” as “same distribution”, not as “same bits”.

In the recipe: “same bits” by construction

The TensorFold lane of the GLM recipe turns that “up to numerical precision” into a contract. The verify kernels keep each row's arithmetic independent of the other rows in the call (split-K partitioning fixed by the weight's shape, tiles that don't change with \(M\), sums between the two Sparks always in rank order), and this is called batch invariance. Verifying 8 rows then gives the same bits as generating them one at a time. The test is a yes-or-no question: "draft": false asks for serial decoding, and the hash of the generated tokens (token_sha) has to match. The recipe's v1.3 records “drafted == serial” in 6 out of 6 cases. Section 8 shows the other half of the trick, the acceptance rule.

Rejection sampling: changing the draw without changing the distribution

If the request is greedy (temperature 0), verifying is easy: the target accepts the draft when it matches its own argmax. But the recipes' defaults ask for \(T=1.0\) and top_p 0.95 (chapter 9). In that case the target doesn't have “one right answer”, it has a distribution, and we want each emitted token to be an honest sample from \(p\), even though the candidate was sampled from \(q\). The solution was published between late 2022 and early 2023 by two groups, independently (Leviathan and colleagues at Google, and Chen and colleagues at DeepMind). It is speculative rejection sampling:

\[ \Pr[\text{accept } x] = \min\!\Big(1,\ \frac{p(x)}{q(x)}\Big), \qquad r(x) = \frac{\max\big(0,\ p(x)-q(x)\big)}{\sum_{y}\max\big(0,\ p(y)-q(y)\big)} \]
  • \(x\): the draft, sampled from \(q\)
  • \(p(x)\): the probability the target gives \(x\) at that position
  • \(q(x)\): the probability the drafter gave \(x\)
  • \(r\): residual distribution, used only when the draft is rejected

In words: if the drafter was more conservative than the target about \(x\) (\(q(x)\le p(x)\)), the draft is always accepted. If it was too optimistic, it's accepted only with probability \(p/q\). When it rejects, the target samples from the residual, that is, only among the tokens for which it wanted more probability than the drafter offered. The proof that this reproduces \(p\) fits in one line:

\[ \Pr[\text{emit } x] \;=\; \underbrace{q(x)\,\min\!\Big(1,\tfrac{p(x)}{q(x)}\Big)}_{=\ \min(p(x),\,q(x))} \;+\; \underbrace{\Big(1-\textstyle\sum_y \min(p(y),q(y))\Big)}_{=\ \Pr[\text{reject}]}\cdot\, r(x) \;=\; \min(p,q)(x) + \big(p(x)-\min(p,q)(x)\big) \;=\; p(x) \]
  • 1st term: sample \(x\) from \(q\) and accept it
  • 2nd term: reject whichever draft was proposed and sample \(x\) from the residual
  • the trick: \(\sum_y \max(0, p-q) = \sum_y \big(p - \min(p,q)\big) = 1 - \sum_y \min(p,q)\), so the denominator of \(r\) is exactly \(\Pr[\text{reject}]\)

The figure turns the proof into a game of darts. Each dart picks a column of \(q\) (the draft) and a uniform height inside \(q\)'s bar in that column. If the dart lands below the height of \(p\), the green zone, the draft is accepted. If it lands in the red zone, where \(q\) exceeds \(p\), it's rejected, and a second, amber dart is thrown into the zone where \(p\) exceeds \(q\). The histogram at the bottom counts what came out.

Push the misalignment all the way up, for a terrible drafter. The acceptance rate plummets, but the histogram keeps converging to \(p\). That's the central message of the chapter: the drafter's quality never changes what comes out, only how fast. Also turn on “greedy draft”. The drafter then always proposes its favorite, and the method stays lossless (section 5 explains why).

In a round with \(k\) drafts, the test is applied in sequence. Position \(i+1\) is only tested if position \(i\) passed, and the distributions \(p_{i+1}\) and \(q_{i+1}\) are already conditioned on the accepted prefix. By induction, the entire sequence of emitted tokens has the target's distribution, and that includes the bonus token, sampled directly from \(p_{k+1}\). That's why the method is called lossless speculation.

Pitfalls

“Lossless” doesn't mean “same text”. At \(T>0\), speculation consumes random numbers in a different order, so the same seed gives different text, with the same distribution. To test a recipe's correctness, compare at \(T=0\). Not every rejection method is lossless. vLLM has rejection_sample_method: "synthetic", which accepts drafts at prescribed rates (synthetic_acceptance_rates) purely for simulation and benchmarking. The generated text is no longer the target's. The production mode is "standard", and GLM sets it explicitly. The exception that proves the rule: the TensorFold lane of GLM replaces rejection with an equality test under keyed sampling and returns the same text for the same key, with or without drafts (section 8).

How much it pays off: acceptance, tokens per step and choosing k

The chance of accepting a draft at a given position is the green area of the previous figure:

\[ \alpha \;=\; \sum_x \min\big(p(x),\,q(x)\big) \;=\; 1 - \mathrm{TV}(p,q), \qquad \mathrm{TV}(p,q) = \tfrac12\sum_x \big|p(x)-q(x)\big| \]
  • \(\alpha\): acceptance rate at that position (on average, over contexts)
  • \(\mathrm{TV}\): total variation distance, half the sum of the absolute differences

This identity shows up literally in the code. In DeepSeek's Stage-C overlay (lane B, explained just below), the function speculative_acceptance_confidence(q, p) returns \(\sum_x \min(p(x), q(x))\), with the docstring “equivalently 1 − TV(p, q)”. It's also the training target for DSpark's confidence head, as chapter 37 shows.

From α to tokens per step

Suppose, to start, that every position accepts with the same probability \(\alpha\), independently. The \(j\)-th token of the round (\(j = 1,\dots,k+1\)) is only emitted if the \(j-1\) drafts before it passed, which happens with probability \(\alpha^{j-1}\). Adding it up:

\[ \tau(k) \;=\; \mathbb{E}[\text{tokens per step}] \;=\; \sum_{j=1}^{k+1}\alpha^{j-1} \;=\; \frac{1-\alpha^{k+1}}{1-\alpha} \;\xrightarrow{\;k\to\infty\;}\; \frac{1}{1-\alpha} \]
  • \(\tau(k)\): tokens per step, counting the bonus or the correction
  • with \(\alpha = 0.8\): \(\tau(3)=2.95\), and the ceiling, with infinite \(k\), is 5
  • the ceiling \(1/(1-\alpha)\) shows that a large \(k\) won't rescue mediocre acceptance

In real life, acceptance isn't constant: it drops with position. Call \(c_i\) the conditional acceptance of position \(i\), given that the previous ones passed. The fraction of steps in which position \(i\) is accepted, the per-position acceptance that vLLM's /metrics reports, is the survival of the prefix:

\[ a_i = \prod_{j\le i} c_j \qquad\qquad \tau(k) = 1 + \sum_{i=1}^{k} a_i \]
  • \(a_i\): unconditional acceptance of position \(i\), always non-increasing in \(i\)
  • \(c_j\): conditional acceptance; the product is the cumulative_survival of the DeepSeek overlay (lane B)
  • the “1 +” is the token the target always emits (bonus or correction)

With the measured curves: Qwen (greedy, batch 1) accepts 0.89 / 0.745 / 0.60, and \(\tau(3) = 3.24\). DeepSeek Vision-Exp on bench_quick accepts 0.89 / 0.73 / 0.49 / 0.34 / 0.23 / 0.15, and \(\tau(6) \approx 3.83\). GLM accepts 0.98 … 0.83 on the “structured” prompt's counting task and 0.75 … 0.06 on prose. Summed, that curve gives 6.41 and 2.33 accepted drafts per step (\(\tau \approx 7.41\) and \(3.33\)). The average the bench reports is a bit higher, 6.71 and 2.39 (acceptance 0.959 and 0.341). The per-position curve and the average come from different lab measurements and roundings, so they don't agree to the second decimal place. The figures use the curve.

From τ to speedup: the cost of the draft and of the extra rows

Tokens per step are not speedup, because the speculative step is more expensive. Add to the target's step the cost of the draft and the cost of the extra verification rows, both measured in units of “one decode step without speculation”:

\[ S(k) \;\approx\; \frac{\tau(k)}{1 + k\,v + T_{\text{draft}}(k)}, \qquad T_{\text{draft}}(k) = \begin{cases} k\,c & \text{sequential draft (MTP, EAGLE, separate model)}\\ c & \text{block draft (DFlash, DSpark)} \end{cases} \]
  • \(S(k)\): per-sequence decode speedup
  • \(v\): cost of each extra verification row (small in a dense model, larger in an MoE)
  • \(c\): cost of one drafter forward
  • with \(v=0\) and sequential drafting, this is Leviathan's formula: \(\frac{1-\alpha^{k+1}}{(1-\alpha)(kc+1)}\)

Check it against Qwen. With \(\tau = 3.24\) and a measured speedup of 2.13×, the implied denominator is ~1.52: the step with MTP costs ~50% more than the plain step (our interpretation). That's what you'd expect from three forwards of the MTP layer, each ending in an lm_head with 248,320 rows, plus three verification rows. Chapter 36 takes this cost apart and shows how the reduced vocabulary cuts it down.

The rule for deciding \(k\) comes from comparing \(\tfrac{\tau + a_{k+1}}{t + \Delta t}\) with \(\tfrac{\tau}{t}\). Position \(k+1\) only pays off if it brings in proportionally more tokens than the time it adds: \(a_{k+1} > \tau(k)\,\Delta t / t(k)\). Build your intuition with the calculator.

Try three scenarios. With \(\alpha = 0.9\) and a cheap draft, the best \(k\) goes past 8. With \(\alpha = 0.6\), the speedup saturates at \(k \approx 3\) and then drops, because every extra row costs something and is almost never accepted. With block drafting turned on, the draft cost stops growing with \(k\) and the optimum shifts to the right, which is why GLM's DFlash2 can afford to verify 7. Overlay the “GLM prose” profile: the real curve falls faster than the geometric one in the last positions. That's suffix decay. Beware of the easy explanation: the geometric curve is also a product, so “acceptance is prefix-based” isn't enough. The measured curve falls faster because the conditional acceptance \(c_i = a_i/a_{i-1}\) decreases with position. In GLM prose, \(c \approx\) 0.75 / 0.77 / 0.71 / 0.68 / 0.57 / 0.56 (the figure draws these as pink diamonds). The cause depends on the kind of drafter. In autoregressive ones (MTP, EAGLE), error accumulates because each guess builds on the previous ones, which may be wrong. In block drafters (DFlash2), the final positions are predicted from masks, without knowing which tokens came before them inside the block. DFlash2's 2-tap convolutions exist precisely to carry information from one position to the next inside the block (chapter 37).

GLM brings the marginal rule into the runtime with GLM53_ADAPTIVE_K (off by default). Instead of always verifying all 7 positions, it verifies only 2, 4 or 7, based on an exponential moving average (EMA) of recent acceptance. Each length becomes a different step shape, which is why the mode requires capture sizes that are multiples of 3, 5 and 8 (section 7 and chapter 37).

Greedy, probabilistic and temperature

Up to now the drafter sampled from \(q\). vLLM offers two ways for the drafter to pick its token, in the draft_sample_method field, and they change the acceptance test.

Greedy draft: the favorite, treated as a sure thing

With "greedy" (vLLM's default), the draft is the argmax \(\hat x\) of \(q\), and the drafter's distribution is treated as one-hot, that is, 100% on \(\hat x\). Apply the general rule with this \(q\):

\[ q = \mathbb{1}[x=\hat x] \;\Rightarrow\; \Pr[\text{accept}] = \min\!\big(1, p(\hat x)\big) = p(\hat x), \qquad r(x) = \frac{p(x)\,\mathbb{1}[x\ne \hat x]}{1-p(\hat x)} \]
  • \(\hat x\): the drafter's favorite
  • \(\alpha_{\text{greedy}} = p(\hat x)\): acceptance is the probability the target gives to the drafter's favorite
  • on rejection, sample from \(p\) without \(\hat x\), renormalized

It's still lossless: the proof in section 3 holds for any \(q\), including a degenerate one. And it's cheap, because there's no need to store the drafter's probabilities, only the token id. When the request is also greedy (\(T=0\)), \(p\) becomes one-hot at the target's argmax and the rule reduces to greedy verification: accept if the two favorites match. vLLM uses a dedicated kernel for this case, rejection_greedy_sample_kernel, which DeepSeek's warmup compiles for widths from 1 to \(k+1\).

Probabilistic draft: the whole distribution on the scale

With "probabilistic", the drafter samples from \(q\) and stores the full distribution, which goes into the ratio \(p/q\). Acceptance is the overlap area, \(\alpha_{\text{prob}} = 1-\mathrm{TV}(p,q)\). The price is memory: vLLM's docstring warns that this mode uses the full draft logits “at the cost of additional GPU memory usage”. In DeepSeek, with a vocabulary of 129,280, that comes to 6 × 129,280 floats per request per step (our math: ~3 MB in FP32). GLM's DFlash2 sidesteps the problem: the selector writes, via _cache_draft_logits_kernel, the scores of the 16 candidates it considered, with \(-\infty\) everywhere else. \(q\) ends up restricted to 16 tokens.

Which of the two accepts more? It depends on how the drafter gets it wrong:

  • \(p = q = (0.5,\ 0.5)\): probabilistic always accepts (\(\alpha = 1\)); greedy accepts 0.5, because the target only gives the favorite 50%.
  • \(p = (0.9,\ 0.1)\), \(q = (0.55,\ 0.45)\): greedy accepts 0.9, because the favorite is right; probabilistic accepts 0.65, because the drafter “spreads” 45% onto a token the target barely wants.

Rule of thumb: probabilistic wins when the drafter is well calibrated and the target is uncertain. Greedy wins when the drafter gets the favorite right but is too unsure of itself. Which case applies to your target/drafter pair is an empirical question, and you answer it with an A/B test. The figure puts both sides on a scale.

Look at what temperature does. Near \(T=0\), both methods converge to “did it get the favorite right?”, and acceptance peaks when the favorites match. As \(T\) rises, greedy falls along with \(p(\hat x)\), because the target spreads out its probability. Probabilistic depends on how similar \(p\) and \(q\) stay as they spread out; in this toy figure it even recovers at high \(T\), because both drift toward uniform. Set “q confidence” to 0.3 and the temperature to ~0.4: the target becomes decisive, the drafter stays hesitant, and greedy pulls ahead. Go back to \(T=1\) and probabilistic retakes the lead. The Qwen report notes that the benchmark numbers (52.1 tok/s, 72.8% acceptance) are greedy, and that the \(T=1.0\) from generation_config makes rejection sampling accept fewer drafts, so real chat is slower than the bench. One important detail: the distribution that verification preserves is the target sampler's already processed distribution, with temperature, top-k and top-p applied. A smaller top_p shrinks the support of \(p\), and that changes \(\alpha\) too.

Parameters"draft_sample_method":"probabilistic" · "rejection_sample_method":"standard"

DFlash2 samples its drafts from its own distribution, restricted to the selector's 16 candidates, and the target verifies with standard rejection: it accepts with \(\min(1,p/q)\) and resamples from the residual. With the realized scores written into draft_logits, probabilistic verification is lossless at \(T>0\) as well. standard is vLLM's default, but the recipe writes it out on purpose: the alternative, synthetic, doesn't preserve \(p\) and is only good for benchmarking, and v0.30 also added block, verification of the whole block at once, which is lossless too.

In the TensorFold lane, the question changes

There is no draft_sample_method there. Verification is by equality with the target's keyed sample, so the verifier never uses \(q\), and the engine keeps no draft_logits. The role that “greedy vs. probabilistic” plays here becomes a different one: how much of the target's keyed noise the drafter uses when it picks the chain. Weight 0 would be greedy drafting (just the favorite); TensorFold's DFlash2 uses 0.7. Section 8 shows why that weight decides acceptance at \(T>0\). The lane's sparkDash numbers are greedy too (temperature 0), the case in which the two rules coincide.

Going deeper: exporting q in batches at T=0

Only DeepSeek's lane B (Stage-C overlay) has VLLM_DSPARK_EXPORT_DRAFT_PROBS, off (0) by default. In lane A, the default, the variable doesn't exist and does nothing. When on, it stores the FP32 softmax of the draft logits, i.e., the full \(q(x)\) at each position. The proposer only exports when every request in the batch is greedy (\(T=0\), the sampling_metadata.all_greedy). Don't confuse this with the greedy draft of draft_sample_method: here it's the request that is greedy. In that case verification compares argmax with argmax, and the exported \(q\) plays no part in the lossless guarantee; it serves diagnostics and the confidence head, at the cost of one softmax over the vocabulary per position per step. The log explains why the non-greedy path is left out: there, DSpark has to sample the block corrected by the Markov head from left to right, and the exported \(q\) would not be the \(q\) actually used.

The simplest of them all: copying from the prompt

Prompt lookup is the drafter that needs no neural network, and that's exactly why it helps you understand all the others. Take the last \(n\) tokens of the generated text, look for that n-gram in the context and, if you find it, propose as the draft the \(k\) tokens that came after it. If you don't find it with \(n\), try with \(n-1\), down to the minimum. In a “rename the variable” task, the answer is almost entirely a copy of the prompt, and the zero-cost drafter gets whole blocks right.

The steps pay off very unevenly. Where the answer copies the prompt, a single step emits \(k+1\) tokens. At the first occurrence of sum, the guess misses, because the prompt said total, and only the correction comes out. It's the same pattern as in the recipes: very predictable text (a count, code that copies the prompt) makes drafters shine, and free-form prose trips them up. Many neural drafters learn, in part, a generalized version of this “copy from the context”.

Speculation inside the engine: batches, KV and recurrent state

So far we've looked at a single request. The engine serves many at once, and speculation changes the geometry of every step.

A step in which every sequence has the same query length is a uniform decode and can be replayed by a recorded CUDA graph, as long as \(M\) is among the capture sizes (chapter 29). Depth under concurrency in TensorFold

With up to 4 streams (the v1.3.2 limit; up to 8 since v1.5) sharing a window of at most 32 rows, the best depth for a stream on its own is not the best for the batch: a row spent on an unlikely draft from one stream could have gone to a likely draft from another. TensorFold offers joint allocation (TF_GLM_MULTI_DEPTH=joint): it walks each stream's chain, computes each draft's chance of surviving (the product of confidences) and hands the round's rows out, one at a time, to the draft with the highest chance, as long as expected tokens per millisecond keep growing, using a curve of ms per row count measured at startup plus 12 ms of overhead. There is also scale:A, which tightens the stop threshold to \(\min(0.95,\ P\cdot S^A)\) with \(S\) streams. The recipe stays on the default policy (each stream with its own policy, as if it were alone). None of these modes changes a reply's bits, and none has a published number.

KV: throwing it away is easy

The KV cache is organized by position, and that makes rollback trivial. Before the step, the scheduler reserves \(k\) extra slots (num_lookahead_tokens). After verification, the rows for the rejected positions simply stop counting and get overwritten in the next round. With asynchronous scheduling, DeepSeek's scheduler doesn't even know yet which tokens are coming and reserves the space with -1 placeholders, which the worker fills in later. The drafter also has its own memory (DFlash2's KV, DSpark's features), and it has to be pruned the same way. Chapter 37 shows how.

Recurrent state: “un-reading” a token doesn't pay off

Hybrid models complicate this story. A linear attention layer (GLM's KDA, Qwen's Gated DeltaNet) doesn't keep one row per token, but a fixed-size recurrent state, a matrix that summarizes the entire past. Verifying \(k+1\) tokens updates that matrix \(k+1\) times. If only \(j\) drafts are accepted, the final state has absorbed \(k-j\) tokens that never existed.

In theory you could undo it by inverting each update. With the rule in the figure below, \(S \leftarrow \gamma S + v\,k^\top\), the previous state is exactly \(S^{(4)} = \big(S^{(5)} - v_5 k_5^\top\big)/\gamma\). In Gated DeltaNet and KDA, the update \(\alpha S(I-\beta k k^\top) + \beta v k^\top\) is also invertible in exact arithmetic. In practice, it doesn't pay off, for three reasons. It's unstable in floating point: dividing by a small gate amplifies rounding error, and \(I-\beta kk^\top\) can become nearly singular. It requires storing the key, value and gates of every draft. And it costs one inverse update per rejected token per layer. Keeping \(k+1\) copies is exact and cheaper. The causal short convolution's window (the conv_state) is even worse: at every token it drops the oldest column, and that information leaves the window for good.

The solution is to keep snapshots. During verification, the kernel saves the state after each of the \(k+1\) tokens, and the number of accepted tokens, num_accepted_tokens, says which snapshot becomes the official state. The convolution gets a wider window, with \(K-1+k\) columns instead of \(K-1\), so it can cut out the right stretch after the verdict. Watch the mechanism with real values in a toy matrix.

Note that the chosen snapshot is the \((j+1)\)-th: the official state is the one that existed after processing the anchor and the \(j\) accepted drafts. The new token, whether correction or bonus, hasn't gone through the target yet and will be the anchor of the next round. The cost is temporary memory: \(k+1\) matrices per recurrent layer per request for the duration of the step. That's also why kernels have a limit on \(k\).

  • Speculative tokens carry num_spec + 1 state columns, and num_accepted_tokens picks the final one
  • conv_state with \(K-1+k\) = 3 + 7 = 10 columns (kernel 4, DFlash2 \(k=7\))
  • In align mode, the per-request reservation is 1 + max_concurrent_batches + num_speculative_blocks = 10 pages (it used to be 9). The breakdown \(1 + 2 + 7\) is ours: max_concurrent_batches = 2 with asynchronous scheduling and num_speculative_blocks = 7

  • The conv's speculative path rewinds the window by num_accepted_tokens − 1, just like causal_conv1d_update

  • No recurrent layers in the target: the compressed KV and the SWA ring are per position

The other way out: two parities and replay

The TensorFold lane of GLM takes no snapshots. Each stream has only two copies of the KDA state, the parities: the verify window reads the current parity and writes to the other, and the confirmed state is never touched by drafts. If every row is accepted, flipping the parity is enough. If only some are accepted, the engine recomputes the recurrence of the kept rows from the keys, values, gates and \(\beta\) saved per row (rollback by replay) and shifts the conv window with those rows' projections. It is the same refusal to “un-read” from the figure, solved by trading memory for compute. With windows of up to 16 rows, snapshots would cost 16 copies of the state per stream, and each copy is ~71 MB per rank (34 KDA layers × 32 heads × 128 × 128 × 4 bytes, our arithmetic). The parities cost two copies and a per-row scratch, and the replay only runs when acceptance is partial, and only over the kept rows.

A third way out: commit the state later

Two more recent recipes of Mia's, also on TensorFold, take the parities one step further: instead of replaying the accepted rows right after the verdict, they defer that work to the next forward pass, a lazy commit of the recurrent state. In Qwen3.8 Flash Next on a single Spark, patch 0012 (TF_FLASHNEXT_KEEP_LATE=1, on by default) makes a partly rejected window stop replaying the Gated DeltaNet state: the kept rows wait in a log, and the next verify pass applies them before its own. It saves about 3 ms on a 5-stream round, with the same bits. In Nemotron-3.5-Bolt (40 Mamba layers), SSM_LAZY=1, also the default, goes further and removes the second copy. The verify window writes no state at all, keep(n) only records \(n\), and the stream's next forward folds the \(n\) saved rows (the scan inputs) in at the start of its scan, writes the state once and carries on with its own rows. Each stream now reserves 0.28 GiB instead of 0.41; at 16 streams that's ~270 MB less traffic per stream per round, ~19 ms of a 220 ms round by the engine's own bandwidth figures. Measured on 2026-10-19 (the same prompt for N clients, 256 tokens each, medians of 3, thinking off): +3–8% tokens per second at 4 to 16 streams in 11 of 12 cells, no change with a single stream, and replies identical token for token (token_sha256). Exactness holds because the arithmetic order is the same: the same rows, in the same order. Beyond the Spark: on any hybrid-model server with speculation (vLLM or SGLang on an H200, with Mamba or Gated DeltaNet layers), the per-request recurrent state caps concurrency as much as the KV does, and swapping whole copies of the state for a small log of inputs plus a deferred commit is a memory and bandwidth lever that doesn't depend on the hardware (our interpretation).

Three neighbors that also need to be able to roll back

  • Grammars. With structured output, the XGrammar automaton has to accept, validate and roll back speculative tokens. An accepted batch can run past the terminal token or past a </think> in the middle of the window. The backports of #52805 and #53046 fix this: in GLM, via patch_xgrammar_termination.py, always on; in DeepSeek, via DSPARK_ENABLE_ISSUE136_XGRAMMAR_HOTFIX, opt-in, combined with hotfix-dsv4-grammar-advance.sh, which advances the grammar using exactly the tokens appended in the step (chapter 34). In the TensorFold lane, before each verify forward the engine cuts the drafts the grammar rejects and then masks the logits of each remaining row, so the constrained reply also equals the serial one.
  • Logit masks. A bad_words list or a tool_choice: none also has to apply to the draft positions during verification, or else a forbidden token slips in as accepted. GLM's tool_choice patch acts on them.
  • Streaming. One step can emit several tokens, and the server packs them into the same SSE chunk. DeepSeek's evaluation measured an undercount of ~4.5× for anyone who counts delta.content events instead of usage.completion_tokens.

Update: exact speculation in TensorFold

The GLM recipe has a second version. The checkpoint is the same (GLM-5.3-Flash-EXL3-TR3-4bpw), the drafter is the same DFlash2 and the machines are the same two DGX Sparks, but the engine is no longer vLLM: it's TensorFold v0.6.0 with the recipe's 53 patches. Chapter 48 explains the engine, and chapter 49, the recipe. For speculation, the change is a deep one. Everything we've seen so far guarantees that the output has the target's distribution. TensorFold guarantees something stronger: with the same key, the drafted reply is, bit for bit, the reply the engine would give generating one token at a time. This section shows the acceptance rule behind that guarantee, what it costs in acceptance, how the recipe decides how many drafts to propose each round and what changes inside the engine.

Verifying is comparing: the equality rule

The ingredient is the keyed sampling from chapter 9. To recap: the target samples the token at position \(t\) with the Gumbel-max trick, \(x_t = \arg\max_v \big(z_{t,v}/T + g_{t,v}\big)\), and the nudge \(g_{t,v} = -\log(-\log u)\) comes from a hash \(u = H(\text{key}, t, v)\) of the seed (or the prompt), the absolute position and the token id. No stream of random numbers is being consumed. The noise for each (position, token) pair is fixed before anyone proposes anything. A round then goes like this:

  1. Propose. The drafter hands over a chain \(d_1,\dots,d_k\), copied from the context or coming from DFlash2, with no probabilities.
  2. Verify. One forward over the window [pending token, \(d_1,\dots,d_k\)] gives the logits of the \(k+1\) positions, and each row is sampled with the key at its absolute position: \(x_{t+1},\dots,x_{t+k+1}\).
  3. Accept. Keep \(d_j\) while \(d_j = x_{t+j}\). At the first mismatch, that row's sample comes out as the correction; if all pass, the last row's sample is the bonus. There is no extra draw and no residual.

Why does this give back the serial reply? The sample of row \(j\) is a deterministic function of that row's logits and the key, and the logits only depend on the prefix. If \(d_1,\dots,d_{j-1}\) were accepted, the prefix of row \(j\) is exactly the serial decoding's, and \(x_{t+j}\) is exactly the serial token. The drafter only decides how many serial tokens come out per round, never which ones. For this to hold in bits, and not just in the math, the second half comes in: the batch invariance from section 2, which keeps a row's logits from depending on the other rows. This is equality verification.

\[ \underbrace{\Pr[\text{accept } d] = \min\!\Big(1,\ \frac{p(d)}{q(d)}\Big)}_{\text{rejection (vLLM)}} \qquad\text{versus}\qquad \underbrace{\text{accept } d \iff d = \arg\max_v\big(z_v/T + g_v\big)}_{\text{equality (TensorFold)}} \]
  • rejection: preserves the distribution \(p\); needs \(q(d)\) and extra random numbers (the test and the residual); the text changes with the drafter, the batch and the order of the draws
  • equality: preserves the serial sequence for that key; doesn't use \(q\); the text is the same for any drafter, stop rule or batch
  • at \(T=0\), both rules ask the same question (“did it hit the argmax?”) and coincide

Deep dive: how much equality accepts

Rejection accepts \(\sum_v\min(p_v,q_v) = 1-\mathrm{TV}(p,q)\), and that is the ceiling for any rule that makes a draft taken from \(q\) coincide with a sample from \(p\). Equality gets close to it or not depending on the noise. With independent noise, the chance of coinciding at a position is \(\sum_v p_v q_v\), which can fall far short: with \(p=q\) uniform over 10 tokens, it's 0.1 against 1. With the same nudge on both sides (\(w=1\)), the two Gumbel races are coupled, and the exact count is \(\Pr[\text{coincide}] = \sum_i \big(\sum_j \max(p_j/p_i,\ q_j/q_i)\big)^{-1}\). It equals 1 when \(q=p\), never exceeds \(1-\mathrm{TV}\) and, by a 2024 result on “drafter-invariant” speculation (Daliri, Musco and Suresh), never drops below \(\frac{1-\mathrm{TV}}{1+\mathrm{TV}}\). With \(\mathrm{TV}=0.2\), rejection accepts 0.80 and the coupling at least 0.67. That's the honest price of equality, and it's why TensorFold's DFlash2 uses the target's noise when picking the chain (with weight 0.7, a TensorFold constant; neither the recipe nor the code we read explains how the value was chosen). In exchange, the verifier doesn't need \(q\): no per-position draft_logits and no residual. And, as in any lossless speculation, approximating the drafter doesn't put the reply at risk; TensorFold drafts with 4-bit copies of DFlash2 and of the head, and that can only move acceptance, never a single bit of the output.

How many drafts per round: fc, fnc and fcost

In the vLLM lane, \(k\) is fixed (7 on GLM) or chosen by a moving average of acceptance (GLM53_ADAPTIVE_K, section 4). TensorFold decides every round, looking at the chain itself. DFlash2 walks the depths and gives each pick a confidence \(\hat c_j\): the estimated chance that the target agrees, given that the previous drafts passed. It is a predicted conditional acceptance, and the product \(\prod_{i\le j}\hat c_i\) is the survival from section 4, only predicted for this chain. The stop rules, which the engine calls policies, use that product:

fcN:P
up to \(N\) drafts while the product stays at \(P\) or above, with the drafter's noise-free confidences. It was the old default: fc5:0.3.
fncN:P
the same rule with noise-aware confidences, \(\hat c_j = \mathrm{softmax}\big((\text{score} + g)/\beta\big)\) at the chosen candidate, with the target's own keyed noise \(g\) and \(\beta = 2.0\) (the full formula is in chapter 9). It is the recipe's default: fnc7:0.3.
fcostN:noisy
the cost-based stop: walks the whole chain and cuts it at the \(k\) that maximizes expected tokens per millisecond, with a cost table measured when the engine starts.
\[ k^\star = \arg\max_{0\le k\le N}\ \frac{E[\text{tokens}\mid k]}{\text{ms}(k)}, \qquad E[\text{tokens}\mid k] = 1 + \sum_{j=1}^{k}\ \prod_{i=1}^{j}\hat c_i \]
  • \(\hat c_i\): noise-aware confidence of the \(i\)-th draft, a predicted conditional acceptance
  • \(E[\text{tokens}\mid k]\): the \(\tau = 1 + \sum_i a_i\) from section 4, now predicted for this chain
  • \(\text{ms}(k)\): cost of a round with \(k\) drafts (verifying \(k+1\) rows and running the drafter's block), read from a table calibrated at startup

It is section 4's marginal rule taken to its limit: instead of one \(k\) for the average traffic, one \(k\) per round. Since the reply doesn't depend on the policy, you can pit the rules against each other on the same chains, with the real cost table from TensorFold's simulator. That's what the figure does.

Three things show up. In very predictable text (counting), cutting barely matters: the chains survive, and “always 7” ties with the rules that cut. In prose, always 7 pays for rows that are almost never accepted, and the rules that cut win. And confidences that underestimate acceptance, like the noise-free ones when the drafter picks with the target's noise, stop the chain too early, which is why fc5 falls behind (our interpretation of the why; in counting, its ceiling of 5 drafts also weighs in). In the recipe, fnc7:0.3 took prose from 47.2 to 50.6 tok/s and code from 51.7 to 55.5 tok/s over fc5:0.3, with two boots each. Under any of them, the reply is the same.

Deep dive: exactness becomes a lab

Tuning stop rules by measuring live is expensive and noisy: each boot varies a little, and the gains being chased are a few percent. TensorFold does it differently. With TF_GLM_DRAFT_DUMP, the request decodes one token per round (the reply is the serial one) and records, for each position, what the DFlash2 block would have proposed (candidates, scores, selector edges, the keyed Gumbel draws) and the token the target committed. A round that starts at position \(i\) and proposes a chain keeps exactly the drafts that equal the committed tokens, so any policy can be replayed exactly, offline, on the CPU, in NumPy, with a cost table turning rounds into tok/s. That is the draft simulator. It comes with an oracle (the hindsight-optimal depths) as the ceiling and measures the calibration of the acceptance predictors (log loss, Brier, AUC). That's how \(\beta = 2.0\) came out: fitted by log loss on 9 logs from 2026-09-30, 7 of sampled prose and 2 of greedy code. With rejection, the same replay would only give a statistical estimate, because a chain's acceptance depends on draws that haven't happened yet (our interpretation).

When each rule makes sense

Choose by need. If you need reproducibility (hash-based regression tests, auditing, resuming a request without resending tokens, tuning policies offline), equality under keyed sampling delivers all of that, on one condition: the whole engine has to be batch-invariant, which constrains the kernels and moves sampling to the host. If you need maximum acceptance with any engine and any kernel, rejection is optimal: \(1-\mathrm{TV}\) is the ceiling for any rule, and vLLM implements it without asking anything of the kernels. At \(T=0\), which is how the benchmarks of both GLM lanes are measured, the two rules coincide. The speed difference between the lanes therefore comes from the drafting policies (copies, a stop decided every round) and from the rest of the engine, and even that difference wasn't measured in a controlled A/B: dates, configurations and tools differ (chapter 49).

In the recipes: assemble, measure and monitor

Everything we've seen fits into one JSON. Here are the three setups, with every parameter from this chapter:

ParameterSPEC_METHOD=dflash → --speculative-config

SPEC_METHOD (default dflash in .env.example; alternatives mtp and none) picks the speculator, and start.sh builds the JSON:

{"method":"dflash","model":"$DFLASH_MODEL_DIR","num_speculative_tokens":7,
 "kv_cache_dtype":"auto","draft_sample_method":"probabilistic",
 "rejection_sample_method":"standard","draft_tensor_parallel_size":2}

\(k=7\) (DFLASH_TOKENS=7) because the trained block has 8 positions (anchor + 7). The drafter's KV stays at auto, which here means BF16. The reason: the target's fp8_ds_mla is an MLA-only layout, which doesn't work for the drafter's dense attention, and SM121 has no FA3/FA4 for plain FP8 KV. The drafter runs at TP=2 (DFLASH_DRAFT_TP) because vLLM only accepts a draft TP equal to 1 or equal to the target's. With 2, the drafter's ~2.3 GiB are split across the two nodes. In the TP3 kit the value drops to 1, because the drafter's 32 query heads and 8 KV heads aren't divisible by 3. With this setup, the 2026-08-30 bench gave 65.1 tok/s on the “structured” prompt, which is counting from 1 to 200 (acceptance 0.959, 6.71 per step), and 27.1 on prose (0.341, 2.39 per step). The README records 62.9 tok/s on a sparkDash “Structured/Code” run from 2026-08-28. The rollback is SPEC_METHOD=mtp ./start.sh restart, which generates {"method":"mtp","num_speculative_tokens":2} with the native MTP layer: ~24.6 tok/s (the baseline cited in the README), the safe fallback.

Parameter--speculative-config (method dspark)

The drafter comes built into the checkpoint (mtp.*). \(k\) comes from MTP_NUM_TOKENS and the sampling method from DRAFT_SAMPLE_METHOD. In lane A, the default, that code isn't on the path and the env is ignored with the warning Unknown vLLM environment variable. ParametersDRAFTER=dflash2 · DRAFT_POLICY=fnc7:0.3 · COPY=1 · COPY_MAX=15 · COPY_CODE=1

The TensorFold lane builds no JSON. The recipe's config.sh becomes engine flags and variables on both ranks (--drafter, TF_GLM_DFLASH_POLICY, TF_GLM_COPY_*, TF_GLM_WIDE_GRAPHS=16), and the two ranks compare these settings at startup and refuse to come up if they differ. With all of this and the rest of the engine, sparkDash (temperature 0, GPU clocks capped at 2,200 MHz, default configuration with 4 streams) measured 60.4 tok/s on prose and 114.7 on structured with one request, and 108.8 and 227.9 tok/s aggregate with four. Acceptance as a vital sign

The acceptance rate is the most sensitive health signal of a recipe that uses speculation, because anything that pulls \(q\) away from \(p\), or that breaks verification, shows up there before it shows up in a quality benchmark. vLLM exposes the spec decode metrics on /metrics: vllm:spec_decode_num_drafts_total (proposals), num_draft_tokens_total (drafts), num_accepted_tokens_total and num_accepted_tokens_per_pos_total{position="i"}. They are cumulative Prometheus counters, so the recipes always measure by delta: \(a_i = \Delta\text{accepted}_i/\Delta\text{drafts}\).

  • spec-acceptance.py (DeepSeek). Reads the counters, fires a burst following the MiaAI methodology (unique, cold prefixes, min = max = 128 tokens, ignore_eos, thinking off; --trials 5 --prompt 256), reads them again and prints the overall acceptance and the curve for that window. It anchors on the _total series, because the sibling _created series carries a timestamp. The recipe's evaluation warns: a repeated-word prompt drags the curve from 0.93 → 0.33 down to 0.72 → 0.11 and looks like a ~45 tok/s regression that doesn't exist. An abliterated checkpoint genuinely drains acceptance (0.585 versus 0.718).
  • spec-accept-gate.sh (GLM). Must be run by hand after every boot with graphs (start.sh doesn't call it), once there are at least 100 drafts. SKIP below that; FAIL if the position-0 ratio goes above 0.999, i.e., pinned at ~1.00; exit 2 if it can't judge (endpoint down, series missing or ambiguous, unreadable value). The target is vllm#53030: a BatchDescriptor collision in PIECEWISE graphs that can pin acceptance at exactly 1.00 at every position. The script only judges position 0; it prints the ratios of the other positions so you can check the decay yourself. A healthy curve decays. A verifier that accepts everything isn't verifying.
  • Qwen. The 89 / 74.5 / 60% curve doubles as the sanity test for the per-block FP8 scales of the MTP's experts. A wrong block would show up as near-random acceptance, not as a crash.
  • GLM · TensorFold. The vllm:spec_decode_* series don't exist in this lane. The correctness signal becomes a hash: the same request with "draft": false must return the same token_sha. A mismatch doesn't point at a bad drafter, which would only make the reply slower, but at something that broke exactness, such as a kernel that stopped being row-independent (our interpretation). The recipe publishes the end-to-end checks: drafted = serial in 6 out of 6 cases and concurrency in 22 out of 22.

Checklist: speculation for your next recipe

  1. See what the checkpoint ships with. MTP layers (num_nextn_predict_layers), a drafter directory (dflash/), a published EAGLE? A drafter trained together with the target almost always beats a generic one. The MiMo in the chapter 46 tutorial ships two: a 3-layer MTP (\(k \le 3\) uses each layer once) and a 5-layer DFlash with block 8 (\(k \le 7\)).
  2. Choose \(k\) by the training, not by wishful thinking. Block drafter: use the trained length, and read the checkpoint's convention. In DFlash the block is anchor + masks, so block 8 gives \(k=7\) (GLM). In DSpark, the trained shape is \(k\) = dspark_block_size (5 in DeepSeek), and a smaller \(k\) truncates the block; DeepSeek uses 6 by default because, without the block-k hotfix, lane A also requires \(k\) to be a multiple of 3. MTP: with \(k \le n_{\text{predict}}\), each layer is used once. With \(k > n_{\text{predict}}\), the layers are reapplied, \(k\) has to be a multiple of \(n_{\text{predict}}\) and acceptance at the reapplied positions tends to drop (Qwen: 1 layer × 3). In DeepSeek, lane A applies the same rule to DSpark (\(n_{\text{predict}} = 3 \Rightarrow k = 6\)), but the 3 stages run only once, in a single block forward; the loss comes from the 6th position, a noise query beyond the trained block of 5. Also check the divisibility rules of your vLLM version.
  3. Leave rejection_sample_method at standard and decide draft_sample_method with an A/B test, on your own traffic and at the temperature your clients use. In an exact engine such as TensorFold the rule is a different one (equality under keyed sampling), and the A/B becomes one between stop policies, which don't even change the reply.
  4. Fit the machine to \(k\). Capture sizes that are multiples of \(k+1\) up to \(S(k+1)\), rounding the ceiling up; kernel buffers (like EXL3_TEMP_ROWS_FUSED) ≥ \(S(k+1)\); in hybrids, check that the recurrent kernel accepts \(k\) speculative tokens.
  5. Measure the curve, not the average. Per-position acceptance by delta, with real, declared prompts (prose, code, real JSON; a count from 1 to 200 measures the ceiling, not your traffic). Apply the marginal rule to each position, and put a sanity gate at boot.
