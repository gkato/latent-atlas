GLM-5.3-Flash stores 321 billion parameters, but each token it generates uses about 18 billion. DeepSeek-V4-Flash stores 284 B and uses 13 B per token. Qwen3.8-Flash-Next has 125 B in its backbone and uses some 6 B. The trick behind these numbers is Mixture-of-Experts (MoE): each layer's big feed-forward network becomes hundreds of small networks, the experts, and a router picks, token by token, which few of them get to work. In this chapter you will take this mechanism apart piece by piece: router, score functions, top-k, combination weights, shared expert, load balancing, hash routing and the effect of all of it on inference. By the end you should be able to read the MoE lines of a config.json (n_routed_experts, num_experts_per_tok, moe_intermediate_size, scoring_func, topk_method, norm_topk_prob, routed_scaling_factor, n_group, first_k_dense_replace, num_hash_layers…) and predict how much each one costs in memory, in bytes per step and in work for the engine.

Capacity without proportional cost

Start with what you already know from the Transformer block. After attention, each token goes through a feed-forward network (FFN), which in all three models is a SwiGLU with three matrices:

\[ \begin{gathered} \mathrm{FFN}(\mathbf x) = W_{\text{down}}\,\big(\mathrm{SiLU}(W_{\text{gate}}\,\mathbf x) \odot W_{\text{up}}\,\mathbf x\big) \\ W_{\text{gate}}, W_{\text{up}} \in \mathbb R^{I\times d}, \quad W_{\text{down}} \in \mathbb R^{d\times I} \end{gathered} \]
  • \(\mathbf x \in \mathbb R^{d}\): the token vector (\(d\) = hidden_size: 4096 in GLM and DeepSeek, 2560 in Qwen)
  • \(I\): the intermediate width, the number of hidden “neurons” in the FFN
  • \(\odot\): element-wise product; the SiLU branch works as a gate that decides how much of each neuron gets through

In GLM's first three layers this FFN is dense, with \(I = 12{,}288\): that is \(3 \times 4096 \times 12{,}288 \approx 151\) million parameters, and every token goes through all of them. In a classic dense Transformer the FFN holds about two thirds of each block's parameters (\(8d^2\) versus \(4d^2\) for attention). There is an intuitive, widely shared reading of what these neurons do: each one is a pattern detector that “writes” something into the residual stream when it fires. For any given token, most of them barely fire. So why compute them all?

MoE's answer is conditional computation. Cut the FFN into \(E\) smaller FFNs, the experts, and let a router pick \(k\) of them for each token. The layer's output becomes a weighted sum:

\[ \begin{gathered} \mathbf y = \sum_{e \in \mathcal T(\mathbf x)} g_e(\mathbf x)\, \mathrm{E}_e(\mathbf x) + \sum_{s=1}^{S} \mathrm{Shared}_s(\mathbf x) \\ |\mathcal T(\mathbf x)| = k \end{gathered} \]
  • \(\mathrm E_e\): expert \(e\), a small SwiGLU of width \(I\) = moe_intermediate_size
  • \(\mathcal T(\mathbf x)\): the set of \(k\) experts chosen by the router (\(k\) = num_experts_per_tok)
  • \(g_e(\mathbf x)\): the combination weight the router gives each chosen expert (section 3)
  • \(\mathrm{Shared}_s\): shared experts, which run for every token (\(S\) = n_shared_experts, section 4)

The figure below plays this transformation in slow motion. Use the steps to watch the dense FFN break apart into experts, the router hand out ratings, top-k light up a few of them and the weighted sum send the result back to the residual stream.

Look at step 4: the token only goes through two of the eight blocks, yet the layer still “knows” everything the eight know. Total parameters grow with \(E\); cost per token grows with \(k\). That is what capacity without proportional cost means. Each GLM and DeepSeek expert is a \(4096 \to 2048 \to 4096\) SwiGLU with \(3 \times 4096 \times 2048 = 25{,}165{,}824\) parameters; each Qwen expert, \(2560 \to 640 \to 2560\), has 4,915,200.

Analogy: the hospital with triage

A hospital does not send every patient through all 288 specialists. A triage nurse (the router) reads the symptoms and refers the patient to the 8 best suited. The general practitioner (the shared expert) sees everyone, because part of the care is common to every case. The hospital has a lot of knowledge stored (total parameters), but each visit takes up the time of only a few doctors (active parameters).

Fine-grained experts: many and small

All three models use fine-grained experts: hundreds of narrow experts instead of a few wide ones. The DeepSeek lineage shows the trend: V2 had 160 experts of width 1536 (top-6, 2 shared); V3 moved to 256 of width 2048 (top-8, 1 shared); V4-Flash kept 256 of 2048 with top-6. One argument in favor is combinatorial. With top-8 out of 288, GLM can form about \(\binom{288}{8} \approx 1.1 \times 10^{15}\) different combinations of experts per layer; Qwen, with top-10 out of 512, reaches \(\approx 3 \times 10^{20}\); DeepSeek, top-6 out of 256, \(\approx 3.7 \times 10^{11}\) (our calculation). More combinations allow finer specialization at the same cost per token.

Total × active: the math that drives the recipe

Split an MoE's parameters into three groups: the routed experts, the shared ones and “everything else” (attention, norms, embedding, lm_head, routers). With \(L\) MoE layers and \(P_e = 3\,d\,I\) parameters per expert:

\[ \begin{gathered} N_{\text{total}} = N_{\text{rest}} + L\,(E + S)\,P_e \\ N_{\text{active}} = N_{\text{rest}} + L\,(k + S)\,P_e \end{gathered} \]
  • \(L\): layers with MoE (42 in GLM, 48 in Qwen, 43 in DeepSeek)
  • \(E, k, S\): routed experts, experts chosen per token, and shared experts
  • \(P_e = 3\,d\,I\): parameters of one SwiGLU expert (gate, up and down)
  • \(N_{\text{rest}}\): everything that is not an expert; it runs for every token

With the real numbers, the asymmetry jumps out. In GLM, the routed experts add up to 304.4 B in the backbone and 311.65 B with the MTP layer, that is, 97.0% of the checkpoint's 321.3 B, and per token only \(42 \times 8 \times 25.17\text{M} = 8.46\) B of them run; the exact count from the shapes of the checkpoint's tensors (our calculation) gives ≈ 17.4 B active with embedding and lm_head, and the repository's roofline table, ≈ 19.5 B. In Qwen, 120.8 B of the backbone's 125.7 B (96%) are experts, and the active parameters come to ≈ 6.0 B (7.3 B counting embedding and lm_head). In DeepSeek, ≈ 277 B of the 284.3 B (97.4%) are routed experts, with ≈ 13.2 B active, not counting the DSpark drafter (≈ 19.8 B) and vision (≈ 0.47 B). The calculator in the last section lets you redo these numbers yourself.

Why this matters

Memory follows the total: the 288 experts of each layer have to live in RAM even if a token uses 8. FLOPs per token follow the active count (≈ 2 × active). And in decode, which is bound by memory bandwidth (the previous chapter), what counts is how many bytes of expert weights are actually read at each step. That is why all three recipes quantize precisely the experts to ~4 bits: 4 bpw EXL3 only on GLM's routed experts, NVFP4 on Qwen's experts, MXFP4 on DeepSeek's (Part IV, starting at Why and how to quantize).

The router: ratings, score functions and top-k

The router is just a linear layer without bias, \(W_r \in \mathbb R^{E \times d}\), whose rows are a kind of “portrait” of each expert. The dot product between the token and each row gives a rating, the affinity logit:

\[ \begin{gathered} \boldsymbol\ell = W_r\,\mathbf x \in \mathbb R^{E}, \qquad s_i = f(\ell_i) \\ \mathcal T = \operatorname{TopK}_k\big(\mathbf s + \mathbf b\big) \end{gathered} \]
  • \(W_r\): the router weights, mlp.gate.weight in the checkpoint: [288, 4096] in GLM, [512, 2560] in Qwen, [256, 4096] in DeepSeek
  • \(f\): the score function (scoring_func)
  • \(\mathbf b\): the balancing correction bias, which enters only the selection (zero when absent; section 5)
  • \(\operatorname{TopK}_k\): indices of the \(k\) largest values

The router is cheap: in GLM it is \(288 \times 4096 \approx 1.18\) M parameters per layer, 0.05 B for the whole model. But it makes a discrete decision, and discrete decisions amplify small errors. The three recipes use three different score functions:

\[ \begin{gathered} \underbrace{s_i = \frac{e^{\ell_i}}{\sum_{j=1}^{E} e^{\ell_j}}}_{\text{softmax (Qwen)}} \qquad \underbrace{s_i = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-\ell_i}}}_{\text{sigmoid (GLM)}} \\[4pt] \underbrace{s_i = \sqrt{\ln\!\big(1 + e^{\ell_i}\big)}}_{\text{sqrtsoftplus (DeepSeek-V4)}} \end{gathered} \]
  • softmax: the scores sum to 1 and compete with each other; raising one expert lowers all the others
  • sigmoid: each expert answers “am I relevant?” on its own, with a score in \((0,1)\) that saturates near 1
  • sqrtsoftplus: the square root of softplus; it equals \(\sqrt{\ln 2} \approx 0.83\) at \(\ell=0\), grows like \(\sqrt{\ell}\) for large logits and never saturates

In the figure, the token vector multiplies the router matrix row by row. Switch the token to see other experts win, switch the function to see how it reshapes the ratings (the small chart shows each logit on the curve of \(f\)) and turn on the bias to see the selection change while the weights stay the same.

A simple calculation shows the difference between the functions. Take two chosen experts with logits 4 and 2. How much more does the first weigh than the second after normalization? With softmax, \(e^{4-2} \approx 7.4\) times. With sigmoid, \(0.982 / 0.881 \approx 1.11\): saturation almost erases the difference in confidence. With sqrtsoftplus, \(2.005 / 1.458 \approx 1.37\) (our calculations). The square root compresses high logits without flattening them completely. One interpretation is that it sits halfway between softmax's explosive competition and sigmoid's saturation, but that is our reading: the V4 report does not justify the switch in these terms.

Deep dive: the kernel needs to know the function

Inference engines do not compute routing with three separate PyTorch calls; they use fused kernels that do score, bias, top-k, renormalization and scaling in one go. That is why the function is part of the kernel's contract. In DeepSeek, FlashInfer's b12x backend (--moe-backend flashinfer_b12x) receives RoutingMethodType.DeepseekV4, which identifies sqrtsoftplus with bias. If a kernel assumed sigmoid instead of sqrtsoftplus, hypothetically the model would load and run without a single error: the biased selection and the weights would change, and quality would drop by an amount only measurement can reveal (nobody has measured this case). It is the kind of defect only quality measurement (KLD and parity) catches.

Combining the voices: normalization, scaling and precision

Once the \(k\) experts are chosen, it remains to decide how much each one gets to say. In all three models, the weights come from the scores without the bias, go through an optional renormalization (norm_topk_prob) and a scaling (routed_scaling_factor):

\[ \begin{gathered} g_e = \rho \cdot \frac{s_e}{\sum_{j \in \mathcal T} s_j} \quad (e \in \mathcal T) \\ \mathbf y = \sum_{e \in \mathcal T} g_e\,\mathrm E_e(\mathbf x) + \mathrm{Shared}(\mathbf x) \end{gathered} \]
  • \(s_e\): the score of the chosen expert (without the balancing bias)
  • the division by \(\sum s_j\) only happens with norm_topk_prob: true; the routed weights then sum to exactly 1
  • \(\rho\): routed_scaling_factor (2.5 in GLM, 1.5 in DeepSeek-V4; absent, and therefore 1, in Qwen)

Why renormalize? With sigmoid, eight high scores can add up to nearly 8; with sqrtsoftplus, the sum of six also comfortably exceeds 1. Without normalization, the “volume” of the routed part would depend on how confident the router is about that particular token. With it, the routed part always has mass 1, and the factor \(\rho\) sets how loud it speaks relative to the shared expert and the residual stream. \(\rho\) is a hyperparameter fixed before training (it lives in config.json, not in the weights); the model was trained with that value, so the engine must apply it exactly, no more, no less. With softmax there is an elegant identity: renormalizing the \(k\) largest is equivalent to applying softmax only to the \(k\) chosen logits, because the common denominator cancels out.

DeepSeek-V2's history helps make sense of the factor. It used softmax over 160 experts without renormalizing, and the 6 chosen summed to well under 1; its routed_scaling_factor was 16. V3 moved to sigmoid + renormalization with a factor of 2.5, and V4 to sqrtsoftplus + renormalization with 1.5. In V2, the factor of 16 made up for the small mass of the 6 non-renormalized softmax weights. In V3 and V4, with renormalization, the weights already sum to 1 whatever the function, and \(\rho\) only sets the volume of the routed part relative to the shared expert and the residual; the reports do not justify choosing 2.5 versus 1.5.

The “deviation” indicator is the question that matters to whoever serves the model: what changes in the output if the engine gets one detail of the contract wrong? Turning off normalization in GLM multiplies the routed part by ~6 (the sum of the 8 sigmoids) and the deviation goes through the roof. Forgetting the factor \(\rho\) shrinks the routed part to 40%. Both cases are blatant. The dangerous one is the subtle error.

Real pitfall: the alias that routed 7 instead of 8

The GLM checkpoint uses the field name num_experts_per_tok, inherited from SGLang, and vLLM's configuration class had the default num_experts_per_token = 7. Without an alias that read the checkpoint's name, the model would route 7 experts per token instead of the 8 it was trained with. There is no error and no crash: the 7 weights are renormalized, sum to 1 and the output “looks” right. The comment in vLLM's code records exactly this (“routing 7 experts/token instead of the trained 8”). The moral for anyone building recipes: check in the boot log the values the engine actually read from the config.

Precision: the router is not quantized

Because the selection is discrete, two experts with nearly tied scores can swap places because of a rounding error, and swapping an expert changes the layer's entire output, not just one bit. Hence: GLM computes the router logits in FP32 (moe_router_dtype: float32), Qwen does the softmax in FP32, and DeepSeek asks FusedMoE for router_logits_dtype=torch.float32. And none of the recipes' quantized checkpoints touch the routers: NVIDIA's NVFP4 and Qwen's FP8-dense leave mlp.gate and shared_expert_gate in BF16, as does GLM's EXL3 checkpoint.

DeterminismVLLM_MOE_DET_FINALIZE=1

The sum \(\sum g_e\,\mathrm E_e(\mathbf x)\) looks innocent, but in floating point the order of the sum changes the result in the last bits. FlashInfer's CUTLASS MoE kernel does this “finalize” (the reduction of the top-k outputs) fused, with no fixed order. With the variable on, Qwen's determinism patch swaps it for an unfused, fixed-order finalize (use_fused_finalize = (env != '1')). Together with QSA's sorted top-k, the logprob spread between runs dropped from 0.25/6.07 nats to 0/0, at a cost of ≈ 4% in speed. GLM's cooperative MoE makes the same design choice: the last block sums the slots in a fixed order. Opt-in; the deep dive is in Generating text.

Batch-invariant routingglue.router · glue.select · glue.combine

The TensorFold version of the GLM recipe (An exact engine) takes this concern to the extreme. That engine's contract is batch invariance: a row's bits must not depend on the other rows in the call. That is what guarantees that the reply with drafts equals the serial one and that four requests served together get the same bits they would get alone. In the MoE, the router is the most sensitive point, for the reason given in the previous subsection: one different last bit in a nearly tied logit swaps the expert. The engine answers with three fixed choices. The logits come from BF16 weights and activations with FP32 sums on the tensor cores, and \(K = 4096\) is always split into 8 slices summed in order; the code explains that this choice depends on shape alone because the sliced sum and the single-pass sum differ in the last bits: if the batch size picked between them, the same token could get a different logit. The top-8 comes from sigmoid + bias, with ties broken toward the lower id, and the weights are renormalized in pick order and multiplied by 2.5. Finally, the shared expert enters as a 9th slot (id 288, weight 1), and the combine adds the nine terms \(g_e\,\mathrm E_e(\mathbf x)\) in FP32, always in slot order (that is each rank's share; the two shares are then added in rank order). The router stays in BF16 even when the other dense weights go to 4 bits.

Trade-off. In the vLLM recipe the logits are FP32 too, but the guarantee is different: speculation is lossless in distribution, and the batch size, the kernel chosen and the drafts can change bits. Qwen's determinism is optional and costs ≈ 4%. In TensorFold there is nothing to switch on, and the cost becomes a design constraint: no kernel may let the number of rows change the order of a row's sums. The router itself shows where the line is: it picks its row block (16, 32, 64 or 128) by batch size, which only changes how many rows each program serves, while the 8 slices of \(K\) stay the same. The recipe publishes the result: replies served four at a time came out identical to the same requests served one at a time in 11 of 11 staggered cases and in 11 of 11 sent in a burst.

The shared expert and the initial dense layers

If every token needs a little of the same knowledge (basic syntax, formatting, very frequent facts), leaving that knowledge scattered across routed experts is wasteful: several of them would end up learning copies of the same thing. The DeepSeekMoE family's solution is the shared expert, a SwiGLU that runs for every token, with no router. The routed experts are then free to specialize. All three models have exactly one, but each recipe handles it differently:

  • GLM: a shared expert of width 2048, stored in BF16 in the EXL3 checkpoint (only the routed experts go to 4 bpw). The shared group of GLM53_DENSE_FP8 can convert it to FP8 Marlin. At TP=2 each rank holds 1024 columns.
  • Qwen: a shared expert of 640 with a gate of its own, the shared expert's sigmoid gate: \(\mathbf y = \sum_e g_e\,\mathrm E_e(\mathbf x) + \sigma(\mathbf w_s \cdot \mathbf x)\,\mathrm{Shared}(\mathbf x)\), with \(\mathbf w_s\) = shared_expert_gate.weight [1, 2560]. It is a single scalar per token, between 0 and 1, that decides how much of the “generic” knowledge comes in. At TP=2 the shared expert gets 320 rows per GPU, which is not a multiple of 128; that is why the FP8-dense checkpoint uses per-channel scales rather than blocks of 128.
  • DeepSeek: a shared expert of 2048 in FP8 (the routed ones are in FP4), passed into the same FusedMoE as the routed experts, which computes it in the same launch.
  • GLM on the TensorFold lane: the same BF16 shared expert from the checkpoint, which there joins the DENSE group with the checkpoint's other BF16 weights (attention, dense layers, head: ~9.7 GiB per Spark). In the default DENSE=q4 it becomes 4-bit affine groups of 64 at load time, lossy; DENSE=fp8 and DENSE=bf16 are the alternatives. It runs as each token's 9th slot, through dense matmuls, not through the routed experts' trellis kernel.
Why GLM's shared expert cannot be replicated at TP=3

In tensor parallelism, each rank computes a slice of the output and an all-reduce sums the slices. 2048 is not divisible by 3. A tempting way out would be to replicate the whole shared expert on every rank (disable_tp), but its output would enter the same sum and be counted three times. GLM's TP3 overlay does the opposite: it pads the width from 2048 to 2112 with zeros and slices normally. Why 2112? Each rank's slice must be a multiple of 64, the alignment that NVFP4/MXFP4 scale blocks and GEMM kernel tiles require: a misaligned slice simply has no kernel (the DeepSeek recipe records “704/rank, multiple of 64 for NVFP4”, and GLM's overlay calls its pad “NVFP4-style”). A width that splits into 3 slices that are multiples of 64 is a multiple of \(3 \times 64 = 192 = \operatorname{lcm}(3, 64)\), and the first one ≥ 2048 is \(2112 = 11 \times 192\), with \(704 = 11 \times 64\) per rank. The 64 extra neurons have zeroed weights: they evaluate to \(\mathrm{SiLU}(0) \cdot 0 = 0\) and do not change the output. DeepSeek at TP=3 does the same math on the experts: moe_intermediate_size 2048 → 2112. The details are in Beyond 2 nodes.

In the recipe: the shared expert does not wait for the router

Because the shared expert does not depend on whom the router picked, an engine can change when it runs without changing the sum. The TensorFold lane uses this twice. In decode, each layer alternates DRAM-bound matmuls with phases in which DRAM sits idle: the wait for the all-gather between the Sparks, the hyper-connection glue, the router and the top-k, among others. With TF_GLM_L2PF=1, on in the recipe, a kernel on a side stream uses the wait for the attention all-gather to bring into L2 the weights that come next, among them the router and the shared expert's gate/up and down. Then order matters: if the routed experts ran first, their “~100+ MB” stream of weights (the code's own estimate) would evict from L2 what had just arrived. So, with prefetch on, the MoE runs the shared expert before the routed ones. The buffers are separate and the kernels are the same, and the combine still adds the shared expert last, so the bits do not change. In prefill, with TF_GLM_PREFILL_OVERLAP=2, the shared expert joins the “front” of the next block, which runs on the rank's own rows while the peer's rows are still on the wire (the peer's rows go through the same work once they arrive). The published gains belong to the whole mechanisms, not to the shared expert alone: L2 prefetch by itself took prose from 48.36 to 49.46 tok/s and code from 59.54 to 61.08 (1 request, two boots each); overlap 2 took a 149k prompt from 92.8 to 90.7 s (also two boots each).

Dense layers at the start

Not every layer needs to be MoE. GLM has first_k_dense_replace: 3: layers 0, 1 and 2 use a dense FFN with intermediate_size 12288 (mlp_layer_types lists 3× dense and 42× sparse). Notice that \(12{,}288 = 6 \times 2048\): the dense layer has the width of six experts, and all of them run for every token. DeepSeek-V3 did the same with 3 dense layers of 18,432 (\(= 9 \times 2048\), the same as 8 routed + 1 shared active). V2 had one. Qwen3.8 has none: all 48 layers are MoE.

Why start dense? A common interpretation is that the first layers deal with representations still very close to the token's identity and to local patterns, useful to everyone, and that routing early, over half-formed representations, makes training unstable and unbalanced. The papers offer no proof, and DeepSeek-V4 took a different path for those same layers: MoE routed by hash (section 6). In the GLM recipe on vLLM, these three layers form a group of their own, dense, in GLM53_DENSE_FP8 (lists dense_gate_up and dense_down), with per-channel FP8 Marlin. On the TensorFold lane , they fall into the same DENSE as the shared expert (4 bits by default).

Balancing: aux loss, loss-free bias and groups

Routers trained carelessly tend toward collapse, a rich-get-richer dynamic. An expert that receives more tokens gets more gradient, gets better, the router starts to prefer it, and it receives even more tokens. In the limit, half a dozen experts do all the work and the rest become dead weight in memory. At inference the damage continues: with expert parallel, the GPU holding the popular experts becomes a bottleneck; in any scheme, very popular experts turn into giant batches in a prefill step (section 7).

The classic solution: an auxiliary loss

The first generation of MoEs adds to the language loss a balancing loss that is minimal when the load is uniform:

\[ \begin{gathered} \mathcal L_{\text{bal}} = \alpha \sum_{i=1}^{E} f_i\,P_i \\ f_i = \frac{E}{k\,T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \mathbb 1\big[i \in \mathcal T_t\big], \qquad P_i = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} s_{t,i} \end{gathered} \]
  • \(f_i\): the (normalized) fraction of the batch's tokens that chose expert \(i\); it averages 1 when the load is uniform
  • \(P_i\): the average score the router gave expert \(i\) (the differentiable part)
  • \(\alpha\): the loss weight; it is router_aux_loss_coef = 0.001 in Qwen and GLM (aux_loss_alpha 0.001 in DeepSeek-V2)
  • \(T\): tokens in the batch

The gradient of \(\mathcal L_{\text{bal}}\) pushes down the scores of overloaded experts. It works, but it has a price. It touches the same logits that define the combination weights and competes with the language loss: a strong \(\alpha\) balances well and hurts quality; a weak one preserves quality and balances poorly. It is training-only: the router_aux_loss_coef in the config has no effect whatsoever on inference.

The DeepSeek lineage's solution: a loss-free bias

DeepSeek-V3 proposed aux-loss-free balancing, which shows up in the config as topk_method: noaux_tc. Each expert gets a bias \(b_i\) that is added to the score only to choose the top-k. The weights still come from \(s\) without the bias. The bias is not learned by gradient; at the end of each training step it is nudged toward balance:

\[ \begin{gathered} \mathcal T = \operatorname{TopK}_k(\mathbf s + \mathbf b), \qquad g_e \propto s_e \\ b_i \leftarrow b_i + \gamma \cdot \operatorname{sign}\big(\bar c - c_i\big) \end{gathered} \]
  • \(c_i\): the load of expert \(i\) in the step; \(\bar c\): the average load
  • \(\gamma\): the bias adjustment speed
  • an overloaded expert loses bias and gets chosen less; an underused one gains bias

After training, \(\mathbf b\) is frozen and goes into the checkpoint as e_score_correction_bias: [288] in FP32 in GLM, [256] in DeepSeek. DeepSeek's loader has to map the checkpoint name, ffn.gate.bias, to e_score_correction_bias. The hash layers (section 6) do not create this parameter. The V4 report also says the strategy was “augmented by a light per-sequence balancing loss”.

The simulation below is a toy with 16 experts and top-2, built to show the dynamics. In no-balancing mode, rich-get-richer collapses the load. The auxiliary loss balances it, but touches the weights. The bias balances it without touching them.

The point to take away is in the last indicator. In bias mode, weight distortion is zero by construction, because the bias changes who gets chosen, but not how much each one weighs. That is exactly DeepSeek-V3's argument for dropping the auxiliary loss.

In the recipe: a bias for images

The DeepSeek-V4-Flash-Vision-Exp checkpoint carries, in each MoE layer, a second bias, bias_vl, used when the row is an image token: image and text tokens have very different statistics, and a single bias would not balance both. In vLLM it becomes e_score_correction_bias_vl. Among other things, the recipe's vision hotfix has to remap ffn.gate.bias_vl → e_score_correction_bias_vl in the DSpark drafter's loader too, which only handled names ending in .ffn.gate.bias. The full story of image routing is in section 6 and in Vision.

Group-limited routing (and why it disappeared)

With expert parallel across many nodes, every expert chosen on a distant node is network traffic: the token has to travel there and back (all-to-all). Group-limited routing splits the experts into n_group groups (typically one per node) and forces each token to choose experts from at most topk_group groups. DeepSeek-V2 used group_limited_greedy with 8 groups and top-3 (group score = the best score within it, in the reference code). V3 used noaux_tc with 8 groups and top-4 (score = sum of the 2 best biased scores). First the groups are chosen, then the top-k within them.

Without groups, a scattered top-8 lands on ~5 of 8 nodes on average in this simulation; with groups, it never goes past 4. GLM keeps the fields, but disabled (n_group: 1, topk_group: 1: a single group with all 288 experts), and V4's config does not even have them. In the recipes this makes sense: at TP=2, each rank holds a slice of every expert (DeepSeek, GLM) and communication is an all-reduce that does not depend on who was chosen. In Qwen, with expert parallel but DP=1, the tokens are already replicated on both GPUs and the all-to-all never runs (section 7 and Splitting the model).

Hash MoE: routing by token identity

DeepSeek-V4 replaced the dense FFNs of its first three layers with MoE using hash routing (num_hash_layers: 3). In these layers, who serves the token is not decided by the router: it comes from a fixed table indexed by the token id.

\[ \begin{gathered} \mathcal T(\mathbf x_t) = \texttt{tid2eid}[\,\mathrm{id}_t\,] \in \{0,\dots,255\}^{6} \\ \texttt{tid2eid} \in \mathbb Z^{129{,}280 \times 6}\ \text{(int32)} \end{gathered} \]
  • \(\mathrm{id}_t\): the id of token \(t\) in the 129,280-entry vocabulary
  • tid2eid: “token id → expert ids”, 6 experts per row (the model's top-6)

A detail the documentation oversimplifies: the recipe docs say these layers have no “router GEMM”. Reading the reference code (Gate.forward), the selection does skip the router, but the weights of the 6 chosen experts still come from the gate: the sqrtsoftplus scores are computed, the 6 from the table are picked out, renormalized and multiplied by 1.5, just like in the other layers. In vLLM, the table enters FusedMoE as hash_indices_table, and these layers do not create e_score_correction_bias (the code comments: “hash MoE doesn't use e_score_correction_bias”).

Why do this? The first layers work on representations that are still, to a large extent, the token's identity. A fixed table gives total determinism and zero routing instability, and it can be built balanced in advance. The model gains the capacity of an MoE where there used to be dense FFNs. The detailed justification is our interpretation; the report only records the switch.

The engineering cost: raw ids and bug #175

Routing by id has a consequence a multimodal engine does not expect. When there is an image, vLLM's runner usually passes the model embeddings (inputs_embeds), with the vision tower's vectors already in place of the placeholders, and not the ids. A hash layer without ids has no idea what to do: vLLM's forward raises "DeepSeek V4 hash MoE routing requires input_ids.". The recipe's vision hotfix declares the model as requires_raw_input_tokens = True, so that the runner delivers the raw ids along with the embeddings.

The second problem was sneakier (issue #175). e_score_correction_bias_vl was loaded, but routing always used the text bias and the hash table. Since id 129264 exists in the vocabulary, all image tokens in layers 0–2 went to the same 6 experts, tid2eid[129264]. An image with hundreds of tokens dumped all of its rows onto 6 experts: the collapse from the previous section, produced by an implementation detail. The fix (fused_topk_bias_split_vl) classifies the batch as text, image or mixed and routes the image rows with bias_vl and hash_indices_table=None.

Deep dive: classify once, not 43 times

Knowing whether the batch contains images requires looking at a tensor that lives on the GPU. Asking this in each of the 43 layers would cost 43 host-device syncs per forward. The fix computes the type once, in embed_input_ids, reusing the is_multimodal sum that was already being done, and publishes the result in a “cell” shared by the 43 gates. During CUDA graph capture it always takes the text path, because the decode graphs only replay text.

Load at inference: thin experts, fat experts and bytes per step

In training, many MoE systems imposed a capacity factor: each expert accepted at most a fixed number of tokens per batch, and the excess was dropped. Inference engines like vLLM never drop anything: every token gets its \(k\) experts. Imbalance stops costing quality and starts costing time. How it shows up depends on the phase.

Decode: few tokens, a matter of bytes

A decode step processes few tokens: \(T = c \times (n_{\text{spec}} + 1)\), with \(c\) concurrent sequences and \(n_{\text{spec}}\) draft tokens to verify (Part VII). Every expert touched by at least one of these tokens has to have its weights read from memory. The number of distinct experts determines the step's bytes:

\[ \begin{gathered} D(T) \le \min(E,\; kT) \\ \mathbb E[D(T)] = E\Big[1 - \big(1 - \tfrac{k}{E}\big)^{T}\Big] \end{gathered} \]
  • \(D(T)\): distinct experts touched in one layer by a step of \(T\) tokens
  • \((1 - k/E)\): the probability that a token does not choose a specific expert (the formula assumes uniform, independent routing)
  • step bytes \(\approx L \times D(T) \times\) bytes per expert (per rank)

In DeepSeek with one sequence and 6 drafts, \(T = 7\) and \(k = 6\): at most 42 distinct experts per layer, ≈ 39 expected. At 6.7 MB per expert per rank (13.4 MB in MXFP4 with scales, half on each TP=2 rank), that is ≈ 12 GB read per rank per step just for experts, the biggest item in the recipe's cost model. In Qwen, with MTP=3, \(T = 4\) and \(k = 10\): ≤ 40 slots, 38.8 expected under the uniform model, but the report's analytical estimate is ~32 unique experts per layer. The report does not explain the difference; one possible reading (our interpretation) is that neighboring tokens tend to choose similar experts. With small experts (2.77 MB) split across the GPUs, they add up to ~2.1 GiB per step, only 23% of the bytes. In GLM with DFlash2, \(8 \times 8 = 64\) slots per layer give ≈ 57–58 distinct experts, ≈ 15 GB per rank per step (our didactic estimate, using the uniform formula with 6 MiB per expert per rank; at the effective bandwidth of ~230 GB/s cited in docs/improve-prefill.md, that would be ~65 ms).

This math explains why speculating in an MoE pays off less than in a dense model. In a dense model, verifying 8 tokens reuses the same weights already read; in an MoE, every extra token can pull in new experts. It also explains why batching amortizes less: with 4 sequences of 8 tokens each in GLM, \(T = 32\) already touches ≈ 171 of the 288 experts per layer.

Measured: when MTP is slower than not drafting

Mia's recipe for Ornith-1.5-122B (Qwen3.5-MoE architecture, ~4 bpw EXL3 on a single Spark, on TensorFold) measured the extreme case of this math. The checkpoint's own MTP drafter works and is exact, but it leaves decode at 0.87× the speed of no drafting at all. The README's explanation is this section's: EXL3 MoE decode is memory-bandwidth bound, and the extra rows of a verify step route to extra experts; each step reads more expert weights than a single token would, and the accepted tokens don't pay for that. DFlash2, which drafts a whole block in one forward pass, wins on code and on prose: with one stream, 52.6 tok/s on prose and 83.3 on code, against about 35 without drafts (sparkDash, thinking off, FP8 KV). In the language of the formula above, the step gets more expensive roughly in proportion to \(\mathbb E[D(T)]\), and speculation only pays off if the accepted tokens per step grow faster than that. The README doesn't break down why DFlash2 pays off and MTP doesn't; one possible reading is that the block accepts more tokens per step and doesn't pay for \(k\) sequential drafter forwards (our interpretation). On an H200 the lesson is the same: on a bandwidth-bound MoE, compare speculation against “no drafts” on your hardware before turning it on by default, because what pays off on a dense model can slow an MoE down (chapter 35).

Measurement trap: the one-token window

At startup, the TensorFold lane times the verification windows of every width. That curve of milliseconds per number of rows feeds the time profile and the optional TF_GLM_MULTI_DEPTH=joint mode, which splits draft rows among streams by expected tokens per millisecond. TensorFold v0.6.0 timed windows made of a single repeated token (id 0). By the formula above, 16 rows with uniform routing touch \(\mathbb E[D(16)] \approx 104\) experts per layer in GLM (for real text, a ceiling); 16 copies of the same token send every row to the same few experts, only 8 in the limit where the 16 rows were identical (our math). The patch's comment records the effect: the 16-row window came out at about half the cost of 16 real rows. The fix (patch glm-timing-tokens) uses fixed, distinct ids, drawn with seed 1234 from the first 150,000 in the vocabulary. The lesson holds for any MoE benchmark: synthetic inputs have to route like real text, or you measure a much smaller model than the one you will serve (Benchmarking).

Prefill: many tokens, fat experts

In prefill the situation flips. A 1024-token chunk with top-8 generates 8192 (token, expert) pairs for 288 experts: all of them get activated, with ~28 rows on average. But real routing is not uniform. The GLM recipe's profiling (called P0 in the repository) showed that, in a 1024-token chunk, the hottest expert in each layer shows up in almost every token: a maximum of 1024 rows, an average maximum of ≈ 920 (at 100K context). In 91% (≈ 16K) to 98.6% (≈ 100K) of all recorded MoE layers (including 74 from warmup and decode), that is, in practically all prefill layers, there was at least one expert above the kernel's 128-row limit, about 10.5 “fat” experts per layer.

With few tokens, almost every expert touched receives one or two rows (thin experts): the cost is reading the weights, and the ideal kernel reads fast. With many tokens, every expert gets read anyway, and what weighs is the compute for the fat ones. No single kernel serves both worlds well.

How GLM dispatches the tokens

Before computing, the work has to be reorganized: the (token, slot) pairs arrive in token order, but each expert needs to receive its own contiguous batch. In the vLLM recipe, the apply_exl3_fused_moe function in the EXL3 overlay does this dispatch without ever asking the CPU anything. Why the insistence? Three GPU concepts explain it.

Deep dive: launches, syncs and CUDA graphs

The CPU is what runs the Python code, and it does not compute the matrices: it queues kernels for the GPU to execute. Each enqueue is a kernel launch, and each one costs CPU and driver time; while the queue is full, CPU and GPU work in parallel. A question about the value of a GPU tensor (.item(), .tolist(), or a bincount, whose output size depends on the data) forces the CPU to stop and wait for the queue to drain before reading the answer: that is a host-device sync, and while it lasts the GPU gets no new work. A CUDA graph goes further: it records a step's entire sequence of kernels, with fixed memory addresses, and then replays it without the CPU in the middle. That is why a capture allows neither syncs (there is no CPU to answer) nor new allocations (the addresses are frozen). Hence the choices in the figure: scatter_add_ instead of bincount, num_active = -1 instead of asking how many experts are active, and buffers sized before capture. The topic comes back in depth in CUDA graphs and warmup.

With that in mind, follow the steps:

  1. Flatten and sort. The \(T \times k\) pairs become a list and are sorted by expert (local.argsort()); tokens and weights are permuted along with them.
  2. Count without leaving the GPU. Rows per expert are counted with scatter_add_, not with torch.bincount, which may go through the CPU and break CUDA graph capture. The kernel is launched with num_active = -1 (maximum-concurrency grid) so it does not need .item().
  3. One launch, if it fits. If tokens ≤ cap, a single exl3_moe handles the whole layer. The condition is on tokens and not on experts because no expert can receive more rows than there are tokens: each token chooses each expert at most once.
  4. Otherwise, tiers for the fat ones. In prefill, the thin ones go to exl3_moe (which skips experts whose count is above the cap) and the fat ones follow the configured tier.
  5. Combine. Each result is multiplied by its weight \(g\) and added into out[token].

The cap is EXL3_TEMP_ROWS_FUSED, the rows per expert that fit in the fused kernel's temporary buffers. The code defaults to 128; the launcher sets 32 when the E3 tier is on and 256 with E2. The README's rule is to keep the cap ≥ MAX_NUM_SEQS × (DFLASH_TOKENS + 1) = 4 × 8 = 32, so that every decode step stays in a single graph-capturable launch. Raising the cap to 1024, to fit the entire prefill, was measured and came out 13–24% slower.

EXL3_FAT_GROUPED_TOPK = 8 is the top-k E3 uses to size its scratch: MAX_NUM_BATCHED_TOKENS × top-k rows, each with a 4096 input buffer and a 1024 intermediate buffer in FP16. With MNBT 7168 that comes to \(7168 \times 8 \times (4096 + 1024) \times 2\) B = 560 MiB, taken out of the KV budget (which is why 1M context no longer fits). It has to match the model's top-8. With a smaller value, the reservation would be undersized: the first full prefill would have to reallocate the scratch outside the already-profiled KV budget, and, if that growth happened during a CUDA graph capture, the code would raise RuntimeError (exl3.py:1119-1123).

EXL3_FAT_EXPERT_LOG emits, every 42 MoE layers (one engine step), the line exl3 fat-expert P0 with fat layers, fat experts, maximum rows and the histogram. The default is 1 on the older tiers and 0 with E3, because E3 does not sync with the CPU and the log would cost one sync per layer. The histogram uses _FAT_BUCKET_EDGES = (16, 32, 64, 128, 256, 512, 1024, 2048): each layer contributes its maximum rows per expert. It was this histogram that showed, in P0 at ~100K, 5043 of 5208 layers in the 513–1024 bucket. The kernels themselves (E2, E3, cooperative decode MoE) are covered in Kernels.

Another engine, another dispatch: light and heavy items

The TensorFold lane of the GLM recipe (chapter 49) runs the same EXL3 experts with a different dispatch design. Instead of a per-expert row cap and a separate path for the fat ones, it cuts each expert's batch into fixed-size items. An item is the triple (expert, first pair, count), with up to 16 (token, slot) pairs in decode and up to 64 in a 2,048-row prompt chunk. The plan is built on the GPU from the router's choices, and the maximum number of items is known in advance: one per expert used, plus one every 64 pairs. An expert with 300 rows in the chunk simply becomes 5 items of the same kernel, and there is no fat expert that needs another path. The size 64 was measured against 128, which came out slower “on real routing”, because most passes are small. With uniform routing, a 2,048-row chunk gives \(2048 \times 8 / 288 \approx 57\) rows per expert (our math), less than one item of 64.

The imbalance does not go away; it changes shape. An expert with a single item (light) reads its weights from DRAM to serve at most 64 rows: many bytes per multiplication, bandwidth-bound work. An expert with several items (heavy) has its passes launched side by side, reusing the same weights through L2: almost pure mma, tensor-core-bound work. In plan order, which is the order of the experts' ids, a heavy expert's items come stuck together, and the GPU alternates between a stretch with DRAM saturated and the tensor cores waiting and another with the reverse. The patch glm-prompt-experts-order (TF_GLM_EXL3_ORDER=1) adds a kernel of one 1024-thread block that classifies each item, computes each one's position with a per-warp prefix sum and interleaves the two lists evenly. The heavy item of rank \(r\) goes to fraction \((r + \tfrac12)/n_h\) of the queue, the light item of rank \(l\) to \((l + \tfrac12)/n_l\), and each list keeps its own order. The shared expert's items are left out. Only which block runs which item changes, so every output keeps its bits.

The trade-off against the vLLM recipe is where the complexity lives. The vLLM recipe sorts experts by class: the thin ones share one fused launch, and the fat ones, above the cap, go to the E3 tier, measured at +37–45% over E2. The TensorFold lane uses a single kernel for all of them and only touches the order. Its gain was not measured in isolation. The only published number that involves it is the 50k prefill, ~1,270 → ~1,730 tok/s, credited to splitting the hyper-connections between the ranks and measured “with patches 0009 and 0020”; the order's share cannot be separated out. The same math (each expert's weights are read once per layer, whatever the rows) explains the lane's multi-prefill. At short lengths, a forward's cost is nearly fixed (~0.12 s on the two Sparks), so four short prompts in one chunk cost little more than one, and each expert is read once for all of them. With four simultaneous prose requests, TTFT fell from 590 to 340 ms (sparkDash).

What about expert parallel?

With EP, each GPU holds whole experts, and the step waits for the most loaded GPU: imbalance turns into idle time on the others. Qwen uses EP (256 of the 512 experts per GPU), but with DP=1 the tokens are already replicated by TP; each GPU computes its local experts, the normal all-reduce sums everything, and the all-to-all is never called. vLLM has dynamic expert rebalancing (EPLB, --enable-eplb), not used in the recipes. All of this is the subject of Splitting the model.

Checklist: reading a new MoE

  1. Count. \(E\), \(k\), \(S\), \(I\), \(d\) and how many layers are MoE (first_k_dense_replace, mlp_layer_types, num_hash_layers). Compute totals and active parameters and check them against the card.
  2. Read the router contract. scoring_func, topk_method (is there an e_score_correction_bias?), norm_topk_prob, routed_scaling_factor, n_group/topk_group. Do the engine and the chosen MoE kernel support exactly this combination? Do the field names match the ones the engine's config class reads? Missing fields may be implicit in the model class (Qwen's softmax and shared expert) or in an engine default (sqrtsoftplus and routed_scaling_factor 1.0 in vLLM's code for DeepSeek-V4): find out which value actually applies.
  3. Follow the bytes. How much does one expert weigh in the chosen format? How many distinct experts does your typical step touch? Where does the shared expert live, and at what precision?
  4. Look for special cases. Dense or hash layers at the start, a gate on the shared expert, per-modality biases, MTP layers with experts in another format.
  5. Plan the split. Do \(E\) and \(I\) divide by your TP? If not, padding (2048 → 2112) or expert parallel, each with its own cost.

MoE solved half of the problem of fitting and being fast: the weights. The other big memory consumer in a recipe is the KV cache, which grows with every token of context. The next chapter, Shrinking the KV, shows how MQA, GQA and Multi-head Latent Attention attack that side.
