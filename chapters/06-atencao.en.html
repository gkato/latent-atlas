Read the sentence “The cat climbed the tree because it was so scared”. You know instantly that “it” is the cat. The model doesn't. In the previous chapter, each token became the vector in its row of the embedding table, and the vector for “it” is the same in this sentence, in a detective novel or in a cake recipe. To find out what “it” refers to, the token has to ask its neighbors. Attention is that question-and-answer mechanism, and in a standard Transformer it is the only piece where one token reads information from another (in the recipes' hybrid models, the linear attention and the short convolutions of GDN and KDA also mix positions; chapters 14 and 16). In this chapter we'll build it from scratch: the Q, K and V projections, the dot product and the \(1/\sqrt d\) scale, the softmax (and how to compute it without overflowing FP16), the causal mask, multiple heads, the \(O(n^2)\) cost and three modern tweaks that show up in the recipes' configs: DeepSeek's attention sink, Qwen's output gate and QK-Norm. By the end you'll read num_attention_heads, head_dim, attn_sink and output_gate_type in a config and know what each number does to memory, to the kernel and to parallelism.

A party where everyone asks questions

Picture the tokens of a sentence as guests at a party. Each guest arrives with three things: a question they want answered (“is anyone here a noun that came before me, something that can get scared?”), a name badge describing what they have to offer (“I'm a noun, singular, an animal”) and a message they hand to whoever is interested. Each guest reads everyone's badge, decides how much to listen to each person and leaves the party carrying a blend of the messages, with more weight on whoever best matched their question.

In attention, the three roles have names: the question is the query \(q\), the badge is the key \(k\) and the message is the value \(v\) (query, key and value). In the figure below, the party actually happens. The weights are illustrative, hand-picked to resemble what a trained head does, but the mechanics are the real ones: compare, normalize, mix. Click a token in the left column to see who it listens to.

Notice three things. First, “it” spends more than half of its attention on “cat”, and the message that arrives carries the information about who “it” is: the pronoun's vector leaves the layer enriched. Second, every token pays a little attention to itself and to the neighbor right before it, a common pattern in real heads. Third, toggle the causal mask on and off in the angler sentence. With the mask, “bank” can't see “river”, which comes later, and is left torn between a riverbank and a financial bank. The one that resolves the ambiguity is “river”, which looks back and sees “bank”. In a model that writes from left to right, information only flows from the past to the future, and disambiguation happens in the following tokens (and layers).

All of attention fits in three verbs, which we'll unpack one per section:

  1. Compare. The query of token \(i\) is compared with the key of each token \(j\) through a dot product, which yields a score \(s_{ij}\).
  2. Normalize. The scores of row \(i\) go through a softmax and become positive weights that sum to 1.
  3. Mix. The output of token \(i\) is the average of the values, weighted by those weights. It is added to the token's residual stream (chapter 7).
Why it matters

In a standard Transformer, attention is the only place where information crosses positions. The FFN (or the MoE) of each block works on each token in isolation. (The recipe models are hybrids: in them, the linear attention and the short causal convolutions of Gated DeltaNet and KDA, the dilated convolution of Qwen's N-gram memory and the grouped convolution of DFlash2 also mix positions; chs. 14 and 16.) That is why everything that depends on context length lives here: the KV cache, the cost that grows with the square of the sequence, the sparse and linear variants and a good share of the patches in the three recipes.

Query, key and value: three readings of the same vector

Where do each guest's three cards come from? From the same token vector \(x_i\), read by three different weight matrices learned during training. Stacking the \(n\) tokens as the rows of a matrix \(X\), the three projections become three matrix multiplications (chapter 3):

\[ Q = X W_Q, \qquad K = X W_K, \qquad V = X W_V, \qquad q_i = x_i W_Q,\; k_j = x_j W_K,\; v_j = x_j W_V \]
  • \(X \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times d}\): one token per row; \(d\) is the hidden_size
  • \(W_Q, W_K \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d_h}\): query and key projections; \(d_h\) is the head_dim
  • \(W_V \in \mathbb{R}^{d\times d_v}\): value projection (usually \(d_v = d_h\))
  • \(q_i, k_j, v_j\): rows \(i\) and \(j\) of \(Q\), \(K\) and \(V\)

Why three matrices, and not \(x\) itself? Because the roles are different. What “it” is looking for (an antecedent) is not what “it” advertises (I'm a pronoun), and neither of those is what “it” delivers to whoever queries it. If we used \(W_Q = W_K\), the score would be symmetric (\(s_{ij} = s_{ji}\)) and each token would tend to match itself more than anyone else, since \(q_i\cdot q_i = \lVert q_i\rVert^2\) is always positive and large. Separate projections allow asymmetric relations, like “the verb looks for the subject” without the subject having to look for the verb. The figure shows the three computations being done cell by cell.

Notice in step 6 that, in the recipe models, the proportions change. In Qwen3.8's softmax attention layers (the QSA, which is sparse: each query reads at most 2048 tokens, ch. 13), \(d = 2560\) and each head has \(d_h = 256\). The query projection of one head is a \(2560 \times 256\) matrix, and the 24 heads together form \(2560 \times 6144\): attention works in a space wider than the hidden itself. Keys and values are much narrower, only 2 heads (\(2560 \times 512\) each), a trick called GQA that section 6 defines and chapter 12 explores in depth.

Reading the shape in the checkpoint

PyTorch stores a linear layer as [out × in], the reverse of the notation above. In the Qwen3.8 checkpoint, self_attn.q_proj.weight has shape [12288, 2560]: 2560 inputs and 12,288 outputs. 12,288 is twice 24 × 256 because half of those rows are the output gate from section 8. k_proj and v_proj are [512, 2560] and o_proj is [2560, 6144]. In all three models, attention_bias is false: the projections have no bias vector, which is one less thing to load and quantize.

Who matches whom: dot product, 1/√d and QK-Norm

The score between the query of token \(i\) and the key of token \(j\) is a dot product: multiply coordinate by coordinate and add everything up. Geometrically, it is the product of the lengths times the cosine of the angle between the two vectors, so the score is high when they point the same way and are long.

\[ s_{ij} = \frac{q_i \cdot k_j}{\sqrt{d_h}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{d_h}}\sum_{c=1}^{d_h} q_{ic}\,k_{jc} = \frac{\lVert q_i\rVert\,\lVert k_j\rVert \cos\theta_{ij}}{\sqrt{d_h}} \]
  • \(s_{ij}\): score (attention logit) of the pair query \(i\), key \(j\)
  • \(q_{ic}, k_{jc}\): coordinate \(c\) of the query and of the key
  • \(\theta_{ij}\): angle between \(q_i\) and \(k_j\)
  • \(\sqrt{d_h}\): the scale; \(d_h\) is the number of dimensions of the head

The \(\sqrt{d_h}\) looks like a detail, but without it attention breaks in large heads. Suppose independent coordinates with mean 0 and variance 1 (roughly what initialization and normalizations produce). Each term \(q_c k_c\) has variance 1, and a sum of \(d_h\) independent terms has variance \(d_h\):

\[ \operatorname{Var}(q\cdot k) = \sum_{c=1}^{d_h}\operatorname{Var}(q_c k_c) = d_h \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \operatorname{std}(q\cdot k) = \sqrt{d_h} \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \operatorname{std}\!\left(\frac{q\cdot k}{\sqrt{d_h}}\right) = 1 \]
  • \(\operatorname{Var}\): variance; std = square root of the variance
  • assumption: \(q_c, k_c\) independent, mean 0, variance 1

With \(d_h = 256\), the unscaled scores would have a standard deviation of 16. A difference of 16 between two scores becomes a factor of \(e^{16} \approx 9\) million in the softmax: the distribution collapses onto a single token, the head ends up seeing just one key and, during training, the gradients of the other keys vanish. This is softmax saturation. Dividing by \(\sqrt{d_h}\) brings the scores back to a standard deviation of 1, whatever the size of the head. That is why the formula is called scaled dot-product attention. Try it below.

With the scale off, increasing \(d\) spreads the histogram and drives the entropy down: with \(d = 256\), the favorite key takes almost all the weight. With the scale on, the histogram stays put. The second control shows a problem the scale doesn't solve.

What if the vectors grow? QK-Norm

The calculation above assumes coordinates with variance 1, and nothing in training guarantees that. The weights of \(W_Q\) and \(W_K\) can grow, and with them the lengths of \(q\) and \(k\). If both grow by a factor \(g\), the score grows by \(g^2\) and the softmax saturates all the same. This logit blow-up is a known cause of loss spikes in large training runs. QK-Norm cuts the problem off at the root: it applies an RMSNorm to each head of \(q\) and of \(k\) before the dot product.

\[ \operatorname{RMSNorm}(x) = \frac{x}{\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{d_h}\sum_c x_c^2 + \varepsilon}} \odot \gamma, \qquad \hat s_{ij} = \frac{\operatorname{RMSNorm}(q_i)\cdot\operatorname{RMSNorm}(k_j)}{\sqrt{d_h}}, \qquad |\hat s_{ij}| \le \sqrt{d_h}\,\gamma_{\max}^2 \]
  • \(\gamma\): learned gain, one per head dimension
  • \(\varepsilon\): small constant that guards against division by zero (rms_norm_eps)
  • \(\odot\): element-wise multiplication
  • bound: after the norm, \(\lVert \hat q\rVert \le \sqrt{d_h}\,\gamma_{\max}\), and likewise for \(\hat k\)

After normalization, the length of \(q\) and \(k\) no longer depends on the size of the weights, only on the gain \(\gamma\). The model can still make a head sharper, but only on purpose, by learning \(\gamma\), not by accident. The alternative without normalization is qk-clip, which rescales \(W_Q\) and \(W_K\) when the largest logit crosses a threshold. The Qwen3.8 and DeepSeek-V4 reports both say they train without this trick, for different reasons. DeepSeek's attributes it to the RMSNorm applied directly to the queries and to the KV entries. Qwen's attributes it to its stability recipe as a whole: above all the residual gate (the Gated Residual, ch. 15), together with the Muon optimizer (ch. 1).

In the recipes

Qwen3.8's QSA has q_norm and k_norm with 256 weights, one RMSNorm per head applied before RoPE (of the “zero-centered” kind, with the weight initialized at 0 and applied as \(1+w\)); the sparse indexer has its own (q_layernorm, k_layernorm of 128). DeepSeek-V4 divides each head of \(q\) by its RMS, with no gain, and passes the KV vector through a kv_norm of 512. GLM's DFlash2 drafter, built on a Qwen3 base, has q_norm/k_norm of 128. When writing the context KV from the target's hidden states, it applies RMSNorm → fused K/V GEMM (all 5 layers at once) → k_norm → RoPE, exactly the order a fused kernel has to reproduce. GLM-5.3's own MLA normalizes the compressed latents (q_a_layernorm of 1536, kv_a_layernorm of 512) instead of each head.

Order matters

RMSNorm and RoPE don't commute: RoPE rotates pairs of coordinates, and the gain \(\gamma\) weights each coordinate differently. Normalizing after rotating is a different function. A reimplementation (an overlay, a fused kernel) that swaps the order, or that forgets the k_norm when writing the KV cache, produces plausible text and wrong numbers. Only a parity test against the reference catches it (chapter 25).

Softmax: from scores to weights, without overflowing

The scores in a row can be any real number. To become the weights of an average, they have to be positive and sum to 1. The softmax does this by exponentiating and normalizing:

\[ a_{ij} = \operatorname{softmax}_j(s_i) = \frac{e^{s_{ij}}}{\sum_{k} e^{s_{ik}}}, \qquad \operatorname{softmax}(s + c) = \frac{e^{c}\,e^{s_{ij}}}{e^{c}\sum_k e^{s_{ik}}} = \operatorname{softmax}(s) \]
  • \(a_{ij}\): attention weight of token \(i\) on token \(j\); \(\sum_j a_{ij} = 1\)
  • \(s_{ik}\): scores of row \(i\) (already scaled)
  • \(c\): any constant added to all the scores in the row

Two properties make everything work. The exponential makes everything positive and amplifies differences: a score one unit higher weighs \(e \approx 2.72\) times more. And the softmax ignores constants: adding the same \(c\) to all the scores changes nothing, because \(e^c\) cancels out of the numerator and the denominator. The scale \(1/\sqrt{d_h}\) works as a fixed temperature, the same idea chapter 9 uses to sample tokens.

The second property saves the computation on the GPU. The largest finite FP16 number is 65,504, so \(e^x\) overflows to infinity when \(x > \ln 65{,}504 \approx 11.09\). A score of 12 already gives Inf; the sum becomes Inf, and \(\text{Inf}/\text{Inf}\) is NaN, which spreads through the residual stream and contaminates every token after it. BF16 and FP32 go up to \(\approx 3.4\cdot 10^{38}\) and overflow at \(x \approx 88.7\): more headroom, same wall (chapter 4). The stable softmax subtracts the maximum \(m\) before exponentiating. The largest exponent becomes \(e^0 = 1\), nothing overflows, and the result is identical. The same trick gives us the log-sum-exp, which sums up the whole row in a single number:

\[ m_i = \max_k s_{ik}, \qquad \ell_i = \sum_k e^{s_{ik}-m_i}, \qquad \operatorname{LSE}_i = \ln\sum_k e^{s_{ik}} = m_i + \ln \ell_i, \qquad \ln a_{ij} = s_{ij} - \operatorname{LSE}_i \]
  • \(m_i\): largest score in row \(i\)
  • \(\ell_i\): normalizer, always between 1 and the number of keys
  • \(\operatorname{LSE}_i\): log-sum-exp; with it, the weights come out without overflow

In pieces: the online softmax

An attention kernel doesn't want to store the whole row of scores. In the decode of a 1-million-token context, that would be 1 million numbers per head, just for one query. FlashAttention-style kernels walk through the keys in blocks (tiles) and keep only three things per query: the running maximum \(m\), the normalizer \(\ell\) and the accumulator \(o\). When a new block brings a larger maximum \(m'\), whatever has already been summed is corrected by the factor \(\alpha = e^{m-m'}\):

\[ m' = \max\!\big(m, \max_{j\in\text{block}} s_j\big), \quad \alpha = e^{m-m'}, \quad \ell' = \alpha\,\ell + \sum_{j\in\text{block}} e^{s_j-m'}, \quad o' = \alpha\,o + \sum_{j\in\text{block}} e^{s_j-m'}\,v_j, \quad \text{output} = \frac{o}{\ell} \]
  • \(m, \ell, o\): maximum, normalizer and accumulator up to the previous block (they start at \(-\infty, 0, 0\))
  • \(\alpha\): factor that “rescales” the past to the new maximum
  • \(v_j\): value of key \(j\); \(o\) has the size of one value

This is the heart of the online softmax. The same reasoning lets you split the keys into several chunks computed in parallel and merge them at the end, using each chunk's LSE. The figure walks through the six ideas with real numbers. In FP16 the naive computation already breaks with the starting scores; switch to BF16 or FP32 and increase \(c\) to see the same wall further out.

Step 5 (split-K) has a name of its own in decode kernels. In decode there is one query per sequence, not much work to keep all the GPU's SMs busy. So the kernel splits the keys across several thread blocks, each one returns its already-normalized output \(\bar o_p\) and its \(\operatorname{LSE}_p\), and a final step computes \(\text{output} = \sum_p e^{\operatorname{LSE}_p - \operatorname{LSE}}\,\bar o_p\), with \(\operatorname{LSE} = \ln\sum_p e^{\operatorname{LSE}_p}\). The QSA sparse kernel writes these partials to partial_output and partial_lse, and DeepSeek-V4's SM120 backend calls them mid_out and mid_lse. The details are in the kernels chapter.

In the recipe: split the keys only when queries are scarce

The GLM recipe has a second version, on another engine: TensorFold v0.6.0 with 53 patches (chapter 48). That engine's latent attention kernels carry exactly this section's trio \((m, \ell, o)\), one block of keys at a time (the _tile function). What changes with the kind of step is the arrangement. In dense attention (the rows in the first 2,051 positions) and in decode's sparse attention, the keys go in pieces of 512: each piece writes its fp32 partials, and a _merge combines them in a fixed order, as in step 5. In prefill, a prompt chunk has up to 2,048 rows, more than enough queries to keep the GPU busy. There, _sparse_onepass (patch 0009) gives each program one row with the rank's 32 heads, walks the up to 2,051 selected tokens in a single online softmax and writes the output straight to BF16, with no partials and no merge. The trade-off: split-K buys parallelism when queries are few and pays for it with a partials buffer and an extra pass; the single pass drops the buffer (memory the startup estimate no longer reserves and hands to the KV pool, patch 0028), but only pays off with many rows (interpretation). The bits change too. Since floating-point addition is not associative, merging pieces and summing in a single chain give slightly different results. That is why the kernel fixes the block (32 heads × 32 keys): a row's bits depend only on its query, its token list and the cache, never on the other rows of the chunk. That is the batch invariance the engine's exactness contract requires.

exp2 and the 1.4427 factor

GPUs have a native instruction for \(2^x\), not for \(e^x\). Since \(e^x = 2^{x\log_2 e}\), the QSA kernel multiplies the scale by \(\log_2 e \approx 1.4427\) once (softmax_scale * 1.4426950408889634) and uses exp2 everywhere. The maximum and the LSE then live in base 2. The math is the same, and one multiplication per score is saved (exp2 and log₂e).

In the recipe: FP8 scales “for free”

The QSA's FP8 KV patch (a backport of vllm#55557 to vLLM 0.30) uses the algebra of the softmax to avoid creating any scale buffer on the GPU. K's dequantization scale multiplies every score in a row, so it becomes part of the temperature: softmax_scale = head_dim**-0.5 * k_scale. V's scale multiplies the whole output, so it goes into the inverse of the normalizer: inv_normalizer = output_scale / max(normalizer, 1e-20). The patch comment points out the detail a beginner would miss: the split-K LSE stays without the scale, so that merging the chunks remains exact.

In the recipe: one scale per token, exact on read

In the TensorFold version, GLM's KV is FP8 as well, but each cache row (the 512-wide latent of one token in one layer) has its own scale: 512 e4m3 codes + 4 bytes of scale + 12 of padding = 528 B (patch 0038), versus 656 B for the vLLM recipe's fp8_ds_mla. A scale that changes from key to key cannot go into the temperature, which applies to the whole row of scores. The kernel applies the same algebra key by key: with codes \(\hat c_j\) and scale \(\kappa_j\), the score is \(s_j = \kappa_j\,(\tilde q\cdot \hat c_j)/\sqrt{d_h}\), where \(\tilde q\) is the absorbed query from section 6. Since in absorbed MLA the latent is key and value at the same time, the weight also takes the scale before the weighted sum, \(o = \sum_j (p_j\kappa_j)\,\hat c_j\), and the normalizer \(\ell\) is left without it. The trick is that \(\kappa_j\) is a power of two: multiplying by it only touches the exponent and rounds nothing. The arithmetic is therefore that of attention over the dequantized rows, with no extra rounding. FP8 loses precision when it writes (it is lossy), but reading adds no rounding at all. The full format is in chapter 23.

The attention matrix, the mask and the weighted sum

Putting it all together in matrix form, the attention of one head over \(n\) tokens is:

\[ A = \operatorname{softmax}_{\text{rows}}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_h}} + M\right), \qquad O = A\,V, \qquad M_{ij} = \begin{cases} 0 & j \le i \\ -\infty & j > i \end{cases} \]
  • \(QK^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\): all the scores at once
  • \(M\): mask added before the softmax (here, the causal one); \(e^{-\infty} = 0\)
  • \(A\): attention matrix; each row sums to 1
  • \(O = AV \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times d_v}\): row \(i\) is the weighted sum \(\sum_j a_{ij} v_j\)

The mask is how you say who may look at whom without changing anything else: a \(-\infty\) in the score becomes a weight of exactly zero. In a language model, the mask is causal for three reasons. In training, the model learns to predict token \(i+1\) from tokens \(\le i\) at every position at once, in a single forward pass, and that is only honest if position \(i\) can't peek at \(i+1\). In generation, the future doesn't exist yet. And, for serving, the most valuable consequence: since no token looks ahead, a token's K and V never change once they're computed. They can be stored and reused, which is the KV cache (chapter 10) and prefix caching (chapter 33). In the figure, run your mouse (or finger) over a row to see its weighted sum.

Switch modes and compare. With the causal mask, the matrix is triangular and the first row can only look at itself, with weight 1. In bidirectional mode, every row sees everything. This is bidirectional attention, the standard in the vision towers of the three models (27 blocks in Qwen3.8, 24 in GLM-5.3, 32 in DeepSeek-V4): the patches of an image all arrive together, and there is no generation order to respect (chapter 17). Block mode is the hybrid used by block drafters (chapter 37): the whole block is predicted at once, and each masked position was trained seeing the other positions of the block, including the ones ahead of it.

DFlash2 drafter config field"is_causal": false

DFlash2 proposes 7 tokens per step in a block of 8 positions (the anchor + 7 [MASK]), with bidirectional attention inside the block and a 2048-token context window. vLLM's stock DFlash drafter code treated every sliding-window layer as causal, and overlay/patch_dflash2.py makes it honor the checkpoint field. The backend also has to support a non-causal mask: on the GB10 (SM121), vLLM picks FLASH_ATTN on its own. When someone pinned TRITON_ATTN, which in that image applies a causal mask inside the block, structured mode dropped to ~29 tok/s with an acceptance of 0.31, versus 65.1 tok/s and an acceptance of 0.959 with the right backend. The first position stayed good and the following ones collapsed. Interpretation: the first position barely depends on the neighbors ahead of it, while the others were trained counting on them.

The mask is a training contract

The same weights with a different mask form a different function. No kernel complains: a backend with the wrong mask runs, generates tokens and just loses quality (or acceptance, in the case of a drafter). When assembling a recipe, check the mask of each module: the target is causal, the vision tower is bidirectional, and a block drafter is bidirectional inside the block.

Attention as associative memory

There is another way to look at the same computation. An ordinary dictionary is a “hard” associative memory: you hand it an exact key and get back the associated value. Attention is the soft version. The query is compared with all the keys, and the answer is the average of the values weighted by similarity. If one key matches much better than the others, attention behaves like the dictionary. If several match about equally well, it returns a blend. The \((k_j, v_j)\) pairs of all previous tokens are the memory's index cards, and the KV cache is the notebook where they are kept.

In the figure, the keys have length 1 and sit on a circle. Since the score is \(\lVert q\rVert \cos\theta\), the direction of the query chooses which key it is looking for, and the length of the query decides how picky it is. The query's norm acts as the inverse of a temperature, which is one more reason for QK-Norm to keep those norms in check.

This reading connects attention to an old family of memory models, Hopfield networks: “modern Hopfield networks” have exactly the update rule of attention (Ramsauer et al., 2020). It also explains what linear attention trades away. Instead of storing one card per token, in a notebook that never stops growing, it condenses all the cards into a fixed-size matrix \(S = \sum_j k_j v_j^\top\). The memory stops growing, at the price of the cards starting to overlap.

Many heads, many questions

A head does one softmax per token, and therefore a single mix. But “it” needs several things at once: what its antecedent is, which verb it belongs to, what tense the sentence is in. Multi-head attention runs \(H\) attentions in parallel, each with its own \(d_h\)-dimensional projections, and mixes the outputs at the end:

\[ \text{head}_h = \operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{Q_h K_h^\top}{\sqrt{d_h}} + M\right) V_h, \qquad \operatorname{MHA}(X) = \big[\,\text{head}_1 \,\Vert\, \text{head}_2 \,\Vert \cdots \Vert\, \text{head}_H\,\big]\, W_O \]
  • \(H\): number of heads (num_attention_heads)
  • \(Q_h = XW_Q^{(h)}\), \(K_h\), \(V_h\): the projections of head \(h\), with \(d_h\) columns
  • \(\Vert\): side-by-side concatenation, which gives \(H\cdot d_v\) columns
  • \(W_O \in \mathbb{R}^{H d_v \times d}\): the output projection, the o_proj

In classic MHA, each query head has its own key and value heads. Current models economize here. In GQA (grouped-query attention), groups of \(H/H_{kv}\) query heads share the same key and the same value: query head \(h\) reads KV head \(\lfloor h\,H_{kv}/H \rfloor\). Qwen3.8's QSA has a 12:1 ratio (24 queries for 2 KV), DFlash2 has 4:1 (32 for 8) and MiMo-V2.6 has 16:1 in the global layers and 8:1 in the window layers. The extreme case, \(H_{kv} = 1\), is MQA (multi-query attention). The QSA indexer is an MQA (4 query heads, 1 key), DeepSeek-V4 stores a single vector that serves as K and V for all 64 heads, and GLM's MLA, in decode, also works as an MQA over the 512-wide latent. The stored KV shrinks by a factor of \(H/H_{kv}\). There is a price for parallelism: the KV heads also have to be split across the GPUs (or replicated), and that is what blocks Qwen at TP=3, as we'll see at the end of the section. Chapter 12 animates these savings.

In practice there are no \(H\) separate multiplications. The code does one big multiplication \(X\,[W_Q^{(1)} \cdots W_Q^{(H)}]\), which gives \(n \times H d_h\), and reshapes the result into \([n, H, d_h]\): a 3D tensor in which each slice is a head. The total cost is the same as that of a single head with \(H d_h\) dimensions. Each head has its own attention matrix, and the figure shows six of them stacked, with stylized patterns of the kind the interpretability literature has found in trained models (“previous token” heads, heads that look at the first token, induction heads that copy patterns; Olsson et al., 2022).   • Qwen3.8 (QSA): 24 × 256 = 6144, 2.4× the hidden of 2560. o_proj is [2560 × 6144].
  • DeepSeek-V4: 64 × 512 = 32,768, 8× the hidden of 4096. A direct 32,768 → 4096 projection would have 134 million parameters. The model splits the heads into 8 groups of 8 (o_groups), projects each group from 4096 to 1024 (wo_a) and joins the 8 × 1024 = 8192 into a wo_b of 8192 → 4096: half the parameters.

Why heads wider than the hidden? Interpretation: a large head gives each question more room and leaves space to split it into parts with and without position (the partial RoPE of chapter 8 rotates only 64 of the 256 dimensions in Qwen). The bill falls on o_proj, which has to bring everything back to the hidden size. It is also the layer that writes to the residual stream, which is why abliteration touches precisely o_proj: Qwen's abliterated checkpoint changes only the o_proj of the 12 QSA layers.

Config parameter (QSA)"head_dim": 256

Each of the 24 query heads and of the 2 key/value heads has 256 dimensions. Three things in the recipe follow from this. The scale: softmax_scale = head_dim**-0.5 = 1/16. The KV per token: 13 layers with KV (12 QSA + 1 MTP) × 2 (K and V) × 2 heads × 256 × 2 bytes = 26,624 B in BF16 (13,312 B per GPU at TP=2) and 13,312 B in FP8. And the shape of the kernels: the QSA sparse attention function requires head_dim >= 16 and a power of 2 (assert head_dim & (head_dim - 1) == 0), and the Triton tiles are sized by it.

Heads are the slices of tensor parallelism

Each head only looks at its own Q, K and V, so heads are the natural unit for splitting attention across GPUs (tensor parallel, chapter 38). Each GPU computes a subset of whole heads, and only o_proj needs to sum the contributions of the GPUs. At TP=2, GLM gets 32 heads per GPU and so does DeepSeek (DeepSeek's SM120 kernel accepts 16, 32, 64 or 128 local heads). Qwen gets 12 query heads and 1 KV head per GPU. The trouble shows up with three nodes: 64 isn't divisible by 3. GLM pads to 66 heads. DeepSeek pads to 72, but in a precise format: 9 groups × 8. The number of groups goes from 8 to 9 and each group keeps its 8 heads, because \(r = 8 \times 512 = 4096\) is a contract of wo_a (the per-group multiplication only accepts that width). Each GPU gets 3 groups and 24 heads, and the 9th group is pure padding, with wo_a/wo_b zeroed out and sink \(-\infty\). DFlash2 goes from 32/8 to 36/9 and runs at TP=1. In Qwen, the 24 query heads divide by 3, but the 2 KV heads don't, and vLLM only replicates KV heads when TP is a multiple of them. Padding heads have zero weights and don't change the result, as long as, when the model has a sink, their sink is \(-\infty\) (section 8). The full calculation is in chapter 40.

Engine variableTF_GLM_LATENT=1 (default)

In the TensorFold version of the recipe, GLM's MLA runs literally as the MQA over the latent that this section described. GLM's attention layers are NoPE (the 256 of \(d_h\) have no RoPE part, as in the table in section 3), so head \(h\)'s key is \(W_K^{(h)}c_j\) and its value is \(W_V^{(h)}c_j\), with \(c_j\) the normalized 512-wide latent of token \(j\). Reassociating the products (ch. 3), \(q_h\cdot(W_K^{(h)}c_j) = (W_K^{(h)\top}q_h)\cdot c_j = \tilde q_h\cdot c_j\) and \(\sum_j a_{hj}W_V^{(h)}c_j = W_V^{(h)}\sum_j a_{hj}c_j\). This is weight absorption: the engine stores only \(c_j\) (1 KB per token and layer in BF16), computes the absorbed query \(\tilde q_h\) once per row, runs every head's attention over the same latents and expands the result by \(W_V^{(h)}\) once at the end. The score is the same computation in a different order, so the scale stays \(1/\sqrt{256} = 1/16\) (scale = c.qk_dim ** -0.5), even though the dot product now has 512 terms. At TP=2 each Spark gets 32 heads and stores the whole latent of every token (interpretation from the code, which does not divide the latent by the number of ranks). The prompt sparse kernel handles a row's 32 heads in a single program, which reads each block of latents once for all of them: the MQA saving showing up in memory bandwidth. Combining the GPUs' contributions changes too. Instead of an all-reduce, each rank receives the other's fp32 partial by all-gather and adds the two in rank order, rank 0's first, and the two Sparks end up with the same bits (chapter 48). In v1.3.2 there was no TP=3: GLM's CUDA engine required exactly two ranks. Since v1.4 (patches 0066–0068) the recipe runs TP=3 without padding: the heads are split unevenly, 22/21/21, with the remainder on the lowest ranks, and the 64 → 66 padding remains the vLLM recipe's choice. It is the same choice that shows up at any TP degree that does not divide the head count, on an H200 cluster too: pad with null heads (uniform shapes, wasted work) or split unevenly (no waste, but the most loaded rank sets the pace). With TF_GLM_LATENT=0 the engine goes back to storing K/V per head. Chapter 12 works through the absorption math.

The price of conversation: O(n²)

At the party, every guest talks to everyone who arrived before them. With \(n\) tokens and a causal mask, that is \(n(n+1)/2\) pairs per head and per layer. Each pair costs a \(d_h\)-dimensional dot product for the score and a \(d_v\)-dimensional multiply-add for the mix:

\[ \text{FLOPs}_{\text{attention}} \approx H\,(d_h + d_v)\,n^2, \qquad \text{materialized scores} = H\,n^2\,b, \qquad \text{stored KV} = n \cdot L_{kv} \cdot H_{kv}\,(d_h + d_v)\,b \]
  • \(n\): tokens in the sequence; \(H\): query heads
  • FLOPs: \(2d_h\) per score and \(2d_v\) per mix, over \(n^2/2\) pairs (causal)
  • \(b\): bytes per number (2 in BF16, 1 in FP8)
  • \(L_{kv}\): layers that store KV; \(H_{kv}\): K/V heads

This is the quadratic complexity of attention. The Q, K, V and O projections cost a fixed amount per token, and attention costs an amount that grows with the context. With 262,144 tokens (Qwen3.8's native context), \(n^2 \approx 6.9\cdot 10^{10}\). In a dense attention with the QSA geometry (\(H = 24\), \(d_h = d_v = 256\)), that would be about \(8.4\cdot 10^{14}\) FLOPs per layer in attention alone (calculation). The score matrix of one head, if stored in BF16, would take \(2^{18}\cdot 2^{18}\cdot 2 = 2^{37}\) bytes = 128 GiB (137 GB): more than all the memory the system sees on a recipe node (128 GB nominal, of which Linux reports about 121.7 GiB).

Three observations. First, FlashAttention never stores the \(n\times n\) matrix, thanks to the online softmax from section 4: memory becomes \(O(n)\). But the FLOPs stay \(O(n^2)\). Second, in decode each new token is a single query and costs \(O(n)\), but it has to read all \(n\) keys and values from the KV cache, which makes long-context decode bound by memory bandwidth. Third, the KV grows linearly and piles up per sequence. In Qwen's QSA, 26,624 B per token in BF16 comes to about 7.0 GB per 262,144-token sequence (calculation). With 24 K/V heads instead of 2 (a classic MHA), it would be 12 times more, about 84 GB.

The three architectures in the recipes exist to escape this bill, each by its own route:

∞

Linear: fixed-size memory

Trades the ever-growing notebook for a \(d\times d\) state updated at every token: KDA in 34 of GLM's 45 layers, Gated DeltaNet in 36 of Qwen's 48 (ch. 14).

✂

Sparse: read only what matters

A cheap indexer picks the keys: top-2048 in GLM, up to 2048 in micro-blocks of 4 in Qwen's QSA, a window of 128 + the top-512 compressed entries in DeepSeek (ch. 13).

⇲

Latent: store less per token

Compress K and V into a small vector: the 512-wide latent of GLM's MLA, DeepSeek-V4's single 512-wide K = V (ch. 12).

Why it matters

The three models are hybrids because of this section. GLM and Qwen alternate 3 linear layers for every 1 (sparse) softmax attention layer. DeepSeek combines a sliding window with KV compressed 4:1 and 128:1. That is why 1 million tokens of context fit in two nodes, and that is why each recipe needs kernels, KV cache groups and patches that a pure-attention model would never ask for.

In the recipe: the n² moves, it does not vanish

The TensorFold version of the GLM recipe serves the full native window, 1,048,576 tokens per request, with up to 4 requests sharing one pool of per-token caches (2,922,496 tokens at the measured start). Past 2,051 tokens, each query of the sparse attention reads at most 2,051 positions: the 512 pools of 4 tokens the indexer picked, plus the tail of the incomplete pool. But the indexer still scores every complete pool the row can see, about \(n/4\) per query. The prefill's total work still grows as \(n^2\), only with a much smaller constant (interpretation). The prefill numbers measured in the recipe (chapter 49) fit this reading: the rate stays near 1,950 tok/s up to about 65 thousand tokens and drops after that. They are 1,952 tok/s with an 8,219-token prompt, 1,979 with 32,790, 1,642 with 262,170 and 1,015 with 981,841 (967 s, needle found). Conditions: sparkDash through the API, GPU clocks capped at 2,200 MHz, default configuration (FP8 KV, 4-bit dense weights, 4 streams). The drop may have causes other than the indexer, and the recipe does not publish that breakdown. In decode, patch 0043 cuts part of this work: each row scores only the pools it actually sees, not the whole bucket rounded up to a power of two for the CUDA graph, and picks the same tokens (chapter 13).

Sinks and gates: when the head doesn't want to look at anyone

The softmax has a hidden rigidity: the weights always sum to 1. A head can't say “there's nothing relevant here”. The output is always an average of values and something always gets written to the residual stream, even when the head would rather stay quiet. Models trained with pure softmax discover a workaround: dump the leftover attention on the first token and learn to make its value nearly zero. This is the attention sink phenomenon, described in StreamingLLM (Xiao et al., 2023). The first tokens receive huge attention regardless of their content, and dropping them from the KV window tanks quality, while keeping just four of them brings it back. Later work links this dumping to the massive activations and outliers that get in the way of quantization. The recipe models bring two explicit solutions.

A place to throw attention: the learned sink

The first is to give the head an official destination for the excess: a learned logit \(\sigma_h\) per head, which enters only the denominator of the softmax. It is like a ghost key whose value is zero:

\[ a_{ij} = \frac{e^{s_{ij}}}{e^{\sigma_h} + \sum_k e^{s_{ik}}}, \qquad \sum_j a_{ij} = 1 - \frac{e^{\sigma_h}}{e^{\sigma_h} + \sum_k e^{s_{ik}}} < 1, \qquad \sigma_h = -\infty \;\Rightarrow\; \text{plain softmax} \]
  • \(\sigma_h\): sink logit of head \(h\) (one learned number per head and layer)
  • the mass missing from 1 “goes to the sink” and writes nothing
  • \(\sigma_h = 0\): becomes the “softmax + 1” (proposed by E. Miller, 2023)
  • \(\sigma_h = -\infty\): \(e^{-\infty} = 0\), the neutral element

In the kernel, the sink enters the stable softmax as if it were one more score, only without a value. The attention kernel of the DSpark drafter does exactly this (simplified from the code):

m   = max(max(scores), sink)          # the sink also competes for the max
w   = exp(scores - m)
den = sum(w) + exp(sink - m)           # and enters only the denominator
out = sum(w * values) / den            # the real weights sum to less than 1

The reference implementation of MiMo-V2.6 (the model from the tutorial) does the same computation another way: it concatenates the sink as an extra column of scores, applies the softmax and throws the column away. The two forms are identical.

Checkpoint parameterattn_sink · 64 FP32 logits per layer

One logit per head, in every DeepSeek-V4 layer (model.py:964-970) and in the stages of the DSpark drafter (dspark_kernels.py:259-262). The DSpark kernels and the FlashInfer backend receive the sink as an argument. Under tensor parallelism it is sliced per head: 32 per GPU at TP=2. In padding heads the sink has to be \(-\infty\): at TP=3 (64 → 72 heads) and when the SM120 backend pads the local heads up to the next size the kernel accepts (24 → 32). The module injected by the TP=3 patch (patches/tp3/apply_tp3_patch.py) warns that a zeroed sink is worth \(e^0 = 1\): it adds a ghost unit to the softmax denominator and dampens the real heads (dsv4_tp_pad.py carries the same warning). That is why the sink doesn't go through the generic loader, which zeroes the destination before copying.

A faucet at the output: Qwen's gate

The second solution leaves the softmax alone. It lets the weights sum to 1, but puts a faucet on the output of each head. This is attention with an output gate:

\[ \tilde o_h = \sigma(g_h) \odot o_h, \qquad g = x\,W_G, \qquad y = \big[\,\tilde o_1 \,\Vert \cdots \Vert\, \tilde o_H\,\big]\,W_O, \qquad \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}} \]
  • \(o_h\): attention output of head \(h\) (\(d_v\) numbers)
  • \(g_h\): \(d_v\) gate numbers, computed from the same token \(x\)
  • \(\sigma\): sigmoid, between 0 (closed) and 1 (open), one per dimension
  • \(W_G\): extra projection; in Qwen3.8 it is the bottom half of each head in q_proj

With the gate, a head with nothing to say shuts itself off (\(\sigma \to 0\)), with no need for a sink. There is a bonus. Between \(V\) and \(W_O\) there is no nonlinearity: for each head, \(W_V^{(h)} W_O^{(h)}\) is a single linear map of rank at most \(d_h\) (256 in Qwen3.8, far less than the hidden of 2560), where \(W_O^{(h)}\) are the \(d_v\) rows of o_proj that receive that head. The gate, which depends on the input, breaks this linear bottleneck. The Qwen team published a study of this variant in 2025 (Qiu et al., “Gated Attention for Large Language Models”) reporting sink-free attention, fewer massive activations and more stable training. (The Qwen3.8 report credits the model's training stability mainly to the residual gate, the Gated Residual of chapter 15, and not to this attention gate.) The figure compares the four situations.

Compare the mechanisms. The sink reduces the sum of the weights: the head goes quiet when no score beats \(\sigma\). The gate multiplies the output: the head goes quiet when the token itself decides to shut it, whatever the score. DeepSeek-V4 chose the sink, Qwen3.8 chose the gate, and MiMo-V2.6 puts sinks only in the sliding-window layers. Interpretation: in a 128-token window, the first token slides out of the window, and without an explicit sink the head would lose its “drain”, which is exactly what StreamingLLM observed. GLM-5.3's config declares neither a sink nor a gate in the MLA.

Config parameter"output_gate_type": "sigmoid"

In Qwen3.8 both mixer families have a sigmoid output gate. The output_gate_type field is documented in the reference configuration as the activation of the linear attention (Gated DeltaNet) output gate: sigmoid, instead of the original GDN's SiLU (the technical report compares the two). In the QSA the gate is always sigmoid (attn_output * torch.sigmoid(gate)). It is what doubles q_proj: [12,288 × 2560] = 24 heads × (256 query + 256 gate), side by side per head, which keeps a head's query and gate always on the same GPU. At TP=2 vLLM fuses everything into one (6656, 2560) projection per GPU: 12 × (256 + 256) of query and gate + 256 of K + 256 of V (inferred decomposition). The QSA sparse kernel receives the gate as an argument (output_gate), that is, the multiplication is done together with the attention instead of costing another pass through memory (interpretation based on the kernel signature).

Sinks need backend support

A sink is one extra term inside the softmax, so the kernel has to receive it. PyTorch's SDPA attention doesn't accept sinks, and the HF implementation of MiMo falls back to eager mode when they exist. In vLLM, the backend has to accept per-layer sinks. If your new model has a sink and the chosen backend ignores it, the result is a plain softmax: no error, just wrong weights.

In practice, when you open the config of a freshly released model, run this checklist for attention:

  1. Count the heads per layer type. num_attention_heads, num_key_value_heads and head_dim (and v_head_dim, if present), split by layer type when there are fields like swa_num_key_value_heads. MiMo-V2.6 has 64 query heads with 4 KV heads in the global layers and 8 in the window layers.
  2. Compute the scale and look for extra multipliers. \(1/\sqrt{d_h}\) is the default, but check the reference code: MiMo multiplies V by attention_value_scale = 0.707 before attention.
  3. Check whether K and V have the same dimension. MiMo has K of 192 and V of 128, and that requires a “DiffKV” backend (on the GB10, Triton), because the common kernels assume \(d_h = d_v\).
  4. Identify each module's mask. Causal target, bidirectional vision, block drafter bidirectional within the block. An is_causal in the drafter config is an instruction for the backend.
  5. Look for sinks, gates and QK-Norm. Fields like attn_sink, add_swa_attention_sink_bias, output_gate_type, q_norm/k_norm tensors or a q_proj twice the expected size. Each one has to be supported by the kernel you're going to use.
  6. Test divisibility by TP. Query and KV heads divided by the number of GPUs, and the kernel constraints (power of 2, list of supported head counts, Q a multiple of KV). Padding is possible, but with zero weights and sink \(-\infty\).
  7. Do the KV-per-token math. \(L_{kv}\cdot H_{kv}\cdot(d_h+d_v)\cdot b\) for the softmax attention layers (linear ones store a fixed-size state), and then refine it with what chapter 10 and Part III add (latents, windows, compression).

Attention is half of every Transformer block. The other half is the feed-forward network that processes each token on its own, and the two are stitched together by the residual stream and the normalizations. That is what the next chapter builds.
