A frontier LLM is not “a program you install”. It is a giant file of numbers (the weights), a mathematical design that says how to combine them (the architecture) and an engine that executes that design dozens of times per second for each conversation — close to a trillion multiplications and additions per second — on hardware with very concrete limits. A recipe is the set of decisions that makes these worlds fit into one another. In this chapter you will meet the three recipes that serve as the lab for the whole platform — with their real numbers, plus a second version of GLM that swaps the engine — do the first calculation every recipe does, peek at the freshly released model from the recipe-from-scratch tutorial (chapter 46), and learn to use the platform, including with a copilot running on your Mac.

Score, orchestra and conductor

Think of a symphony. The score is the weights: billions of numbers learned during training. The orchestra is the hardware: memory, compute units, the network that links the machines. The conductor is the inference engine (in all three recipes, vLLM): it decides who plays what, when, and how to make the most of every musician without leaving anyone idle. And the musical form — sonata, fugue, rondo — is the architecture: the rule that says in what order and in what way those notes combine. The conductor can also be swapped: the GLM recipe gained a second version, with the same score and the same orchestra, conducted by another engine, and the numbers change a lot (section 6).

In the figure below, walk through the four worlds. Start with the raw numbers and finish at the machines: each step shows what that world contributes to the recipe and where the difficulty lives.

Look at step 2. What the model “does” with a token is, essentially, multiplying a vector by matrices: each output is the dot product of one row of the matrix with the vector — multiply, add, next row. A GLM-5.3-Flash token goes through 45 layers, each with several matrices like this, and every number of every matrix used has to leave memory and reach the compute units. Hold on to that picture: it is what explains, in a moment, why an LLM's speed is usually limited by memory and not by compute. (The algebra behind it lives in Tensors & algebra.)

Analogy

A score written for 120 musicians doesn't fit a chamber orchestra. You can rewrite the score with fewer notes (quantization), split the parts between two rooms connected by headphones (distributed parallelism), or ask the conductor to anticipate entire measures and only correct the mistakes (speculative decoding). Every recipe is a combination of these strategies — plus a fourth one, which comes from the composer himself: writing for only a few musicians to play at a time (Mixture-of-Experts).

Throughout the platform you will see the same three questions come back, at ever deeper levels:

  1. Does it fit? The weights, the KV cache and the activations have to fit in the machines' combined memory — with headroom for the operating system.
  2. Is it fast? Generating a token requires reading every active weight from memory. Speed is limited by memory bandwidth far more than by compute capacity — section 3 does the math that shows why.
  3. Is it still right? Every shortcut (fewer bits, new kernels, speculative drafts) has to be measured against the reference, or the model “gets dumber” without telling you.

Each question has its own part of the trail: “does it fit?” lives in quantization and memory (Parts IV and V); “is it fast?” in the engine, speculation and distribution (Parts VI to VIII); and “is it still right?” in the quality metrics (Measuring quality) and in the measurement discipline (Benchmarking).

The anatomy of a recipe

All three recipes studied here follow the same skeleton, even if the details change a lot. Walk through the layers in the figure: each one will be a chapter (or several) later on — and the text of each step shows how the three recipes filled in that layer (and, where it changes, the version of GLM on the TensorFold engine, with the badge).

Notice that the bottom layers (hardware, network, software image) are “infrastructure” decisions, and the top ones (topology, arguments, speculation) are “inference” decisions. In the middle sits the layer that surprises newcomers the most: patches and overlays. A model released last week almost never runs “out of the box” on unusual hardware; the recipe ships the fixes, each with its reason. A well-made recipe documents the why of every decision — and that is what you will learn to reproduce.

Going deeper: why “recipe” and not “script”

A script runs; a recipe explains and defends itself. The three recipes pin versions (image digest, checkpoint revision), refuse to apply patches to code other than the expected one (fail-closed) and publish every number with its date, configuration and protocol. The lab's rule — every number is a measurement, not an estimate — runs through this entire platform. The chapter Recipe engineering breaks this craft down.

The central problem: memory and bandwidth

Before getting into any details, do the calculation every recipe does first — the back-of-the-envelope calculation. It rests on a distinction that will keep coming back: a generation has two phases. In prefill, the whole prompt goes through the model at once: each weight matrix is read once and multiplied by thousands of tokens (matrix × matrix multiplications, the big GEMMs), and the bottleneck is compute. In decode, each step produces one new token per conversation: the same matrices are read in full to multiply one vector, and the bottleneck becomes bandwidth. (The two phases, and the KV cache that connects them, are the subject of Prefill, decode & KV.)

The calculation itself is simple. A parameter stored in 16 bits takes 2 bytes; in 4 bits, half a byte. Multiply by the total parameters and compare with the available memory. Then remember that in decode every new token has to read all the active parameters from memory — and that the active ones are not always in the same format as the rest of the model.

\[ \text{weight memory} \approx N_{\text{total}} \times \frac{b_{\text{total}}}{8}\ \text{bytes} \] \[ \text{tokens/s} \lesssim \tau \times \frac{\text{aggregate bandwidth}}{B_{\text{step}}}, \qquad B_{\text{step}} \approx N_{\text{active}} \times \frac{b_{\text{active}}}{8} \]
  • \(N_{\text{total}}\): the model's total parameters (they decide whether it fits)
  • \(b_{\text{total}}\): bits per parameter averaged over the whole checkpoint (size in bytes × 8 ÷ parameters)
  • \(N_{\text{active}}\): parameters used per token (in MoE, far fewer than the total; they decide the speed)
  • \(b_{\text{active}}\): effective bits of what is read for each token. In a model entirely in one format, ≈ \(b_{\text{total}}\); in a mixed-precision MoE, much larger — the experts are at ~4 bits, but each token's read is dominated by the dense parts in FP8/BF16
  • \(B_{\text{step}}\): bytes read from memory per decode step, summed over the nodes
  • aggregate bandwidth: bytes per second that the memory of all nodes delivers together (~273 GB/s theoretical per node here)
  • \(\tau\): tokens kept per step — 1 without speculation; more than 1 when a drafter gets it right (in an MoE, verifying several tokens also increases \(B_{\text{step}}\))
The math behind “bandwidth-bound”

Multiplying a weight matrix by one vector does 2 operations per weight read (one multiplication and one addition). In BF16 each weight takes 2 bytes: ~1 FLOP per byte read; in 4 bits, ~4. This ratio is the arithmetic intensity. The GB10 does ~125 TFLOPS of dense BF16 and reads ~273 GB/s, so it can only use all of its compute above 125×10¹² ÷ 273×10⁹ ≈ 458 FLOP per byte — the ridge. At 1 to 4 FLOP/B, the GPU uses less than 1% of its compute and spends the rest of the time waiting for memory. Batches and speculation help because they put \(M\) tokens in the same step: \(I \approx 2M/b_w\), with \(b_w\) in bytes per weight. Prefill, with thousands of tokens per chunk, crosses the ridge in the dense parts. In an MoE's experts, each one receives only a fraction of the tokens, and the GLM recipe estimates ~112 FLOP/B with 1024-token chunks: still below the ridge.

The calculator below does exactly this calculation, with one extra detail on the memory side: the engine does not use all the memory. The --gpu-memory-utilization argument (the GMU) sets the fraction of each node's 121.7 GiB that vLLM may budget. Inside it go the weights, a few GiB of activations, CUDA graphs and workspaces, and whatever is left becomes KV cache; outside it sits the operating system. Start in free mode, then pick one of the presets: they load the checkpoints' real numbers and show, next to the theoretical ceiling, what the recipe measured.

Play with it a bit before moving on. With 1 node, none of the three checkpoints fits — which is why the recipes use two (Qwen has a single-node lane, but with a different, smaller checkpoint and part of the weights off the GPU). With the GLM preset, the weights take up two-thirds of the combined memory; with a GMU of 0.85 and ~9 GiB of activations, graphs and workspaces, only ~13 GiB per node is left for the KV cache (the recipe pins the pool at 11 GiB). And the ~18 GiB outside the budget aren't spare either: they belong to the operating system, and at 0.87 a 256k prefill left a node with no free memory and took it down (2026-09-06). Now look at the speedometer: with \(\tau = 1\), the presets' ceilings range from ~36 tok/s (GLM) to ~55 tok/s (DeepSeek). GLM measures 65.1 tok/s on structured text — almost twice its \(\tau = 1\) ceiling — because speculation gets several tokens out of each read of the weights.

Why this matters

This calculation explains almost every choice in the recipes: why quantize to ~4 bits (otherwise it doesn't fit), why an MoE model is viable (few active parameters per token), why split across two nodes (it adds up memory and bandwidth), why the recipes convert the dense parts to FP8 (they dominate the per-token read) and why speculative decoding is worth its weight in gold (it gets several tokens out of each read of the weights).

A ceiling, not a promise

The presets use two bit counts. The checkpoint average decides memory; the effective bits of what is read for each token decide speed — and, in a mixed-precision MoE, the two are very different. Using the checkpoint average for both is off by 1.5–2×: for GLM it would give 9.5 GB read per token and a ceiling of 57 tok/s, while the repository's cost model gives ~15 GB (≈ 5.4 GB of dense parts and ≈ 2.1 GB of experts per GPU) and ~36 tok/s. Even with the right math, the measured number lands below the ceiling. In the Qwen preset the \(\tau = 1\) ceiling is ~47 tok/s, and the recipe measured 24.5 tok/s without speculation (2026-09-05). The calculation ignores ~106 network collectives per step, small kernels that don't saturate the bandwidth, and the effective bandwidth, which sits at 230–250 GB/s rather than 273. The repository itself models the step with MTP=3 (≈ 9.2 GiB read per GPU, 40–42 ms) and concludes that single-user decode is at the bandwidth limit, but the measured step is ~60 ms: ~1/3 of it is fixed cost (see Benchmarking). Moral: the calculation tells you whether it's worth trying; the measurement tells you the rest.

Going deeper: speculating in an MoE costs more than it seems

Raising \(\tau\) in the calculator multiplies the ceiling, but verifying several tokens at once isn't free in an MoE: each position picks its own experts. In the DeepSeek recipe's cost model (TP=2, k=6), verifying 7 positions wakes as many as ~42 distinct experts per layer — not 6 — adding up to ≈ 16.5–17 GB read per GPU per step (68–74 ms). That is \(\tau \approx 4.15\) tokens per step (≈3.15 accepted drafts + the bonus token); the weight reads alone would give ~56–61 tok/s, and the range measured with one user was 62–83 tok/s on the 0731 checkpoint (on code, acceptance goes up and each step yields more tokens). The details are in Speculation: fundamentals.

The model as a stack of tensors

In practice, “the model” is a stack of layers, each with its own weight matrices. In the figure below, each slab is a layer and each little square is a block of a matrix. For scale: a GLM MoE layer has 288 experts, each with three matrices — gate and up of shape [2048 × 4096] and down of [4096 × 2048] — for 25.2 million numbers per expert. Multiply by 288 experts and 42 MoE layers and you get ~304 billion parameters in the decoder's routed experts alone (311.65 billion, the 97% that EXL3 quantizes, counting those in the MTP layer).

When we talk about tensor parallel (TP), this is what we mean: cut each slab and put half on each machine. Each node does half of the math of every layer, and the two exchange partial results over the network at every layer. In GLM, for example, the experts' gate/up matrices are split along the output dimension (column-parallel, in Megatron jargon: each node computes 1024 of the 2048 outputs, without talking to the other) and down along the input dimension (row-parallel: each node multiplies its half and produces a partial sum of the 4096 outputs). Combining the two partials costs one sum over the network, the all-reduce, per MoE layer. Qwen adds expert parallel: instead of slicing each expert, each GPU holds 256 of the 512 experts whole. Why each recipe chose what it chose is the subject of Parallelism.

Three creatures side by side

The stack above treats every layer as the same. The three recipes prove otherwise: the frontier models of 2026 are hybrids — different layers use different mechanisms to mix information across tokens (the token mixer). You don't need to know yet how each one works inside (that's Part III); one sentence of intuition for each is enough:

Global attention
each token queries all the previous ones; the KV grows with the context. It's the classic Transformer (Attention).
Sparse global
a cheap “librarian” (the indexer) picks ~512 blocks of 4 tokens (≈ 2048 positions), and the expensive attention only looks at those. In GLM (DSA) and Qwen (QSA) the attention reads those ~2048 tokens; in DeepSeek (CSA) it reads the 512 compressed entries directly (Sparse attention).
Linear / recurrent
instead of storing every token, the layer carries a fixed-size summary matrix, updated at every token — KDA in GLM, Gated DeltaNet in Qwen (Linear attention).
Sliding window
only the last W tokens (SWA; W = 128 in DeepSeek and MiMo): fixed KV per conversation.
Compressed
DeepSeek merges blocks of 4 (CSA) or 128 (HCA) tokens into a single learned KV entry (CSA/HCA).

Now look at the four real stacks side by side — the three recipes and, if you like, the model from the recipe-from-scratch tutorial (chapter 46). Each tick is a layer, in the order the token goes through them. Switch the color mode, turn on the drafters and hover over a layer to read what it is.

Notice three things. First, few layers pay KV per token: 11 of the 45 in GLM, 12 of the 48 (plus the MTP layer) in Qwen; in DeepSeek, the 41 layers whose KV grows store compressed versions (¼ or 1/128) — and all 43 also keep a fixed window with the last 128 tokens in raw form. That's why conversations of 850 thousand to 1 million tokens fit in KV pools of 11 to 17 GiB per GPU (GLM and DeepSeek). Second, almost everything is MoE: only GLM (first 3 layers) and MiMo (layer 0) have a dense FFN; DeepSeek picks the experts of its first 3 layers from a fixed table, by token id. Third, the drafters (MTP, DFlash2, DSpark) are not standalone models: they read hidden states from specific layers of the big model — the triangles mark these taps.

Why this matters for the recipe

The hybrid layout is what makes long context cheap — and also the source of a good share of the patches. Kernels and allocators written for “all layers alike” break: vLLM's allocator needs same-size pages for very different groups (in GLM, this forces 3584-token KV blocks, and the prefix cache only hits at multiples of that), and the recurrent state of the linear layers only exists at checkpoint points. An engine written for the family works around part of this: the variant keeps the prompt state on a 64-token grid instead of 3584-token pages (Exact engine). The complete explorer, field by field, is in The three models.

The spec sheet of the three recipes

All three recipes run on the same pair of machines: two DGX Spark units (GB10 GPU, compute capability sm_121, a nominal 128 GB of LPDDR5X unified memory per node — the physical pool is 128 GiB, and CUDA sees 121.7 GiB — and ~273 GB/s theoretical), connected by a ConnectX-7 cable with RoCE at 200 Gb/s. Unusual hardware

The GB10 is not a shrunken datacenter GPU. Pitfall: comparing numbers without a receipt

“Qwen does 76 tok/s and DeepSeek does 56” is a meaningless sentence: the prompts are of different kinds (structured text is easy to draft, prose is hard), and the dates and engine versions differ. And the DeepSeek lane measured 15–25% less on 2026-09-02 than on August 14 with no .env knob to explain it, according to the README — but, between the two dates, the checkpoint changed from 0731 to Vision-Exp and k from 5 to 6: one more reason to demand the full receipt. Every number on this platform comes with its date, lane and protocol — the receipt. Only compare what was measured the same way.

In the recipe

In the same lab test (bench_decode.py, 2026-08-30, median of 5 runs of 400 tokens, draft at TP=2), GLM measures 65.1 tok/s on structured text and 27.1 on prose — same recipe, same configuration, same date. The difference is speculation: DFlash2's acceptance rate is 0.959 on structured text (6.71 of 7 tokens per step), but only 0.341 on prose (2.39). That is exactly the phenomenon that motivated adaptive k, which, together with dense FP8, brought prose to 36.1 tok/s on 2026-09-17 — another run, another configuration, so that number doesn't compare with the table's 62.9 for structured text from 2026-08-28. And it is a good example of why the question “is it fast?” always comes with “for which workload?”.

Same score, another conductor: GLM on TensorFold

The GLM recipe gained a second version, in a repository of its own (v1.3.2, 2026-10-01), that touches only one layer of the anatomy, and precisely the deepest one: the engine. The checkpoint is the same 4 bpw EXL3 from the table above, running on the same two Sparks linked by the same cable. In place of vLLM comes TensorFold v0.6.0 (by Ash Hart, Apache-2.0 license), plus the recipe's 53 patches. The design bet is the opposite of vLLM's. Instead of a generic platform into which each model plugs through adapters, it is a per-family engine that brings its own kernels for GLM's layers (KDA, DSA, MoE and the hyper-connection glue). In a hybrid model, adapters are expensive, and the cost can be measured in bytes. To use vLLM's sparse MLA kernel, the recipe stores 656 B per token in each DSA layer, 128 B of which are zero-filled RoPE, in a model that doesn't use RoPE. TensorFold stores 528 B: 512 FP8 e4m3 codes, a power-of-two scale and alignment. And where vLLM's prefix cache only hits at multiples of 3584 tokens, TensorFold keeps the prompt state on a 64-token grid.

Since 2026-10-04 the author recommends this lane: the vLLM recipe's README opens with “Use the TensorFold recipe” (cafea0f), and both lanes serve the same new checkpoint. The recipes on this platform are dated snapshots: GLM vLLM at 94ae731 (2026-09-28), GLM·TF at v1.3.2 (2026-10-01), Qwen and DeepSeek read on 2026-09-28. What changed in TensorFold after that is in what changed since v1.3.2.

The engine also brings a contract vLLM doesn't offer: bit-for-bit exactness. Each token's draw uses a key (derived from the prompt or the seed, the position and the token id). That's why the reply with drafts is the same as token-by-token generation, four requests served together get the same bits they would get alone, and a prompt resumed from a kept state gives the same result as one computed from scratch. In vLLM, speculation is lossless in distribution, but batch size and the kernel path change bits. Exactness becomes an engineering tool: every optimization gets an objective test, which is checking whether the reply's hash changed. The table compares the two versions only where they differ. Architecture, expert weights and hardware are the ones in the table above.

They were measured with sparkDash through the API, from another machine on the network, under its lab protocol (temperature 0, thinking off), with the GPU clocks capped at 2,200 MHz and in the default configuration (4 streams, 1M window, FP8 KV, 4-bit dense weights, DFlash2 + copy drafts, vision on). Those in the vLLM column come from other dates and configurations. Neither recipe publishes a controlled A/B between the engines. Read the jump from 36.1 to 60.4 tok/s on prose as an order of magnitude, not as the exact gain of the swap. In the figure above, the bars carry that receipt next to them.

The engine is a recipe decision too

The swap has a price. In v1.3.2, GLM's CUDA engine required exactly two ranks, with no TP=3 or TP=4, and served at most 4 requests; since v1.4 the recipe runs on 3 Sparks (experimental, patches 0066–0068) and, since v1.5, up to 8 requests (patch 0069): see what changed since v1.3.2. Three defaults, chosen for speed and to fit 1M, are not exact against the BF16 checkpoint: 4-bit dense weights and FP8 KV are lossy, and the prefill KDA in chunked form is close to the serial kernel, but without its bits. With DENSE=q4, short French coding prompts lost the end of turn in 12 of 48 replies (1 of 48 with bf16 or fp8). The API is smaller and startup takes 2 to 6 min instead of ~2. The DFlash2 drafter still has a non-commercial license, as in the vLLM version, and the alternative without it (DRAFTER=mtp) serves one request at a time. With these defaults, the recipe publishes GSM8K 98.0% (250 problems) and HumanEval 97.6% (164) on the TR3 checkpoint, and it checked that replies come out identical with and without drafts, and with 4 requests together or alone. The engine from the inside is in Exact engine; the recipe, parameter by parameter, in GLM recipe · TensorFold.

Portrait of the newcomer: MiMo-V2.6-Flash

The platform's recipe-from-scratch tutorial (chapter 46, Recipe from scratch) takes a model published on September 27, 2026 and builds, step by step, a recipe that nobody has written to the lab's standard: Xiaomi's MiMo-V2.6-Flash (MOPD variant). It shows up here as a teaser — look at the spec sheet and try to guess, with what you have already seen in this chapter, where it's going to hurt.

The figure shows the trick that makes long context cheap in this model. Drag the context and compare the two rows: the window layers only look at the last 128 tokens, the global ones look at everything. Then switch the panel to see the other detail that is going to cause trouble: K and V of different sizes.

Notice how big the difference is: with the context full, the illustrative calculation with all 48 layers global would give almost ten times more KV. It's the same idea as in the three recipes — pay KV per token in only a few layers — by a different route. The DiffKV panel, in turn, shows why engine support is delicate: many attention kernels assume K and V have the same size per head, and on the GB10 that pushes all of MiMo's attention onto a specific Triton kernel.

  1. It fits, just barely. ~82.7 GiB per GPU measured in the lab's own SGLang recipe (sibling RL checkpoint, TP=2) against the 121.7 GiB visible: that leaves ~15–20 GiB of KV per GPU. The calculator in section 3 has a MiMo preset — do the math yourself.
  2. Silent bugs. In vLLM as of 2026-09-28 (with fixes still in PR), --kv-cache-dtype fp8 was ignored without warning, and the 128-token window could leak into the global layers through cache_config — they would then see only 128 tokens. Nothing breaks; the model just gets worse.
  3. Pre-sliced weights. The fused QKV comes on disk already cut for TP=4, and you will run TP=2: each GPU has to join pieces in the right order.
  4. Which drafter? In a community recipe on vLLM (TP=2, DFlash k=7), an average of 6.85 of the 7 draft tokens per step were accepted on structured text, versus 1.23 on prose; MTP did better on prose on another engine. Only measuring on your own workload will tell.

How to use the platform (and the copilot)

Latent Atlas was designed to be read in order, but used as a workbench. A few shortcuts worth knowing right away:

🧭

The sidebar lists the parts and chapters; the top bar shows your progress. At the end of each chapter there is a summary, a quiz and the “mark as completed” button. Shift + ←/→ switches chapters.

🔎

⌘/Ctrl K searches chapters, terms and parameters (e.g., max-num-seqs). Underlined terms show their definition on hover; the Glossary page gathers them all.

Every parameter and modification of the three recipes, with its value, file and source line, all searchable.

🎚️

Live figures

Play with the controls. ⤢ opens the figure in full screen, ✦ ask sends the figure (with the state of its controls) to the copilot, and the pause button at the top freezes every animation. The light/dark theme toggle sits right next to it.

The copilot: a tutor that reads the page

The floating button (or ⌘/Ctrl J) opens the copilot. It builds a system message with the trail and the page text — only the visible section, the whole chapter or nothing, depending on the context selector — adds your question and sends everything to a language model. Select a passage of the text and click “✦ Ask the copilot”, or use “✦ ask” at the top of any figure. The nicest detail: it speaks the OpenAI Chat Completions contract, the same one the recipes' vLLM exposes. The figure shows the path of a question.

Look at the request that shows up in the controls: only the base URL changes (plus the odd field, such as max_completion_tokens on OpenAI). That's why the same interface talks to a model on your Mac, to the cloud or to your own recipe — when you finish the trail, the tutor can be GLM, Qwen or DeepSeek running on your Sparks.

A 100% local copilot on the Mac, with MLX

If you have a Mac with Apple Silicon, you can run the tutor with no cloud at all, using Apple's MLX and the mlx_lm.server server. The platform ships a script that picks the model based on the Mac's memory:

  1. Serve the platform over HTTP. ./serve.sh opens it at http://localhost:8765. Over file://, MLX works too, but other local servers reject the browser's null origin (CORS).
  2. Start the model. ./tools/start-mlx-copilot.sh installs mlx-lm if it's missing (it requires Python ≥ 3.10, and macOS's python3 is 3.9 — the script uses uv or brew) and starts the server on 127.0.0.1:8080. With ≥ 64 GB it picks Qwen3.8-27B-8bit; ≥ 32 GB, Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507-4bit; ≥ 24 GB, gemma-4-26b-a4b-it-4bit; ≥ 16 GB, Qwen3.5-9B-MLX-4bit; below that, Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507-4bit.
  3. Configure the copilot. ⚙ → provider “Local MLX”, Base URL http://127.0.0.1:8080/v1, API key empty, Model empty → “Test connection”.
./serve.sh                              # platform at http://localhost:8765
./tools/start-mlx-copilot.sh            # picks the model based on the Mac's RAM
./tools/start-mlx-copilot.sh 24         # forces the 24 GB profile
MODEL=mlx-community/gpt-oss-20b-MXFP4-Q8 ./tools/start-mlx-copilot.sh   # a model of your choice
curl http://127.0.0.1:8080/v1/models    # checks that the server is up
Three gotchas of the local copilot

Leave the Model field empty: if the copilot sends an id different from the one loaded, mlx_lm.server switches models and, if the new one isn't in the cache, downloads it from Hugging Face on the spot. Watch out for thinking: in one test, a 4 B model spent 1500 of 1500 tokens just reasoning and returned an empty answer — that's why the MLX preset already asks for an answer without thinking (chat_template_kwargs: {"enable_thinking": false}). Weights ≤ ~60% of RAM: the rest goes to the KV cache, macOS and the browser.

The back-of-the-envelope calculation works on your Mac too

The model names already tell this chapter's story: “4bit” and “MXFP4” are quantization (Part IV); “30B-A3B” means 30 billion total and 3 billion active — an MoE, fast because it reads few bytes per token. Do the math: ~30.5 B × ~4.5 bits (4 bits + group scales) ÷ 8 ≈ 17 GB of weights — practically the size of Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct-2507-4bit (17.18 GB).

How this trail is organized

The order follows the sequence in which you learn: each part builds on the previous ones. Parts X to XIII form Module 2 (post-training), which starts at chapter 50. If you already master a subject, skip it — the prerequisites at the top of each chapter say exactly what it depends on. A fun way to look at the trail is to ask the reverse question: to understand a real parameter of a recipe, which stations do I need to pass through? Pick a parameter in the figure and follow the train.

Notice that every route starts at the bottom — numbers, tensors, attention — and ends in a recipe. That's no accident: a parameter like --kv-cache-dtype fp8 looks like a single configuration line, but behind it are the FP8 format, the geometry of MLA's KV, vLLM's paged allocator and a kernel that has to know how to read that layout. It's this stack of whys that the trail builds, one station at a time.
