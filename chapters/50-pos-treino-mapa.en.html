Ask a base model “What is the capital of France?” and it may answer “What is the capital of Germany?”. That is not a bug: in exercise lists on the web, one question is usually followed by another. The same tensors, after a few stages that cost a fraction of pretraining, become an assistant that follows instructions, refuses what it should refuse and thinks before answering. For InstructGPT, SFT on the 175B model cost 4.9 petaflop/s-days and RL (PPO-ptx) cost 60, against 3,640 for GPT-3's pretraining. That set of stages is post-training, and this chapter opens Module 2 with the whole map of it: why it exists, the pipeline and its 2026 variants, the four concepts that cut across every method, the module's taxonomy, what GLM, Qwen, DeepSeek and MiMo have published, and where the Module 1 inference engine enters the loop.

What pretraining does not deliver

Pretraining is the giant, self-supervised phase: predicting the next token over trillions of tokens. MiMo-V2-Flash used 27 trillion tokens in mixed FP8; DeepSeek-V4 went past 32 trillion; GLM-5.3-Flash, according to its card, saw 30 trillion multimodal tokens. The result is a model that knows a lot (facts, grammar, code, math) but can do only one thing with that knowledge: continue text in the most likely way. As you saw in Learning from data, the pretraining loss is the next-token cross-entropy, and it rewards imitating the web, not helping someone.

There are five gaps, and every method in this module attacks one or more of them:

  1. Format. An assistant converses in turns (system, user, assistant), written with the special tokens of the chat template, and knows how to stop at the end of its turn. The base model knows none of this: it chains one question onto the next and keeps going until the token limit.
  2. Following instructions. “Answer in JSON”, “three items”, “no markdown”. The base sometimes obeys by accident, when the web text it imitates happened to do so.
  3. Preferences. Between two correct answers, which one is more useful, clearer, in the right tone? That is not written anywhere in the corpus. It comes from human (or AI) comparisons.
  4. Safety. Refusing what should be refused, without refusing what is legitimate (over-refusal is a defect too).
  5. Verifiable reasoning. Spending tokens thinking before answering, so that the final answer passes a verifier (the math checks out, the code passes the tests). It is the hallmark of reasoning models.

The recent history of post-training has three rungs. The base model completes text. The instruct model (assistant) gains format, instruction following and refusal. The milestone is InstructGPT (2022): a post-trained 1.3B model was preferred by raters over the 175B GPT-3, a model more than 100 times larger. And the reasoning model (2024–25) learns, through reinforcement learning on tasks with checkable answers, to write a long chain of thought between <think> and </think>. In the figure, the same prompt goes through all three rungs. Watch the next token's probability mass move around.

Notice two things. In the base model, the mass is spread over “web” continuations (a line break, another question) and the entropy is high; SFT concentrates it on the answer format, and reasoning RL pushes almost all of it onto <think>. And, on the arithmetic prompt, the instruct model still puts a good share of the mass on wrong results: that is the gap that RL with verifiable reward attacks, with nobody writing down the right answer, only a verifier that says whether “408” is right.

Analogy: the encyclopedic intern

The base model is an intern who has read the entire library and never worked a day. It knows everything, but doesn't know how to do the job. SFT is the first month of “watch how it's done”: the intern copies demonstrations. Preference optimization is the boss saying “this answer is better than that one”. RL with verifiable reward is objective targets (do the report's numbers add up? does the code pass the tests?), and the intern figures out alone how to get there. And the KL leash, which we'll see shortly, is company policy: change as much as you like, as long as you stay recognizable.

An influential thesis says that almost all knowledge comes from pretraining and that post-training mostly teaches format and style: LIMA's “superficial alignment hypothesis”. With only 1,000 curated examples on a LLaMA-65B, LIMA's answers were equivalent to or preferred over GPT-4's in 43% of cases. Verifiable reasoning changed that story. DeepSeek-R1-Zero, trained by RL directly on the base, without a single demonstration, went from 15.6% to 71.0% accuracy on AIME 2024 (77.9% in the paper's revised version), and response length grew from hundreds to thousands of tokens without anyone asking for it. Modern post-training doesn't only teach style: it also develops capabilities the base had only in potential.

Why this matters for whoever serves models

Post-training does not change the architecture: it is the same tensors, adjusted. Everything Module 1 taught (quantization, KV, speculation, parallelism) applies to any post-trained checkpoint. But post-training leaves marks the server must respect: the chat template, the reasoning parser, the generation_config with the recommended sampling (Qwen3.8 asks for temperature 1.0, top_p 0.95, top_k 20 in thinking mode) and the end tokens (eos_token_id [248046, 248044], that is, <|im_end|> and <|endoftext|>). Serve it wrong and you undo the post-training.

The fourth pipeline is the pattern that took hold in 2025–26: specialists + fusion. Instead of a single RL run that tries to teach everything at once (and suffers from interference between domains), several specialists are trained from the same SFT model, each with its own RL (math, code, agents, tools, safety), and then everything is fused into a single model. The fusion comes in three flavors in the sources: (a) offline distillation on data generated by the specialists followed by mixed RL (DeepSeek-V3.2, GLM-4.5); (b) on-policy distillation with several teachers, MiMo's MOPD and DeepSeek-V4's OPD; (c) sequential RL with “cross-stage” distillation so as not to forget what the earlier stages taught (GLM-5). In DeepSeek-V3.2, the post-training budget already exceeded 10% of the pretraining cost, and climbing.

Distilling is cheap; RL is expensive

For small models, copying a strong teacher pays off more than doing RL from scratch. The R1-Distill-* models are SFT only on R1's 800K examples, and the 32B distilled model scored 72.6% on AIME 2024 versus 47.0% for an RL run of more than 10,000 steps at the same size. On Qwen3-8B, on-policy distillation reached 74.4 on AIME’24 versus 67.6 for RL, with 1,800 GPU-hours versus 17,920 (~1/10). RL is left for frontier models, which have no one to copy from, and for the teachers.

The leash: KL to the reference model

Almost every method in this module carries, explicitly or implicitly, the same objective. Maximize a reward, but pay a toll proportional to how far you stray from a frozen reference model, almost always the SFT checkpoint:

\[ \max_{\pi_\theta}\;\; \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal D,\; y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\big[\,r(x,y)\,\big] \;-\; \beta\,\mathrm{KL}\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\,\big\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big) \]
  • \(x\): the prompt, drawn from the prompt set \(\mathcal D\)
  • \(y\): the whole response, sampled from the policy \(\pi_\theta\) itself (the LLM being trained, with weights \(\theta\))
  • \(r(x,y)\): scalar reward (reward model, verifier or judge)
  • \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\): the frozen reference policy (the SFT model)
  • \(\beta\): leash strength; \(\mathrm{KL}\) in nats, the Kullback–Leibler divergence you measured in Measuring quality

This problem has a closed-form solution, if the policy can be any distribution at all. It is the reference exponentially tilted by the reward:

\[ \pi^*(y\mid x) \;=\; \frac{1}{Z(x)}\;\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\;\exp\!\Big(\frac{r(x,y)}{\beta}\Big), \qquad Z(x) = \sum_{y}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,e^{\,r(x,y)/\beta} \]
  • \(\pi^*\): the optimal policy for the objective above
  • \(Z(x)\): normalization constant (a sum over all possible responses, hence intractable in practice)
  • \(\beta\) plays the role of a temperature: \(\beta\to\infty\) gives \(\pi^* = \pi_{\text{ref}}\); \(\beta\to 0\) concentrates everything on the argmax of \(r\)

Why is that the solution? Solve the formula for \(r = \beta\log(\pi^*/\pi_{\text{ref}}) + \beta\log Z\) and substitute it into the objective; one line of algebra gives

\[ \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi}\big[r(x,y)\big] - \beta\,\mathrm{KL}\big(\pi\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}\big) \;=\; -\beta\,\mathrm{KL}\big(\pi\,\|\,\pi^*\big) + \beta\log Z(x) \]

Since KL is ≥ 0 and is zero only at \(\pi = \pi^*\), the optimum is \(\pi^*\). And maximizing the objective is exactly minimizing the reverse KL to \(\pi^*\), which will come back in the next section. Read the formula as a reweighting rule: each response keeps the weight the SFT model gave it, multiplied by \(e^{r/\beta}\). Good responses gain mass and bad ones lose it, but nothing the SFT model considered impossible suddenly becomes likely. The figure does this calculation exactly for eight toy responses. Lower \(\beta\) and watch the leash stretch. Then turn on the “RM with a loophole”: the reward model starts overrating a bad response (a long, repetitive list, the kind of thing RMs with a length bias love).

With the honest RM, the two curves rise together. With the loophole, the RM curve keeps climbing while the true quality rises, peaks and then collapses: that is Goodhart's law (“when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure”) in chart form. Gao, Schulman and Hilton measured this hump with a synthetic “gold” RM: the true reward rises and then falls as the proxy RM is optimized, as a function of \(d = \sqrt{\mathrm{KL}}\). That is why the leash exists. The sources give three reasons:

  1. Keep the RM valid. The RM is only reliable near the responses it saw in training. The leash keeps the policy from escaping into regions where it is wrong (the main reason in Stiennon et al.'s summarization paper).
  2. Preserve capabilities. Forgetting tracks the KL to the base model on the new task's distribution (“RL's Razor”). A short leash forgets less.
  3. Entropy bonus. Since \(-\beta\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi\|\pi_{\text{ref}}) = \beta H(\pi) + \beta\,\mathbb E_\pi[\log\pi_{\text{ref}}]\), the penalty rewards the policy's entropy and keeps it from collapsing into a single response mode.

In the classic implementation (InstructGPT, PPO), the leash goes into the per-token reward. The RM score only shows up at the last token, and every token pays its share of KL:

\[ r_t \;=\; -\beta\Big(\log\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t\mid s_t) - \log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_t\mid s_t)\Big) \;+\; \mathbb 1[t=T]\;r_\phi(x,y) \]
  • \(s_t = (x, y_{<t})\): the state is the prompt plus the prefix generated so far; the action is the token \(y_t\)
  • \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\): the policy that generated the batch; \(T\): the last token of the response (the EOS)
  • \(r_\phi(x,y)\): the reward model's score; \(\mathbb 1[t=T]\) equals 1 only at the last token
  • With discount \(\gamma = 1\), the sum of the \(r_t\) is \(r_\phi - \beta\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}\): a score of 2.0 with \(\beta = 0.05\) and a summed log-ratio of 10 nats is worth 1.5
Deep dive: estimating KL from a single sample

The exact KL sums over the entire vocabulary at each position. In practice it is estimated with the sampled token, using \(x = \pi_{\text{ref}}(y_t)/\pi_\theta(y_t)\). There are three classic estimators (Schulman, 2020): \(k_1 = -\log x\) (unbiased, high variance, can go negative), \(k_2 = \tfrac12(\log x)^2\) (biased, low variance) and \(k_3 = (x-1) - \log x\) (unbiased and always ≥ 0, which is what GRPO uses in its loss). With \(\pi_\theta = 0.01\) and \(\pi_{\text{ref}} = 0.2\): \(k_1 = -2.996\) (negative!), \(k_2 = 4.487\) and \(k_3 = 16.004\), which blows up when the policy gives little mass to a token the reference finds likely. That case motivated DeepSeek-V3.2's “unbiased KL”; details in PPO & RLHF and GRPO & RLVR.

How big is \(\beta\) in practice? It depends on the method and on the normalization, so never compare \(\beta\) across variants. But the order of magnitude tells a story:

And hold on to the inversion we used above, \(r = \beta\log(\pi^*/\pi_{\text{ref}}) + \beta\log Z\): it is what gives birth to DPO, which optimizes preferences with no reward model and no RL.

Whose data is it? On-policy and off-policy

Call \(\mu\) the policy that generated the data and \(\pi_\theta\) the one being trained. Training is on-policy when \(\mu = \pi_\theta\) (or very close to it): the model learns from text it has just produced itself. PPO, GRPO, RLOO, on-policy distillation and MOPD work this way. Training is off-policy when \(\mu \neq \pi_\theta\): the data comes from humans, from a teacher or from an old version of the model. SFT, off-policy distillation, offline DPO and AWR work this way.

The difference is not a technicality. In SFT, the model is trained with teacher forcing: at each position, the prefix is the dataset's, and it is perfect. At generation time, the prefix is the model's own, mistakes included. A small deviation is enough for the model to land in a state it never saw in training. There it makes more mistakes, drifts further, and the errors compound. That is exposure bias. On-policy methods train exactly on the states the model visits, including the ones that come after a mistake, and that is why they learn to recover.

Even with \(\varepsilon = 2\%\) per token, only 45% of 40-token responses come out without a single deviation; a chain of thought has thousands of tokens. That is why on-policy distillation and RL generalize better in long generation, and why MiMo-V2.6's MOPD2, as we'll see, has the student generate from realistic prefixes.

Forward KL and reverse KL

There is a more mathematical way to see the same difference, and it is the most useful conceptual distinction in the module. Minimizing SFT's cross-entropy is minimizing the forward KL from the data to the model. Training on-policy with KL, or distilling on-policy, is minimizing the reverse KL, from the model to the target:

\[ \mathcal L_{\text{SFT}} = \mathbb E_{y\sim p_{\text{data}}}\big[-\log\pi_\theta(y\mid x)\big] = \mathrm{KL}\big(p_{\text{data}}\,\|\,\pi_\theta\big) + H(p_{\text{data}}) \] \[ \mathcal L_{\text{OPD}} = \mathbb E_{y\sim\pi_\theta}\Big[\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_T(y\mid x)}\Big] = \mathrm{KL}\big(\pi_\theta\,\|\,\pi_T\big) \]
  • \(p_{\text{data}}\): the distribution of the demonstrations; \(H(p_{\text{data}})\) does not depend on \(\theta\)
  • \(\pi_T\): the target (a teacher, in distillation; \(\pi^*\), in RL with KL)
  • Note where \(y\) comes from: in the first line, from the dataset (off-policy); in the second, from the model itself (on-policy)

The forward KL punishes the model whenever it gives low probability to something the data does. That is why it is mass-covering: it spreads mass over every mode, including the valley between them, where the data has nothing. The reverse KL punishes the model for putting mass where the target puts none. That is why it is mode-seeking: it picks one mode it can represent well and covers that one properly. When the student is smaller than the teacher and can't imitate everything, this second choice is usually the better one. The figure fits a Gaussian (the student) to a target with two modes. The control interpolates between the two KLs through the generalized JSD that TRL's DistillationTrainer uses (\(\beta = 0\): forward; \(\beta = 1\): reverse; the DistillationConfig default is 1).

At the forward-KL extreme, the student becomes a wide Gaussian centered near zero, with a good share of its mass in the valley: it generates “average” answers that not even the target would give. At the reverse extreme, it snaps onto the larger mode and ignores the other. On this target the switch is almost a jump: up to \(\beta = 0.4\) the student stays wide, and already at \(\beta = 0.5\) it jumps to the left mode (from the figure's own grid search). Neither one is “the right one”; they are two different bets. The table below summarizes the combination that made on-policy distillation so popular in 2025–26:

“On-policy” in practice is approximate

In RL frameworks, the responses are generated by an inference engine (vLLM or SGLang) and the gradient is computed in the training engine (FSDP or Megatron). Different kernels, different precisions (FP8 rollouts, for example) and different summation orders make \(\mu_\theta \neq \pi_\theta\) even with the same weights, and asynchronous generation adds staleness on top. That is why MiMo weights each token by \(w_t = \mathrm{sg}[\pi_\theta(y_t)/\mu_\theta(y_t)]\) and discards the tokens whose ratio falls outside a range \([\varepsilon_{\text{low}}, \varepsilon_{\text{high}}]\). It is the same theme as the nondeterminism in Numbers in the computer and Measuring quality: floating-point addition is not associative, so two “identical” engines don't give the same log-probs.

Reward, preference and who judges

Every method needs some kind of yardstick. There are two basic formats. A reward is a number per response (or per step): 1 if the math checks out, 0.73 according to a reward model. A preference is a comparison between two responses to the same prompt: “A is better than B”. Humans are better at comparing than at scoring: Christiano et al. (2017), at the origin of modern RLHF, noted that consistent comparisons are much easier to get than absolute scores, because comparing doesn't require a yardstick shared across annotators. The Bradley–Terry model bridges the two worlds: it assumes a latent reward and says that the probability of preferring \(y_w\) over \(y_l\) depends only on the difference.

\[ P(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) = \sigma\big(r(x,y_w) - r(x,y_l)\big), \qquad \mathcal L_{\text{RM}} = -\log\sigma\big(r_\phi(x,y_w) - r_\phi(x,y_l)\big) \]
  • \(y_w, y_l\): the preferred response (winner, “chosen”) and the rejected one (loser, “rejected”)
  • \(\sigma(z) = 1/(1+e^{-z})\): the sigmoid
  • \(r_\phi\): the reward model, usually the LLM itself with a scalar head read at the last token
  • The loss is a logistic regression: training the RM is teaching a classifier “which of the two won”

The second question is who judges. The sources bring up five judges, and the module devotes chapters to each of them:

Humans
compare pairs or rank lists. InstructGPT used about 40 contractors, screened for agreement with the researchers. It is expensive, slow and limited by inter-annotator agreement, which is the RM's ceiling (Preferences & RMs).
A reward model
learned from those pairs, it becomes a cheap, tireless judge, but one that can be fooled (reward hacking).
A verifier
an executable rule: numerical equivalence of the answer in \boxed{}, a compiler and test cases, F2P/P2P tests in a real repository, a format regex, the share of words in the right language. It is the judge of RLVR.
An AI with principles
an LLM compares responses according to a written constitution: the RLAIF of Constitutional AI, and the generative judges with rubrics that DeepSeek-V3.2/V4, GLM-5 and MiMo use for hard-to-verify tasks.
A teacher or the model itself
in on-policy distillation, the judge is the log-prob of a stronger model; in self-play and self-rewarding, it is a version of the model itself (Self-play & debate). In DeepSeek-V4, the actor is itself the generative judge, and RL also optimizes the ability to judge.

A single lens: the gradient coefficient

The GRPO paper (DeepSeekMath, 2024) proposes seeing SFT, rejection sampling, PPO, GRPO and company as cases of one and the same gradient. They all push the log-prob of each token of the response up or down; what changes is where the data comes from and how hard, and in which direction, each token is pushed:

\[ \nabla_\theta J_{\mathcal A}(\theta) \;=\; \mathbb E_{(q,o)\sim\mathcal D}\Big[\frac{1}{|o|}\sum_{t}\;\mathrm{GC}_{\mathcal A}(q,o,t,\pi_{rf})\;\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(o_t\mid q, o_{<t})\Big] \]
  • \(q\): the prompt; \(o\): the response, with \(|o|\) tokens; \(o_t\): the \(t\)-th token
  • \(\mathcal D\): the data source (a fixed dataset? samples from the current policy?)
  • \(\pi_{rf}\): the reward source (rule, RM, verifier, teacher)
  • \(\mathrm{GC}_{\mathcal A}\): the gradient coefficient of method \(\mathcal A\); positive raises the token, negative lowers it

Three RL words show up in almost every coefficient. The advantage \(\hat A\) measures how much a response (or a token) came out better than expected: the reward minus a baseline \(b\). Subtracting \(b\) doesn't change the expected gradient, because \(\sum_y \pi_\theta(y)\,\nabla\log\pi_\theta(y) = \nabla\sum_y \pi_\theta(y) = \nabla 1 = 0\), so \(\mathbb E[\nabla\log\pi_\theta]\,b = 0\); it only reduces the variance. Without it, a response with reward 0.9 would be pushed up even in a group where the others score 1.0. In GRPO, \(b\) is the group mean. In PPO, \(b\) is the prediction \(V(s_t)\) of a critic, a second network that estimates, at each prefix, the reward still to come, and GAE sums that prediction's errors from there onward:

\[ \hat A = r - b, \qquad \delta_t = r_t + V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t), \qquad \hat A_t^{\text{GAE}} = \sum_{k\ge 0} (\gamma\lambda)^k\,\delta_{t+k} \]
  • \(b\): the baseline; any value that does not depend on the sampled action
  • \(\delta_t\): the “surprise” of token \(t\), how much the critic's prediction changed after it
  • \(\gamma\): discount (1 in LLMs); \(\lambda \in [0, 1]\): how far back the surprise spreads (less variance with a small \(\lambda\), less bias with a large \(\lambda\))

In SFT, \(\mathrm{GC} = 1\) on every token of the demonstration. In rejection sampling (RFT), it is 1 on the correct responses and 0 on the wrong ones. In GRPO, it is the group advantage, \(\hat A_i = (r_i - \text{mean}(r))/\mathrm{std}(r)\), the same for every token of response \(i\). In PPO, it is GAE's per-token advantage: in the figure's PPO mode, the (illustrative) critic predicted 0.72 before “418” and 0.05 after it, so \(\delta \approx -0.67\) at that token, and GAE spreads this surprise to the earlier ones. In on-policy distillation, it is \(\log\pi_T(o_t) - \log\pi_\theta(o_t)\), different at each token. The figure paints each case over the same arithmetic problem, “17 × 24”.

The figure sums up the whole module in six rows. SFT pushes everything equally, including tokens the model already knew. GRPO only knows whether the whole response was right: it punishes the “17”, “×” and “24” of the wrong response as much as the “418” that was the actual error. PPO's critic tries to distribute the credit, at the cost of a second trainable model. And on-policy distillation points a finger at the exact token: it is the dense signal the table in the previous section was talking about. That is why it gets so much out of each sample: in the MOPD paper, the student reaches the teacher's plateau with ~25K IF samples and ~30K SWE samples, versus 150–180K per domain in mixed RL.

The judge defines what gets optimized and what gets hacked

A strong optimizer finds the judge's loophole. The 2019 summarizers turned into “smart copiers”, copying whole sentences from the text because the labelers used copying as a quality heuristic. In MiMo-V2.6, the penalty for excessive tool calls only kicked in above 32 calls in a turn, and RL kept amplifying the behavior just below that threshold: the model repeated identical calls, “looking busy without making progress”. Verifiers are hackable too: responses with several \boxed{} boxes, ones that repeat the problem statement, or ones that leave the answer only inside <think>. Defense starts with designing the judge together with the method.

The taxonomy: every method in the module and what each one requires

With the four axes in hand (leash, data origin, signal format, judge), every method can be placed on the map. One sentence of intuition for each family:

SFT
imitation by maximum likelihood. The foundation of everything, including RFT/STaR (generate, keep the correct ones, do SFT on them) and off-policy distillation (SFT on a teacher's outputs).
RLHF
human preferences → reward model (Bradley–Terry) → PPO with KL to the SFT model. Four models in memory: policy, reference, RM and critic.
DPO, IPO, KTO, SimPO
direct preference optimization, with no explicit RM and no RL. DPO uses the closed-form solution \(\pi^*\) to turn RLHF into a logistic loss over pairs; IPO swaps the logistic for a quadratic loss that doesn't saturate with deterministic preferences; KTO learns from binary good/bad labels, without pairs; SimPO drops the reference model and uses the mean per-token log-prob with a margin.
PPO and custom RL
PPO is RLHF's default algorithm (importance ratio with clipping, a critic with GAE). Against an RM or a verifier, the optimizer can be swapped: A3C and TRPO, where the ideas of asynchronous actors and of a trust region come from, and the critic-free family (REINFORCE, RLOO, GRPO), whose baseline is the group mean.
AWR and contrastive
AWR (Advantage-Weighted Regression) does SFT weighted by \(e^{A/\beta}\), the same “exponential tilt” as \(\pi^*\); contrastive methods (unlikelihood, SLiC, CPO) push the positive up and the negative down directly.
Constitutional AI
written principles replace human harm labels: the model critiques and revises its own responses (SL-CAI), and an AI labels pairs according to the principles to train a preference model and do RL (RL-CAI, or RLAIF).
Self-play and debate
the model generates its own adversary, curriculum or judge. SPIN is a DPO in which the rejected response comes from the model's own previous iteration; in debate, two agents argue and a weaker judge decides.

The figure is a requirements matrix: rows are methods, columns are ingredients. Pick an ingredient and the rows that need it rise to the top. Click a row to see the details and the memory math.

The memory math explains half of the module's story. A model trained with AdamW in mixed precision stores 2 bytes of BF16 weights, 2 of gradients and 12 of FP32 optimizer state (master copy, momentum and variance): 16 bytes per parameter. Classic PPO has two trainable models (policy and critic) and two frozen ones (reference and RM), or \(2\times 16N + 2\times 2N = 36N\) bytes. With \(N = 7\)B, that is 252 GB (234.7 GiB) of states alone, nearly all the combined memory of two DGX Sparks (2 × 121.69 GiB visible to CUDA), before activations and the rollouts' KV cache. Hence the search for critic-free methods (GRPO, RLOO), RL-free ones (DPO) and reference-free ones (SimPO, ORPO). And hence LoRA: with adapters, the reference becomes “the same network with the adapter switched off”, with no extra copy. The full math is in Training memory & LoRA.

Quick selection guide

Have demonstrations? Start with SFT, always. Have pairs and an SFT model? DPO (IPO if the labels are few or deterministic). Have imbalanced 👍/👎 from production? KTO. Short on memory and no reference? SimPO or ORPO. Have a verifier (math, code, format)? GRPO with RLVR. No verifier and no pairs? An AI judge with rubrics, or a constitution. Want to fix one specific defect without redoing the RL? Train a small teacher and distill on-policy, as MiMo did with the repeated tool calls. This guide is an interpretation of the papers' own recommendations.

What the Module 1 families did

The three recipes serve checkpoints from three families, and the tutorial in the Recipe from scratch chapter serves a fourth. Only technical reports, cards and official blogs count as primary sources, and none of the three recipe checkpoints (GLM-5.3-Flash, Qwen3.8-Flash-Next, DeepSeek-V4-Flash) details its own post-training: whatever is stated comes from the families' reports.

GLM (Z.ai): RL in series and distillation back

The GLM-5.3-Flash card points to the GLM-5 report (Feb 2026). There, SFT has three data groups, context of up to 202,752 tokens and a chat template with three thinking modes: Interleaved (thinks before every response and every tool call), Preserved (keeps the thinking blocks across the conversation, in coding agents) and Turn-level (on or off per turn). Then come three RL stages in series. The reasoning one uses GRPO with asymmetric clipping (\(\varepsilon_{\text{low}} = 0.2\), \(\varepsilon_{\text{high}} = 0.28\)) and the IcePop mask (threshold \(\beta = 2\), a different β, not the leash's: tokens with \(\pi_{\text{train}}/\pi_{\text{inference}}\) outside \([0.5, 2]\) are dropped from the gradient), a group size of 32, fully on-policy and with no KL term, with a deterministic DSA indexer top-k and the indexer frozen. The agentic one is asynchronous, built on the slime framework, with more than 10,000 verifiable software-engineering and terminal scenarios. The general one uses hybrid rewards (rules, ORM and generative judges) and responses from human experts as style anchors. At the end, a cross-stage on-policy distillation, with earlier checkpoints as teachers, recovers what the earlier stages knew. In GLM-5.2, agentic RL switched to SAO, bringing a critic back. As for GLM-5.3-Flash, the official API doesn't let you turn thinking off and offers reasoning_effort low/high/max (default max).

Qwen (Alibaba): four stages and distillation for the small ones

The Qwen3 report (2025) describes four stages for the flagship models: a cold start with verified long chains of thought; reasoning RL with GRPO on 3,995 query–verifier pairs (AIME’24 from 70.1 to 85.1 in 170 steps); fusion of the thinking and non-thinking modes through SFT, with the /think and /no_think flags and a thinking budget; and general RL on more than 20 tasks. The small models get “strong-to-weak” distillation, first off-policy and then on-policy. After that, Qwen published GSPO (“contributed to the remarkable improvements in the latest Qwen3 models”) and SAPO (used in the Qwen3-VL series). The Qwen3.8 README and the Qwen3.8-Flash-Next technical report, which covers architecture and training stability (QSA, Gated Residual, Muon), do not describe the post-training.

DeepSeek: from GRPO to OPD with more than ten teachers

V3 (Dec 2024) did SFT with 1.5 million instances, whose reasoning data came from an internal R1, then RL with GRPO and rule-based and model-based reward models, the latter trained with the chain of thought that leads to the reward (to make hacking harder). R1 you have already seen. V3.2 (Dec 2025) trained specialists in six domains from the same base and fused them all in a single-stage mixed RL, with stabilizers for GRPO at scale (unbiased KL, masking of highly off-policy sequences, Keep Routing). V4 (2026) mirrors V3.2 with one critical swap: the mixed RL was entirely replaced by on-policy distillation, with more than 10 teachers and reverse KL computed over the logits of the whole vocabulary (the version with a per-token advantage had high variance). Each of the Non-think, Think High and Think Max modes the server offers was taught by a distinct RL specialist (a different length penalty, a different context window; the report evaluates the modes at 8K, 128K and 384K), and a single student learned all three through OPD. And V4's post-training used FP4 QAT on the experts: the rollouts already run in the format that goes to production.

MiMo (Xiaomi): MOPD and the $90K defect

MiMo-V2-Flash (Jan 2026) formalized MOPD (Multi-Teacher On-Policy Distillation) in three stages: general SFT; domain-specialized RL (from search to safety), each teacher with its own algorithm; and MOPD, in which the student, reinitialized from the SFT model, generates responses, each prompt is routed to the teacher of its domain, and the advantage of each token is \(\hat A_t = \mathrm{sg}[\log\pi_{\text{teacher}}(y_t) - \log\pi_\theta(y_t)]\), added by default to \(\alpha\,\hat A_{\text{ORM}}\). V2.6 (Sep 2026) is a new cycle: after SFT, a single mixed RL run (“You Only RL Once”, $0.9 million on Flash and $2.6 million on Pro) and only then the MOPD2 of the -MOPD checkpoint, with RL and SFT teachers and prefixes from trajectories and demonstrations, from which the student generates a new turn. The most instructive case is the one about repeated tool calls: redoing 20 RL steps with a lower threshold would have cost about $2.31 million (the MiMo-V2.6 report's MixRL estimate; since it exceeds the whole Flash RL, it is not a Flash-only cost); instead, a specialized RL teacher was trained for 12 steps (~7,000 examples), which brought the repetition down to zero in replay, and it was distilled with MOPD. Cost: about $90K, 4% of the alternative.

Go through the four tabs and notice the convergence: they all end in a stage of fusion by on-policy distillation, whether from earlier checkpoints (GLM), from specialists (DeepSeek-V4, MiMo) or from a larger model (Qwen). On the scoreboard, MOPD matches or beats the best teacher in math, code and tools, but falls below it on IFBench (−2.2), SWE-Bench Verified (−0.8), creative writing (−3.9) and BrowseComp (−6.3): fusion doesn't come for free in every domain.

Bridge to Module 1: the inference engine inside the loop

Every on-policy method has a step that Module 1 knows by heart: generating text. And generation dominates wall-clock time. The OpenRLHF paper says the inference phase often accounts for more than 90% of the total RLHF or RLVR time. In Llama 2, generating with the training FSDP itself was about 20× slower, even with a large batch and a KV cache. That is why RL frameworks use a dedicated inference engine for the rollouts (vLLM or SGLang, with PagedAttention and continuous batching from Part VI and CUDA graphs from chapter 29) and a training engine (DeepSpeed, FSDP, Megatron) for the gradients. The vLLM documentation lists TRL, verl, OpenRLHF, NeMo-RL and Unsloth among its users; slime uses SGLang + Megatron and describes itself as the RL framework behind the entire GLM-4.5 to GLM-5.3 series.

There are two arrangements. In the colocated one, the same GPUs alternate between generating and training, and the inference engine has to release weights and KV during training (vLLM's sleep mode). In the disaggregated one, separate pools generate and train, and the new weights cross the network at every step: 7B BF16 weights are 14 GB, which over one PCIe function of the GB10's ConnectX-7 (~15.7 GB/s) take ~0.9 s, or ~0.56 s with both functions (the full 200 Gb/s port, ~25 GB/s). So that neither side waits for the other, asynchronous RL generates batch \(i+1\) while training on batch \(i\), accepting one step of staleness: in Noukhovitch et al.'s work, the same result came out ~40% faster on an 8B chatbot and ~70% faster on a 1B model on GSM8K.

The long tail is the reason for almost all of modern RL engineering: partial rollouts, which pause long sequences and resume them in the next step (MiMo-V2.6 tolerates staleness of up to 4 steps), dynamic sampling that discards groups where every answer is right or every answer is wrong, and orchestrators with thousands of concurrent rollouts. And numerical mismatch is the reason for the rest. With MoE, things get worse: according to the R3 paper (Rollout Routing Replay), ~94% of tokens pick different experts in at least one layer between inference and training. The fix is to record the rollout's routing and replay it during training. And the PR that brought RL to GLM-5.3-Flash in the miles framework had to reproduce, in training, the inference's indexer top-k and MoE routing to bring the mean next-token log-prob difference down from 0.257 to 0.029: the same fragile points (indexer, routing, precision) that the Module 1 recipe deals with when serving this model on sm_121.

On the other side of the loop is serving: the inference recipe still applies, with four precautions that this module will go into:

  1. The template is part of the model. Serving with a different chat template, different end tokens or a different parser is serving a different model (The API).
  2. reasoning_effort is a post-training artifact. In DeepSeek-V4, each level was taught by an RL teacher with a different length penalty and a different window (interpretation: reasoning_effort selects the behavior inherited from each of them). A max_tokens smaller than the one used in training cuts off reasoning that RL taught to be long.
  3. Post-hoc quantization measures what survives. GLM's EXL3 4 bpw and Qwen's NVFP4 were applied after post-training. Measuring KLD against BF16, as the recipes do, is measuring how much of the post-trained behavior survived. If you run a fine-tune, the quantization calibration has to be redone on the new model.
  4. The drafter has to keep up with the policy. MTP, DFlash and DSpark were trained against the original target; after SFT or RL, acceptance tends to drop. In MiMo-V2.6's RL, a block-6 DFlash retrained on rollout logs had a mean accepted length 31.3% longer than that of the MTP-3 inherited from SFT.
Trained thinkingreasoning_effort: low

GLM-5.3-Flash seems to have been post-trained essentially with reasoning on (interpretation, based on the official API, which doesn't let you turn thinking off). In the recipe (issue #257), with thinking off, 11 of 12 responses with long numeric tables came out corrupted; with thinking on and reasoning_effort: low, 0 of 6. The reading (interpretation) is that turning thinking off takes the model into a region post-training barely covered. The recipe also mounts, with GLM53_EXPOSE_CACHE_RESET=1, only the /reset_prefix_cache, /reset_mm_cache and /reset_encoder_cache routes, without the /sleep and weight-update routes that VLLM_SERVER_DEV_MODE=1 would open: a serving recipe deliberately shuts the doors that an RL loop needs to open.

Truly on-policyVLLM_MOE_DET_FINALIZE=1

The Qwen recipe's determinism overlay (VLLM_QSA_DET_TOPK=1 sorts QSA's top-k indices; VLLM_MOE_DET_FINALIZE=1 with VLLM_FLASHINFER_MOE_FUSED_FINALIZE=0 uses the MoE's unfused finalize) brought the prompt logprob spread from 0.25 and 6.07 nats down to 0 and 0, and the distinct greedy outputs from 5 out of 5 to 1 out of 5, at a cost of ~4% in decode. That is exactly the class of fix that “truly on-policy” RL demands from the rollout engine. On the serving side, TensorFold (chapter 48) takes this to the extreme: drafted = serial and concurrent = solo, bit for bit.

Modes inherited from teachersreasoning_effort: max

In the DeepSeek recipe, the reasoning effort selects a behavior inherited from an RL teacher, not a temperature. And the MXFP4 experts the recipe serves are in the format in which post-training ran the rollouts (FP4 QAT), which makes this the family where the served behavior comes closest to the trained one (interpretation).

How to study this module

Module 2 has fourteen chapters in four parts, in dependency order. Part X lays the foundation: this map, training memory and LoRA and SFT, with on-policy distillation and MOPD. Part XI builds up RL: preferences and reward models, RL fundamentals (policy gradient, A3C, TRPO), PPO and the RLHF pipeline and GRPO and RLVR. Part XII takes RL out of the picture: DPO, IPO, KTO and SimPO, AWR and contrastive methods. Part XIII wraps up with Constitutional AI, self-play and debate, evaluation and the hands-on recipe that post-trains, quantizes and serves.

If you have a specific goal, you don't need to read everything in order. Pick the destination in the figure and follow the train: the lit-up route is the minimum path of prerequisites within the module, and the transfers point to the Module 1 chapters worth reviewing.

An honest warning about the lab: the recipes' frontier MoEs don't fit on two Sparks for post-training. GLM-5.3-Flash's BF16 weights alone add up to ~643 GB, and the first third-party RL support for its architecture (KDA, mHC, DSA) was only merged on September 25, 2026, in a PR to the miles framework. The module's hands-on recipe uses Qwen3.8-27B on the Sparks, from the same family as Qwen3.8-Flash-Next (model_type: qwen3_5), and a Qwen3-4B on the Mac with MLX, so that every stage (SFT, DPO, GRPO, merge, NVFP4, vLLM) is the same as in the recipes, just at a scale that fits.

Four questions to bring to every chapter

Faced with any new method, ask: (1) Where does the data come from? From the dataset, from a teacher or from the model itself? (2) Who judges? A human, an RM, a verifier, an AI, a teacher? (3) What keeps the model close to where it was? A KL term, clipping, the mere fact of being on-policy, nothing at all? (4) How much does it cost? How many models in memory, and how much time spent generating? With these four answers, any 2026 post-training paper fits on this chapter's map.

Next stop: Training memory & LoRA. Before training anything, you need to know whether it fits, and you'll find out why the same two Sparks that serve a 320-billion-parameter MoE can barely manage a full fine-tune of an 8-billion-parameter model.
