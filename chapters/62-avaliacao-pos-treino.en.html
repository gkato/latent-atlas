Your DPO beat the base model on 58 of 100 questions, according to an LLM judge. Is it better? Maybe. With 100 prompts, a win rate near 50% has a margin of error of ±9.8 points; the judge may have preferred the longer answer, the first one, or the one that sounded like itself; and the model may have forgotten how to obey “answer in three bullet points,” or become more willing to explain what it shouldn't. In chapter 25 we wanted KL close to zero. Here the question flips: we want the model to change, but only where we asked. This chapter builds the toolkit to answer honestly and ends with a preregistered battery, with decision gates, ready for the lab in chapter 63.

Four axes: change only where we asked

Every Module 2 method is an intervention that moves probability mass (chapter 50). SFT pulls the model toward the demonstrations, DPO pushes the preferred response up and the rejected one down, GRPO reinforces whatever the verifier approved. None of them knows where to stop. Mass that rises in one place falls somewhere else, and evaluation exists to find out where it fell. That's why a single number is never enough. Evaluate on four axes, always against the starting model:

AxisQuestionMain instrumentDesired direction
TargetDid the trained behavior improve?paired win rate (LLM or human judge), verifiable domain metrics, validation loss↑
RegressionDid I lose general capability?fixed battery (IFEval, GSM8K/AIME, code, tools, knowledge)≈ base (Δ within the CI)
SafetyDoes it refuse too much? Does it comply with what it shouldn't?XSTest/OR-Bench (over-refusal); StrongREJECT/HarmBench/JailbreakBench (undue compliance)as the policy dictates
DriftHow much did the distribution change, and where?KL(πref‖πft) per position, teacher-forced on fixed generic and domain text (the ch. 25 ruler); length; MTP acceptance↑ in the domain, ≈ 0 outside it

The target measures what you trained (does the technical tutor answer better than the base?). Regression measures the side effect, the alignment tax: the model learned the style of the data and unlearned something else. Safety has two sides: refusing harmless requests or complying with harmful ones. Drift is the numeric axis, in nats per token, with the chapter 25 ruler: KLD(base‖candidate) with teacher forcing on fixed text (section 9 explains why it isn't the kl the trainer logs). We want large drift in the domain, which is the intended effect, and almost none on generic text.

Four principles apply to every axis:

  1. Compare pairs of models under the same conditions. Same chat template, same enable_thinking, same sampling parameters, same max_tokens, same server. A template difference is the No. 1 cause of fake “regressions.”
  2. Preregister before running (the frozen protocol from chapter 25): evaluation set, metrics, acceptance thresholds and number of samples. Without it you end up picking the checkpoint “that did best on the last benchmark I looked at,” the trap in section 4.
  3. Confidence intervals on everything. With 400 pairs, a 50% win rate has a 95% CI of ≈ ±4.9 points. Differences smaller than that are noise.
  4. Measure the baseline noise. Run the base itself twice (different seeds or batches) and compute the metric between the two runs: it's the stock-vs-stock null of the KLD in the GLM recipe (“near-tie tokens flip between identical runs”). An effect smaller than that noise doesn't exist.

Step through the five candidates. The verbose DPO has the highest raw win rate of all and fails the target gate: almost all of its advantage came from length. The SFT that forgot is great in the domain and loses 7 points of IFEval. The one with eroded safety was trained only on benign data and still started complying with harmful requests. The RL with a gamed reward barely scrapes through regression and only really shows up on the target and drift axes. In every case, a single number would tell the wrong story.

Analogy: the post-surgery checkup

You had knee surgery. Checking whether it bends is the target axis. But the doctor also takes your blood pressure (regression), tests your reflexes (safety) and checks whether the anesthesia messed with anything it shouldn't have (drift), always against your exams from before, done at the same lab. An exam from a different machine produces “changes” that are just equipment differences: that's the different chat template.

Win rate: the duel scoreboard and its margin of error

The most direct measure of the target axis is paired judgment. For each prompt \(x\) in the evaluation set, generate one response from candidate \(A\) and one from baseline \(B\). A judge, human or LLM, returns \(A \succ B\), \(B \succ A\) or a tie. The win rate is the fraction of duels won, with a tie worth half a point:

\[ \mathrm{WR}(A\,|\,B) = \frac{W + \tfrac12 T}{W + L + T} \]
  • \(W\): prompts where the judge preferred \(A\)
  • \(L\): prompts where it preferred \(B\)
  • \(T\): ties (half a point to each side, the AlpacaEval and Arena-Hard convention)
  • also report \(W/L/T\) separately: 50% with 0 ties and 50% with 80% ties are different stories

Why paired? Because the same prompt goes to both models. The prompt's difficulty, which is the largest source of variation in an evaluation, affects both sides equally and cancels out in the duel. It's the same logic as the A/B/A in chapter 45: compare what changed while holding everything else fixed.

How many prompts?

Each prompt is a loaded coin with probability \(p\) of landing on “A wins.” By the normal approximation, the standard error of the win rate and the number of prompts for a half-width \(E\) are:

\[ \mathrm{SE}(\widehat{\mathrm{WR}}) \approx \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}, \qquad \mathrm{CI}_{95\%} \approx \widehat{\mathrm{WR}} \pm 1.96\,\mathrm{SE}, \qquad n \approx \frac{z^2\,p(1-p)}{E^2} \]
  • \(p\): true win rate (the worst case is \(p = 0.5\))
  • \(n\): number of prompts (judged pairs)
  • \(z = 1.96\): normal quantile for 95%
  • \(E\): desired half-width of the interval

At \(p = 0.5\): with \(n = 100\), the 95% CI is ±9.8 points; with 400, ±4.9; with 1,000, ±3.1. For \(E = 5\) points, \(n \approx 1.96^2 \times 0.25 / 0.05^2 \approx 384\). To separate 55% from 50% comfortably, you need about 400 to 800 prompts. Ties reduce the variance a little, so these numbers are conservative.

Bootstrap: the interval without a formula

For the length-controlled win rate, the difference between two candidates or Bradley–Terry scores, there is no comfortable closed-form formula. The bootstrap handles all of them with the same trick: treat your sample of prompts as the population and resample from it.

  1. Per-prompt results. You have \(n\) values \(y_i \in \{0, \tfrac12, 1\}\).
  2. Resample. Draw \(n\) indices with replacement. Some prompts appear two or three times, others not at all (on average, a fraction \(\approx 1/e \approx 37\%\) is left out).
  3. Recompute the statistic (WR, WR-LC, difference…) on this fake sample.
  4. Repeat \(B = 2{,}000\) times and take the 2.5% and 97.5% percentiles of the replicates: \(\mathrm{CI}_{95\%} = [\,Q_{0.025},\ Q_{0.975}\,]\).
  5. To compare two candidates evaluated on the same prompts, resample pairs: the same drawn index applies to both.

Play with \(n = 100\) and a true WR of 56%. Click “new experiment” several times: the observed WR jumps between ~47% and ~65%, and the interval crosses 50% most of the time. Now go up to 800 prompts. The histogram narrows and the interval pulls away from 50%. The effect was the same. The sample was just too small to see it.

When there are no ties, the Wilson interval is a better analytic alternative than the normal one for small \(n\) or for \(p\) near 0 or 1:

\[ \mathrm{CI}_{\text{Wilson}} = \frac{\hat p + \frac{z^2}{2n} \pm z\sqrt{\frac{\hat p(1-\hat p)}{n} + \frac{z^2}{4n^2}}}{1 + \frac{z^2}{n}} \]
  • \(\hat p\): observed fraction of wins
  • \(z = 1.96\); the \(z^2\) terms pull the center toward 0.5 and avoid impossible intervals (below 0 or above 1)
Going deeper: why resample prompts and not verdicts?

The unit of chance is the prompt. The two judge calls for the same prompt (section 3) aren't independent, and neither are several responses to the same prompt with different seeds. Resample prompts, carrying along everything that belongs to each one (a cluster bootstrap). Resampling loose verdicts pretends there is more information than there is and artificially shrinks the interval.

In the lab

The recommended domain test has 300 to 500 questions about terms that never appeared in training, which gives a CI of ±4 to ±6 points on the win rate. Split train and test by term, not by row. If the same concept shows up on both sides, the test measures memorization.

The judge is a model too: LLM-as-judge and its biases

Judging 800 pairs by hand is expensive. The alternative is LLM-as-judge: a strong model reads the question and both answers and picks one. The founding work (Zheng and colleagues, NeurIPS 2023) showed that strong judges such as GPT-4 reach over 80% agreement with human preferences, the same level of agreement humans have with each other. It also cataloged the biases that come along: the judge is a language model conditioned on a text, and everything in that text, including the order and the length of the answers, factors in.

BiasEvidenceMitigation
PositionWang and colleagues (2023): with ChatGPT as the judge, just by reordering the answers, Vicuna-13B “beat” ChatGPT on 66 of 80 questionsjudge in both orders; ask for evidence before the score; send ambiguous cases to humans

Why does order matter? One interpretation: the verdict is a token conditioned on a text in which one answer comes before the other, and small reading asymmetries turn into a systematic push to one side, which doesn't depend on quality and therefore cancels out across both orders.

The practical rule is simple. Make two calls per pair, \((A, B)\) and \((B, A)\). If the judge picks the same model in both, the verdict stands. If it picks the same position in both, that is, if it changes its mind when the answers swap places, count a tie. That's what the lab's judging script does, with an explicit rubric and a request for a short justification before the verdict:

RUBRIC = ("Compare answers A and B to the question. Criteria, in order: (1) technical correctness, "
          "(2) answers exactly what was asked, (3) correct numbers/units, (4) concision. "
          "Ignore length and formatting. Explain in at most 3 sentences and end with 'VERDICT: A', "
          "'VERDICT: B' or 'VERDICT: TIE'.")

def compare(q, a, b):            # +1 a wins, -1 b wins, 0 tie/inconsistent
    v1, v2 = verdict(q, a, b), verdict(q, b, a)
    if v1 == "A" and v2 == "B": return 1
    if v1 == "B" and v2 == "A": return -1
    return 0

A panel of judges from different families

A large judge is expensive and carries the biases of its family. The PoLL panel of judges (Verga and colleagues, 2024) replaces the single judge with several smaller models from disjoint families, with a majority vote. Across 3 settings and 6 datasets, the panel showed less intra-model bias and was more than seven times cheaper than one large judge. The biases of different families don't all point the same way and, in the vote, they tend to dilute.

Module 1 already hands you such a panel for free: the three recipes serve, on the same two DGX Sparks (OpenAI API on port 8888), three distinct families: GLM-5.3-Flash-EXL3 , qwen3.8-flash-next and deepseek-v4-flash-vision-exp . The exclusion rule: drop from the panel the family of the teacher that generated the training data, because the student learned to sound like it, and that of the student.

The lab's impartial judgeJUDGE = "deepseek-v4-flash-vision-exp"

In the chapter 63 lab, the teacher is GLM and the student is Qwen3.8-27B, a relative of Qwen3.8-Flash-Next. Of the three recipes, the only impartial family is DeepSeek. If you want a panel, use GLM and Qwen-Flash-Next as secondary judges and report their agreement with DeepSeek separately. If the judge always “prefers” the fine-tuned model, suspect self-preference or position bias first.

Start with equal quality (true WR 50%) and position bias 1.0. With a single order, the candidate always appears first and “wins” ~73% of the duels: a pure artifact. With two orders, the push shows up in both directions and becomes a tie, and the score returns to 50%. The math is exact: with \(+b\) favoring the first position, the candidate wins with \(\sigma(\Delta q + b)\) when it comes first and \(\sigma(\Delta q - b)\) when it comes second, and the expected value across both orders, \(\tfrac12[\sigma(\Delta q+b) + \sigma(\Delta q-b)]\), is exactly 0.5 when \(\Delta q = 0\). Now make the candidate 100% longer. Swapping positions does not fix it, because the verbosity bias pushes toward the same model in both orders. Then turn on the judge from the teacher's family. The panel dilutes self-preference, because only one of the three judges is biased (in the example, from ~67% to ~62%, against a real 50%), but doesn't eliminate it: that's why the lab's rule is to exclude the teacher's family, not dilute it. When the judge becomes a reward source (RLAIF, ch. 60; Self-Rewarding, ch. 61), these biases stop merely distorting the measurement and start being optimized.

Calibrate the judge against humans

Before trusting the judge on a new task, also send ~10% of the pairs to a human and compute Cohen's κ, the chance-corrected agreement:

\[ \kappa = \frac{p_o - p_e}{1 - p_e} \]
  • \(p_o\): fraction of pairs on which judge and human agree
  • \(p_e\): agreement expected by chance, given each label's frequencies for the two raters
  • \(\kappa = 1\): perfect agreement; \(\kappa = 0\): nothing beyond chance

If \(\kappa\) falls below ~0.4, the judge isn't fit for that task. That threshold is a common “moderate agreement” convention, a recommendation rather than a sourced number. For math and code, prefer a judge with a reference answer in the prompt or, better yet, rule-based verification (section 6).

From duel to ranking: Bradley–Terry, Elo and the leaderboard illusion

The win rate compares two models. In a real project you have several checkpoints: the base, the SFT, two DPOs with different \(\beta\), the quantized version. Judge pairs across them and fit the Bradley–Terry model, the same one that trains the reward model in chapter 53. There it gave a score to each response. Here it gives a score to each model:

\[ P(i \succ j) = \sigma(s_i - s_j), \qquad \ell(s) = \sum_{\text{duels}} \Big[\, y\log\sigma(s_a-s_b) + (1-y)\log\sigma(s_b-s_a) \Big], \qquad R_i = C + \frac{400}{\ln 10}\,s_i \]
  • \(s_i\): latent strength of model \(i\); only differences matter, so fix \(s_{\text{base}} = 0\); the letter is \(s\), not \(\beta\), because \(\beta\) is already the strength of DPO's KL anchor (section 9)
  • \(\sigma\): logistic sigmoid
  • \(y\): 1 if \(a\) won the duel, 0 if it lost, ½ for a tie (or use the Davidson model for ties)
  • \(\ell\): log-likelihood, maximized over all duels at once
  • \(R_i\): Elo-like scale; \(400/\ln 10 \approx 173.7\), so a 400-point difference = 10-to-1 odds; the lab script uses \(C = 1000\) for the base

Classic chess Elo does something else: it updates the scores online, duel by duel, with \(R_a \leftarrow R_a + K(S - E_a)\) and \(E_a = 1/(1 + 10^{(R_b-R_a)/400})\). The result depends on the order of the duels and on the \(K\) factor, and recent duels weigh more. That makes sense for players who change over time, not for frozen checkpoints. That's why Chatbot Arena (now LMArena) uses maximum-likelihood Bradley–Terry over all battles and only reports the result on an Elo-like scale.

Let the figure shuffle a few times, or click the button yourself, and raise \(K\). The × marks spread out: the online Elo of the same checkpoint, with the same battles, moves by dozens of points depending on the order. The BT diamond doesn't budge, because the likelihood is a sum over duels and a sum doesn't depend on order.

How the public arena evolved

The 2024 Chatbot Arena paper reported more than 240,000 human votes in anonymous battles. Three later changes matter for the lab:

Style control is a logistic regression with covariates: \(\operatorname{logit} P(i \succ j) = s_i - s_j + \gamma^\top(f_i - f_j)\), where \(f\) gathers normalized length and the number of headers, lists and bold spans. The ranking publishes the \(s\) with \(\gamma\) fitted, that is, it answers “who would win if both wrote in the same style?” Keep this idea in mind, because it comes back in section 5.

The leaderboard illusion

“The Leaderboard Illusion” (Singh and colleagues, 2025) documented that some providers privately test many variants before a launch (≈ 27 Meta variants before Llama-4) and publish only the best one, as well as a data asymmetry (Google ≈ 19.2% and OpenAI ≈ 20.4% of the Arena data, versus ≈ 29.7% for 83 open-weight models combined).

The statistical mechanism behind the first criticism is the winner's curse. Every measurement has noise. If you measure 27 equally good variants and publish the top-scoring one, you publish the luckiest one. The expected maximum of \(N\) independent normals grows with \(N\), and that “gain” is pure selected noise.

With 27 variants and 1,000 votes each, the published champion lands, on average, ~22 points above the truth without having improved at all (\(\sigma \approx 11\) points times \(\mathbb{E}[\max] \approx 2.0\) for 27 normals). The lesson applies to your lab: testing 20 checkpoints against the same 200 prompts and reporting the best one is exactly this mechanism. Keep a selection set, where you choose hyperparameters and checkpoints, separate from a reporting set, which you look at only once, at the end.

Length and style: AlpacaEval-LC, Arena-Hard and MT-Bench

Long, well-formatted answers look more complete, and judges, human and LLM alike, fall for it. The bias exists on the training side too: Singhal and colleagues (2023) decomposed PPO's reward gain and found that, in two of the scenarios studied, 70 to 90% of the improvement came from length alone (chapter 55). DPO also exploits verbosity, which is why LN-DPO and SimPO exist (chapter 58). In evaluation, the antidote is statistical: estimate how much of the score comes from length and discount it.

AlpacaEval 2 and the length-controlled win rate

AlpacaEval 2 has 805 instructions and the default baseline gpt4_turbo; it costs less than US$10 and runs in under 3 minutes. The length-controlled version, WR-LC, fits a generalized linear (logistic) model that predicts the annotator's preference from the model's identity, the length difference and the instruction's difficulty. The WR-LC is that model's prediction with the length difference set to zero, averaged over the instructions. This raised the Spearman correlation with Chatbot Arena from 0.94 to 0.98. The lab's simplified version, with your judge and your domain set:

\[ \operatorname{logit} P(y = 1 \mid x) = \theta + \varphi\,\tanh\!\Big(\frac{\Delta\ell(x)}{\sigma_{\Delta\ell}}\Big), \qquad \Delta\ell = \ell_{\text{cand}} - \ell_{\text{base}}, \qquad \mathrm{WR}_{\text{LC}} = 100\cdot\sigma(\theta) \]
  • \(y\): the pair's verdict (1 = candidate wins; a tie enters as a soft label of 0.5)
  • \(\Delta\ell\): length difference in tokens between the two answers
  • \(\sigma_{\Delta\ell}\): standard deviation of \(\Delta\ell\) over the set; the \(\tanh\) caps the effect of gigantic answers
  • \(\varphi\): how much the judge rewards length (0 = not at all)
  • \(\theta\): the candidate's advantage at equal length; \(\sigma(\theta)\) is the counterfactual \(\Delta\ell = 0\)
  • the AlpacaEval-LC paper adds a per-instruction difficulty term, \((\psi_m - \psi_b)\gamma_x\)

Leave the real quality at 50%, the candidate writing 200 more tokens and the verbosity bias at 1.2. The raw WR goes above 60%, but the WR-LC stays near 50%: the candidate isn't better, it's longer. Set the bias to zero and the two numbers coincide. The regression separates the two things because it uses the variation of \(\Delta\ell\) across pairs: the pairs where the candidate wrote little show how the judge decides without the length push. But it assumes that length, by itself, isn't quality. If in your domain longer answers really are more complete, WR-LC discounts real merit. And if almost every pair has the same \(\Delta\ell\), \(\theta\) and \(\varphi\) become confounded (the bootstrap CI blows up). Read the two numbers together and reread samples.

How the curve is fitted

With \(p_i = \sigma(\theta + \varphi x_i)\) and \(x_i = \tanh(\Delta\ell_i/\sigma_{\Delta\ell})\), the fit maximizes the log-likelihood \(\ell(\theta,\varphi) = \sum_i \big[y_i \log p_i + (1-y_i)\log(1-p_i)\big]\), which is concave: it has a single maximum. The gradient is \(g = \sum_i (y_i - p_i)\,(1, x_i)\) and the Hessian is \(H = -\sum_i p_i(1-p_i)\,(1, x_i)(1, x_i)^\top\). The Newton step, \(w \leftarrow w - H^{-1} g\) with \(w = (\theta, \varphi)\), approximates \(\ell\) by a parabola and jumps to its top. It's the IRLS (iteratively reweighted least squares) of GLMs and converges in ~5 iterations; the figure animates one at a time. The tie's soft label \(y = 0.5\) works because cross-entropy is linear in \(y\): a tie is worth half a win plus half a loss. It's the same likelihood as the Bradley–Terry in chapter 53, with one extra covariate.

The authors themselves warn that the length bias shrinks but doesn't vanish and that high-stakes decisions require humans. Since the official AlpacaEval depends on a paid annotator (GPT-4 Turbo), the lab reuses the idea with the local judge:

# wr_lc.py — WR with a bootstrap CI and length-controlled WR
import json, sys, numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize
rows = [json.loads(l) for l in open(sys.argv[1])]   # judge JSONL: {"y": 1|0|0.5, "len_c": …, "len_b": …}
y = np.array([r["y"] for r in rows]); d = np.array([r["len_c"] - r["len_b"] for r in rows], float)
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
boot = [y[rng.integers(0, len(y), len(y))].mean() for _ in range(2000)]
print(f"WR = {100*y.mean():.1f}%  95% CI [{100*np.percentile(boot,2.5):.1f}, {100*np.percentile(boot,97.5):.1f}]")
x = np.tanh(d / (d.std() + 1e-9))
def nll(w):                                     # fractional y (0, 0.5, 1) = cross-entropy with a soft label
    z = w[0] + w[1] * x; p = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-z))
    return -np.sum(y * np.log(p + 1e-12) + (1 - y) * np.log(1 - p + 1e-12))
w = minimize(nll, np.zeros(2)).x
print(f"WR-LC = {100/(1+np.exp(-w[0])):.1f}%  (length coef. φ = {w[1]:.3f})")

Arena-Hard-Auto and MT-Bench

Arena-Hard-Auto v0.1 gathered 500 hard prompts distilled from Chatbot Arena (the BenchBuilder pipeline), with 3× more separation between models than MT-Bench, 98.6% correlation with human rankings and a cost of US$20. v2.0 has 750 prompts (500 new real queries + 250 creative-writing ones), Gemini-2.5 as the official judge (or GPT-4.1, or an ensemble) and style control in show_result.py --control-features; the pipeline accepts OpenAI-API-compatible servers such as vLLM. With a local judge, it gives you an “out-of-domain” number to catch conversational-quality regression, which isn't comparable to the leaderboard, because the judge is different.

MT-Bench has 80 two-turn questions in 8 categories, scored 1 to 10 by a strong judge. It's saturated and judge-sensitive, but it still catches multi-turn regression: the SFT that broke the second turn because all of its data had a single turn.

Don't break what worked: regression and verifiable benchmarks

The alignment tax has a technical name, forgetting, and it comes in doses. “LoRA Learns Less and Forgets Less” (2024) showed that, on code and math, LoRA lags well behind full fine-tuning but better preserves out-of-domain performance (full fine-tuning learns perturbations of 10 to 100× higher rank). “LoRA Without Regret” (Thinking Machines, September 2025) refined the picture: LoRA matches full fine-tuning when applied to all matrices and with an LR ≈ 10× higher (chapter 51). Moral: how much you forget depends on how you trained, and only the fixed battery shows it.

Instructions: IFEval and IFBench

IFEval has 25 types of code-verifiable instructions (word counts, JSON, no commas…) in ~500 prompts (541 rows in google/IFEval). Metrics: prompt_level_strict_acc, inst_level_strict_acc and the loose versions; lm-eval's ifeval task generates up to 1,280 tokens, 0-shot. It's the cheap detector of the most common regression: domain SFT teaches the style of the data and the model stops obeying “answer in 3 bullet points.” Its successor, IFBench, already shows up in 2026 model cards (nvidia/Qwen3.8-27B-NVFP4: 80.07 in BF16, 78.93 in NVFP4).

Code and agents

In code, HumanEval (164 Python functions with tests) and HumanEval+ are saturated and serve only for regression, with lm-eval's humaneval_instruct and humaneval_plus tasks (which require --confirm_run_unsafe_code). What still separates models (LiveCodeBench with a date window, SWE-bench Pro and Terminal-Bench) is in the table in section 7. For tools, BFCL V4 (updated on April 12, 2026) covers non-live calls, enterprise/OSS functions, multi-turn and agentic web search; the overall score is the unweighted average of the subcategories. τ²-bench (v1.0.1, July 2026) simulates customer service with a user simulator and tools on both sides, in the airline, retail, telecom and banking_knowledge domains. If your SFT touched tool calling, run a BFCL subset and 20 to 50 retail tasks with --num-trials 4, always with the same parser vLLM will use in production (--tool-call-parser qwen3_xml in the RedHatAI cards for Qwen3.8-27B; chapter 34 explains why the parser is part of the model).

Agents call for a different metric. pass@k asks “does it work at least once in k tries?” pass^k asks “does it work every time, across all k tries?”, which is the right question for an agent that will serve customers:

\[ \text{pass@}k = \mathbb{E}_{\text{problems}}\!\left[1 - \frac{\binom{n-c}{k}}{\binom{n}{k}}\right], \qquad \text{pass}^k = \mathbb{E}_{\text{tasks}}\!\left[\frac{\binom{c}{k}}{\binom{n}{k}}\right] \]
  • \(n\): samples generated per problem; \(c\): how many are correct
  • pass@k (Chen and colleagues, 2021): unbiased estimator of “at least one of k is correct”
  • pass^k (Yao and colleagues, 2024, τ-bench): “all k are correct”; \(\text{pass}^1 = \text{pass@}1\), and pass^k falls with k

A task the agent gets right 80% of the time, with independent attempts, has pass@4 = \(1 - 0.2^4 \approx 0.998\) and pass^4 = \(0.8^4 \approx 0.41\). The same agent “solves almost everything” and “fails more than half of the customers who come back four times.”

The fixed battery and the paired test

The regression battery proposed for the lab: full IFEval, full GSM8K (0-shot CoT), AIME 2025 or 2026 (k = 4 samples), HumanEval-instruct, MMLU-Pro (stratified sample of ~1,000), a BFCL subset, XSTest and KL on generic text. Run it on the base and on each candidate and report Δ with a CI. For per-item binary metrics, use McNemar's test, which looks only at the items that changed:

\[ \chi^2 = \frac{\big(|b - c| - 1\big)^2}{b + c} \quad (1 \text{ degree of freedom}) \]
  • \(b\): items right on the base and wrong on the candidate (what you lost)
  • \(c\): items wrong on the base and right on the candidate (what you gained)
  • the \(-1\) is the continuity correction; for small \(b + c\), use the exact binomial test
  • items that both get right or both get wrong carry no information about the difference

With \(b = 20\) and \(c = 5\), accuracy drops from 78% to 75%. Treating the two evaluations as independent samples, the CI of the difference runs from about −8 to +2 points and crosses zero: “inconclusive.” Paired, \(\chi^2 = (15-1)^2/25 = 7.84\) and \(p \approx 0.005\): a real regression. Pairing wins because most of the variance across items is difficulty, and difficulty is the same for both models. It cancels out, as in the paired win rate.

Benchmarks age: saturation and contamination

Every benchmark is born hard, becomes popular, saturates and sometimes rots. Saturating is natural: when every competent model scores 95%, the test stops separating models and is only good for catching regression. Rotting is worse: it's contamination, when test items leak into training and the score starts measuring memory. It comes in three ways:

  1. Pretraining. The benchmark is on the web and made it into the base model's trillions of tokens.
  2. You. When you generate synthetic data with a teacher that saw the benchmark, or when you scrape the web. If you ask the teacher to “generate math problems similar to these,” it may hand back near-copies of GSM8K that it memorized.
  3. Selection. Choosing a checkpoint or hyperparameter by looking at the test set. No item leaked, but the decision did (the winner's curse from section 4).

The evidence is concrete. GSM1k (Zhang and colleagues, 2024) wrote new problems in the GSM8K style and saw drops of up to 8%, whole families with signs of overfitting and a relationship (Spearman \(r^2 = 0.36\)) between a model's probability of generating GSM8K examples and the size of its drop. MathArena evaluates competitions on the day they come out and found “strong signs of contamination in AIME 2024”. For 2026, use AIME 2026 and HMMT February 2026, not AIME 2024.

This matters for claims of absolute capability. For regression (Δ base × candidate), pretraining contamination weighs on both sides and cancels out in the pairing; that's why the battery still accepts AIME 2025. What invalidates the Δ is your SFT containing evaluation items, hence the decontamination below.

How to detect it

  • N-gram overlap between training and evaluation, on normalized text. Checking 8- to 13-grams is common practice; the exact threshold varies across technical reports, and 13 follows, as far as we know, the practice popularized by the GPT-3 report (not verified in the sources).
  • Min-K% Prob (Shi and colleagues): texts seen in training have fewer “surprise words.” The method gained 7.4% over previous ones on WikiMIA.
  • Dated sets (LiveCodeBench, MathArena, the year's competitions) and fresh parallel sets (GSM1k).
  • Canary strings embedded in evaluation datasets: if the model completes the string, it has seen the dataset.
\[ \text{Min-}K\%(x) = \frac{1}{|S_K|}\sum_{t \in S_K} \log p_\theta(x_t \mid x_{<t}) \]
  • \(x\): the suspect text, read with teacher forcing
  • \(\log p_\theta(x_t \mid x_{<t})\): the log-probability the model gives each real token
  • \(S_K\): the \(K\%\) tokens with the lowest log-prob (the biggest surprises)
  • high score (barely negative) = not even the worst surprises are surprising = the text was likely seen

The n-gram detector catches the near-copy with any \(N\) and the light paraphrase only with \(N \le 10\). Lowering \(N\) catches more, but also produces false positives on common phrases (“how much did she spend in total”). The heavy paraphrase gets past all of them: it's the same question in different words, and no lexical method can see it. That's why dated sets are the strongest defense: a problem written after the model's cutoff can't have leaked into pretraining.

In the lab, run decontamination of your SFT and DPO set against every evaluation set (domain, IFEval, GSM8K, AIME, HumanEval…) before training. The support script removes from training every item that shares a normalized 13-gram with any evaluation set; for short, one-line questions, complement it with approximate similarity (MinHash) and with a same-glossary-term check between train and test.

Safety in both directions

A model can fail at safety in two opposite ways. It can refuse too much (“how do I kill a process in Linux?” → “I can't help with violence”) or it can comply with what it shouldn't. Post-training can push it to either side, and evaluation has to measure both. The benchmarks are the ones chapter 60 used to measure CAI:

BenchmarkContentMeasures
XSTest250 safe prompts (10 types) that a calibrated model shouldn't refuse + 200 unsafe contrast promptsover-refusal (“exaggerated safety”)
OR-Bencha large set of “seemingly toxic” promptsover-refusal at scale
HarmBenchstandardized framework: 18 red-teaming methods × 33 LLMs and defensesattack success rate (ASR), with a classifier
StrongREJECTprompts that require specific harmful information + an evaluator that measures the response's usefulness“useful” undue compliance
JailbreakBenchstandardized behaviors and attack artifactsjailbreak robustness

Prefer the StrongREJECT score to a binary ASR: jailbreaks that break through the refusal tend to degrade capability, and counting “didn't refuse” as a success overestimates the real danger.

Three findings anyone doing post-training should know

Fine-tuning erodes alignment. Qi and colleagues (2023) broke GPT-3.5 Turbo's safeguards with 10 adversarial examples, for less than US$0.20, through the fine-tuning API. They also showed that even benign data degrades safety, “though to a lesser extent”. Consequence: every domain SFT needs the safety axis in its battery, even without any “dangerous” data.

DPO can misalign by accident. During DPO, the probability of the preferred response often falls along with that of the rejected one; what rises is the margin (chapter 57). When the pairs have similar embeddings (measured by the CHES score), the freed-up mass can migrate to responses with the opposite meaning. This is likelihood displacement. In the paper's example, training to refuse reduced Llama-3-8B-Instruct's refusal rate from 74.4% to 33.4%. The diagnosis shows up in the training metrics: logps/chosen falling while rewards/margins rises (section 9).

Refusal is (almost) a direction. Arditi and colleagues showed, across 13 open chat models of up to 72B, that refusal is mediated by a one-dimensional subspace: erasing the direction turns refusal off, and adding it triggers refusals on harmless prompts. It's the basis of abliteration in chapter 19. In evaluation, it becomes the refusal probe: estimate the refusal direction \(\hat r\) on the base, project the residual activations (middle-to-late layers) of harmful and harmless prompts onto it, and compare before and after fine-tuning. A large drop in separation anticipates, cheaply and deterministically, the safety regression that StrongREJECT or HarmBench would show (an inference from the verified facts, not a published result).

Benign SFT moves only a little, but upward. The 10 adversarial examples and abliteration end up in the “loose” corner, and with abliteration the two probe curves collapse to zero, because the direction was removed from the residual stream. Overdone safety post-training runs off to the right: it refuses too much, and the harmless curve slides toward the harmful one. Both mistakes become evaluation failures if you measure only one axis.

Responsible use

Abliteration doesn't replace safety evaluation: the GLM recipe itself records “KLD not measured with ablit.” In an abliterated or post-trained model, the four axes remain mandatory, and removing refusals without governance is a policy decision, not an engineering one: document who may use the model and for what. Abliterating after post-training requires re-estimating \(\hat r\) on the new model, because the direction changes with the fine-tune.

Drift and reward hacking: KL as a budget

RLHF and DPO optimize reward while tied to an anchor (chapter 55):

\[ \max_\pi\ \mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi}\big[r(x,y)\big] - \beta\,\mathrm{KL}\big(\pi \,\|\, \pi_{\text{ref}}\big), \qquad \pi^*(y\mid x) \propto \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,e^{\,r(x,y)/\beta} \]
  • \(r\): reward (reward model, verifier or implicit reward)
  • \(\beta\): strength of the KL anchor; high \(\beta\) = small change
  • \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\): reference model, usually the SFT
  • \(\pi^*\): optimal policy: \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) exponentially reweighted by the reward; that's where DPO gets its implicit reward \(\hat r = \beta\log(\pi/\pi_{\text{ref}})\), TRL's rewards/* metrics

The realized KL is therefore the budget spent by post-training: how far the model moved away from the reference to buy reward. In practice it's estimated by sampling, on the tokens generated by the policy itself, with the per-token ratio \(\rho = \pi_{\text{ref}}(y_t)/\pi_\theta(y_t)\) (Schulman, “Approximating KL Divergence”, 2020):

\[ k_1 = -\log\rho, \qquad k_2 = \tfrac12(\log\rho)^2, \qquad k_3 = (\rho - 1) - \log\rho = \operatorname{expm1}(\Delta) - \Delta, \quad \Delta = \log\pi_{\text{ref}} - \log\pi_\theta \]
  • \(k_1\): unbiased, high variance, can be negative on a single sample
  • \(k_2\): biased, low variance
  • \(k_3\): unbiased and always \(\ge 0\); it's the one GRPO uses (DeepSeekMath)
  • example: \(\Delta = +0.5\) gives \(k_1 = -0.5\), \(k_2 = 0.125\), \(k_3 \approx 0.149\)

mlx-lm-lora implements exactly expm1(log_ref − log_policy) − (log_ref − log_policy), with the log-ratio capped at 20, and reports in kl_clip_ratio how often the cap was hit.

Two rulers of “how much it changed”

(a) \(\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta\|\pi_{\text{ref}})\) estimated with \(k_1\) or \(k_3\) on the tokens the policy itself generates. It's what the RLHF objective penalizes and what TRL's kl metric logs, and it depends on what the policy chose to write. (b) \(\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\text{ref}}\|\pi_{\text{ft}})\) computed over the whole distribution, at each position of a fixed text, with teacher forcing. This one is comparable across candidates and with chapter 25, and it's the one the gates use. Since \(\mathrm{KL}(P\|Q) \ne \mathrm{KL}(Q\|P)\) and the measured texts are different, the two numbers aren't interchangeable: always report the direction and the text you measured on.

Goodhart, measured: over-optimization

“When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure” (Goodhart's law). The reward model is a learned proxy, and optimizing it too hard exploits its errors: that's reward hacking. Gao, Schulman and Hilton (2022) measured this in a synthetic experiment. A large RM (6B) played the role of the “human” (the gold RM) and labeled pairs to train proxy RMs from 3M to 3B; the policies were optimized against the proxy and evaluated by the gold one. The true reward rises and then falls, and the curves follow simple shapes in the distance \(d = \sqrt{\mathrm{KL}(\pi\,\|\,\pi_{\text{init}})}\):

\[ R_{\text{bon}}(d) = d\,(\alpha_{\text{bon}} - \beta_{\text{bon}}\,d), \qquad R_{\text{RL}}(d) = d\,(\alpha_{\text{RL}} - \beta_{\text{RL}}\log d), \qquad \mathrm{KL}_{\text{bon}} = \log n - \frac{n-1}{n} \]
  • \(R\): gold reward, with \(R(0) = 0\)
  • \(\alpha, \beta\): fitted coefficients; they depend on the proxy RM's size and on the data (not to be confused with the anchor's \(\beta\))
  • bon = best-of-\(n\): generate \(n\) responses and keep the one with the highest proxy score; the KL comes from the analytic formula, an upper bound that is nearly exact for long responses without ties (chapter 59): n = 64 → ≤ 3.17 nats; n = 1,000 → ≤ 5.91; n = 60,000 → ≤ 10.00
  • peaks: best-of-n at \(d^* = \alpha/2\beta\); RL at \(d^* = e^{\alpha/\beta - 1}\)

Loosen the leash and the robot overshoots the peak: the proxy keeps rising and the gold falls, quickly in best-of-n and slowly in RL (the shape has a \(\log d\)). With the illustrative coefficients, the best-of-n peak sits at \(d = 2\) (KL = 4 nats) and the RL one at \(d = e^{1.5} \approx 4.5\) (KL ≈ 20 nats), with a lower maximum gold (≈ 0.90 versus 1.0): RL spent 5× more KL to reach a lower peak. Near \(d = 0\) the RL shape has an infinite slope, and the authors themselves warn that it probably doesn't hold there. Hence Gao and colleagues' two lessons for evaluation. First: since RL “consumes” much more KL than best-of-n for the same gain, KL is no good for comparing how much optimization different methods did. Second: in their setup, the KL penalty had an effect similar to early stopping; it didn't improve the gold × KL frontier.

Logistics matter too: the judges (the Module 1 recipes) and the candidates compete for the same two Sparks. The order that works: (1) serve the candidates and generate and record all responses (domain, XSTest, StrongREJECT, optional Arena-Hard v2); (2) run the verifiable tasks with lm-eval against the same servers; (3) tear everything down, bring up the judge recipe and judge the recorded responses. Same seed and same sampling for base and candidate; report mean ± CI across seeds (RedHatAI uses 3, and 8 for AIME). An adapter is only valid on the base it was trained on. After quantizing, repeat the battery on the quantized model: RedHatAI publishes a “recovery” between 98.27% and 100.88% for the NVFP4 of the Qwen3.8-27B base, the level to aim for.
