So far, every drafter has written like a typist: one key at a time. Qwen's MTP runs its layer three times in a row to propose three tokens. DFlash2 (GLM's drafter) and DSpark (DeepSeek's) write like a rubber stamp: they take a block of blank positions and fill all of them in the same forward. That comes at a price. Positions filled in parallel don't know what their neighbors picked, and the ones at the end come out worse than the ones at the start. In this chapter you'll see how each method pays that price and what the recipes changed in vLLM to serve them: a bidirectional mask, a KV of their own, adaptive k, per-request slots, and the rule that pins DeepSeek at k=6. And since the GLM recipe gained a second version on a different engine, TensorFold , you'll also see the same DFlash2 served another way: verified by equality, with stop rules tuned in an offline simulator and one context ring per conversation.

From typist to rubber stamp: why draft in blocks

Recall speculative decoding (chapter 35). A cheap drafter proposes \(k\) tokens. The target verifies all \(k+1\) in a single forward and accepts the longest matching prefix, and rejection sampling preserves the target's distribution. The gain comes from an asymmetry of decode: reading the weights costs almost the same for 1 position as for 8.

What changes between methods is the cost of drafting. MTP and EAGLE (chapter 36) are autoregressive: draft \(i\) needs token \(i-1\), so that's \(k\) forwards in a row. A block drafter takes the anchor token (the last verified one) followed by empty positions and returns the whole block at once. The cost barely grows with \(k\).

\[ \begin{aligned} \tau(k) &= 1 + \sum_{i=1}^{k} a_i, \qquad a_i = \prod_{j=1}^{i} c_j \\ \text{speedup} &\approx \frac{\tau(k)}{T_{\text{verif}}(k) + T_{\text{draft}}(k)} \end{aligned} \]
  • \(\tau\): tokens emitted per step, including the bonus the target always produces
  • \(a_i\): per-position acceptance, the fraction of steps in which draft \(i\) and all the earlier ones passed
  • \(c_j\): conditional acceptance of position \(j\), given that the earlier ones passed
  • \(T_{\text{verif}}, T_{\text{draft}}\): costs in units of “one decode step without speculation”. Sequential: \(T_{\text{draft}} \approx k\cdot t_d\). Block: \(T_{\text{draft}} \approx t_d\), nearly fixed

The figure is a race between three conveyor belts: no speculation, sequential drafting and block drafting. All three use the same acceptance profile measured in the recipes; the only thing that changes is how much time drafting takes. Every token character that lands in the tray is a token delivered to the user.

On the sequential belt, the lavender draft bar grows with \(k\) and eats the gain. On the block belt it barely moves. That's why DFlash2 proposes 7 tokens while GLM's MTP stopped at 2. Now switch to prose: raising \(k\) from 4 to 7 adds only \(0.16+0.09+0.06 = 0.31\) tokens per step, and those positions still cost verification. That math comes back in the section on adaptive k.

Analogy

The typist types “New”, looks, then types “York”. The stamp prints everything at once, but it was carved without knowing which city the customer wanted. If the mold blends two plausible answers, out comes “New Lake City”. Much of this chapter is about giving the stamp the typist's coherence.

Deep dive: why MoE makes things harder

In a dense model, 8 positions reuse the same weights once they're read. In MoE (chapter 11) each position picks its own experts. On GLM, verifying 8 tokens with top-8 creates 64 expert “slots” per layer, and the inventory estimates ≈57 distinct experts (out of 288), ≈15 GB read per rank per step. Speculating still pays off, but less than in a dense model.

Block diffusion: anchor, masks and an attention that can't be causal

The idea comes from block masked diffusion: the network receives a block full of gaps and fills all of them, like a fill-in-the-blanks exercise. A “real” diffusion LM does this in \(T\) steps: it fills the block, keeps only the positions it's most confident about, re-masks the others and repeats, refining bit by bit. DFlash stops at the first step. It doesn't need to deliver a perfect block, because the target verifies every token right afterwards and throws out whatever is wrong; spending \(T\) forwards on the draft would give back the cost the block saved. In DFlash, block_size = 8: one anchor and 7 mask tokens (on GLM, id 154856, outside the 154,856 real tokens, in the embeddings' padding range; the inventory's interpretation). Each mask predicts the token that will occupy it, which gives 7 drafts. In DSpark, dspark_block_size = 5, with “noise” (dspark_noise_token_id = 128799) after the anchor. The convention is different: the anchor position itself predicts the next token (“anchor + [0, γ)”, as in DeepSpec training), so 5 positions give 5 drafts.

The context is not re-read. The target has already computed hidden states for every verified position, and the drafter projects them into keys and values for its own layers. This is called KV injection. Queries come only from the block; keys and values come from a sliding context window (2048 on GLM, 128 on DSpark) and from the block itself.

Inside the block, the positions were trained with bidirectional attention: mask 3 sees masks 4 through 7, because none of them is an “already written” token. In the figure, each row is a query from the block and each column is a key. Toggle the masks and hover over a row.

The triangle that disappears in causal mode is the information training taught the drafter to use. The first mask sees almost the same thing in both modes (context and anchor) and survives. The late ones were trained to coordinate with their neighbors; without that view, their input falls outside the training distribution and they degenerate. (The position-by-position reading is our interpretation; the collapse is measured.) An independent card on SM120 tells the same story: a partially ported Triton mask scored 1.017 weighted acceptance, and only “full bidirectional visibility within the block” brought it to 5.068/5.739.

In the recipe

The DFlash2 checkpoint declares is_causal: false. The image's rule was “SWA layer ⇒ causal”, and patch_dflash2.py makes _dflash_layer_causal honor the field before that rule kicks in. The other half is not pinning the drafter's backend: on SM121 the automatic selector picks FLASH_ATTN, which does non-causal SWA. On DeepSeek, the drafter's Triton kernel (_dspark_sparse_scores_kernel + _dspark_sparse_out_kernel) makes two passes over 133 keys (128-token window + 5-token block). The window is masked up to min(pos+1, 128), the block is fully visible, and the softmax has a sink in the denominator (a learned per-head logit that lets attention “look at nothing”; chapter 6).

Inside DFlash2: a drafter that reads the target's mind

GLM's DFlash2 (incoai/GLM-5.3-Flash-DFlash2) has 1.17 B parameters in BF16 (≈2.34 GB) and was trained for this target. It has 5 Qwen3-style layers: hidden_size 4096, a 12288-wide SwiGLU, GQA with 32/8 heads, head_dim 128, QK-norm, RoPE θ=10000 (the drafter has RoPE; the NoPE target doesn't) and a 2048 sliding window. It has neither embed_tokens nor lm_head and uses the target's.

The features: five taps into the target

The fuel is the target's auxiliary hidden states, the EAGLE-3 contract. target_layer_ids = [5, 14, 24, 33, 42] covers shallow to deep, all of them KDA layers. Because the hook captures after the layer, the log shows (6, 15, 25, 34, 43). On GLM the residual is mHC with 4 streams (chapter 15), stored as [tokens, 4, 4096] with the hc_post deferred. patch_glm_eagle3.py materializes the hc_post at those layers, contracts the streams (hc_contract, a mean) down to [tokens, 4096] and gives the model SupportsEagle3.

\[ \begin{aligned} h_p &= \mathrm{RMSNorm}\!\left(W_{fc}\,[\,h^{(5)}_p; h^{(14)}_p; h^{(24)}_p; h^{(33)}_p; h^{(42)}_p\,]\right) \\ K_{\ell,p} &= \mathrm{RoPE}_p\!\left(\mathrm{knorm}(W_{k,\ell}\,h_p)\right), \qquad V_{\ell,p} = W_{v,\ell}\,h_p \end{aligned} \]
  • \(h^{(L)}_p \in \mathbb{R}^{4096}\): the target's state at verified position \(p\), after layer \(L\) (mean of the 4 mHC streams)
  • \(W_{fc} \in \mathbb{R}^{4096\times 20480}\): the fc tensor, which fuses the 5 features
  • \(K_{\ell,p}, V_{\ell,p}\): key and value of drafter layer \(\ell\) for context position \(p\). A single fused GEMM computes all 5 layers (precompute_and_store_context_kv) and the result goes into the 2048 SWA cache

One difference from EAGLE shows up in prefix caching (chapter 33). In EAGLE, the KV at \(p\) embeds token \(p+1\). In DFlash, it depends only on the target's state at \(p\). That's why the drafter's lookup can end exactly at the boundary ([glm53-dflash-boundary-lookup-v1]), without EAGLE's “lookahead” block.

The “2” in DFlash2: dynamic convolution and a candidate selector

The original DFlash suffers from suffix decay: the further toward the end of the block, the worse the draft. According to the DFlash 2 blog, attention devotes little capacity to dependencies within the block (30% in layer 1, 8% in layer 5), and recall@16 drops from 99.5% at position 1 to 87.8% at position 6. On GLM, on prose, acceptance goes from 0.75 at position 1 to 0.06 at position 7. DFlash2 attacks this with two cheap pieces.

Grouped 2-tap dynamic convolution

Before and after each layer's attention and MLP sits a dynamic convolution along the block: each position mixes its own vector with the previous position's. The coefficients add a learned fixed part (base_kernel) to a correction that depends on the content of the current token (kernel_projection).

\[ o_t = \big(b_0 + \delta_0(h_t)\big)\odot h_t \;+\; \mathbb{1}\!\left[t \bmod B \ge 1\right]\,\big(b_1 + \delta_1(h_t)\big)\odot h_{t-1} \]
  • \(h_t\): vector at block position \(t\) (4096 channels); \(o_t\): mixed output
  • \(b_0, b_1\): base coefficients of taps 0 and 1 for one side \(s\), that is, base_kernel[s,0,:] and base_kernel[s,1,:], one per channel (total shape [2 sides, 2 taps, 4096]). Tap 0 multiplies \(h_t\); tap 1 multiplies \(h_{t-1}\)
  • \(\delta_0, \delta_1\): dynamic corrections for those two taps. kernel_projection (4096 → 1024 = 2 sides × 2 taps × 256 groups) produces one number per group of 16 channels (conv_group_size = 16)
  • sides: side \(s=0\) applies the formula to the sublayer's input (\(h_t\) is that input); side \(s=1\) applies the same formula to the sublayer's output, but with coefficients already computed from the input
  • \(B = 1 + k\): block size. The indicator prevents crossing the boundary, since the anchor has no predecessor within the block

In DFlashGroupedConv, prepare() computes the coefficients for both sides from the state before the sublayer. It applies side 0 to the input and stores side 1, which finish() applies to the output. Per layer: norm → conv → attention → conv → norm → conv → MLP → conv.

With the dynamic part off, every position mixes with the same weights, like a fixed filter. With it on, each token decides how much to pull from the previous position, group by group. It's a cheap left→right dependency inside a parallel forward: ≈8.4 M parameters per layer (42.1 M across the 5), and the blog reports +0.7% latency for the conv. The blog also mentions +16.5 M parameters, but that number is from its own model, at a different scale; in this checkpoint the math gives 42.1 M. Watch out: the conv's block is 1 + num_speculative_tokens, so changing \(k\) changes the boundaries it sees.

The candidate selector and the Gumbel walk

The candidate selector (CandidateSelector) replaces the independent per-position argmax with something better. It keeps the 16 best candidates at each position (selector_top_k = 16, via the target's lm_head + torch.topk, because this image's LogitsProcessor has no get_top_k_tokens) and scores the transitions with a low-rank bilinear model, a cousin of a CRF:

\[ s_i(p \to c) = u_{i,c} \;+\; \big\langle\, P[\,c_{i-1,p}\,] \odot W_h\,h_i \,,\; S[\,c_{i,c}\,] \,\big\rangle \]
  • \(u_{i,c}\): “unary” logit of candidate \(c\) at position \(i\)
  • \(P, S \in \mathbb{R}^{154880\times 256}\): predecessor and successor codebooks (selector_rank = 256)
  • \(W_h\,h_i \in \mathbb{R}^{256}\): hidden_projection, a context gate that says which dimensions of the transition matter there
  • \(c_{i-1,p}\): candidate chosen at the previous position (at position 1, the anchor)

The choice is a walk through the trellis (_selector_walk_kernel). At each step, the kernel reads the 16 scores conditioned on the previous choice and takes the largest. Temperature 0 is pure argmax. With \(T>0\) the Gumbel-max trick comes in (chapter 9): \(\arg\max_c\,(s_c/T + g_c)\) with \(g_c = -\log(-\log U_c)\) samples exactly from \(\mathrm{softmax}(s/T)\), with a seed per request and position. Then _cache_draft_logits_kernel writes the 16 realized scores as the draft's logits (\(-\infty\) everywhere else). That's the \(q(x)\) rejection sampling needs to stay lossless.

In “independent argmax” mode, each column picks its own favorite and out comes a sentence nobody would write. In the walk, the first choice drags the following ones along. The blog credits the selector with +0.34 tokens per step at T=0, for +2.0 M parameters and +0.6% latency. Those numbers are from the blog's model; in this checkpoint the selector totals 80.3 M, almost all of it in the two [154880 × 256] codebooks, because GLM's vocabulary is large and every token needs a row in each codebook. The bottom panel of the figure above shows where the transition score comes from, with the real shapes. That's why the drafter's EXL3 pack keeps hidden_projection in BF16: “the selector's precision directly drives acceptance”.

In the TensorFold lane, the walk uses the target's noise

TensorFold runs the same selector with a different picking rule. At position \(d\) of the chain, the winner is \(\arg\max_c\big(s_{d,c} + 0.7\,g_{d,c}\big)\), with \(s = (\ell + 0.6\cdot\text{edge})/T\) (EDGE = 0.6) and \(g\) the target's keyed Gumbel draws for those candidates at those positions (NOISE = 0.7), not noise of the drafter's own. That is only possible because, in that lane, the target's draw is a known function of (seed, position, token), as keyed sampling in chapter 9 shows. The \(q(x)\) is no longer needed either. Equality verification accepts the draft if it is exactly the token the target sampled, so no draft logit has to be kept for a \(p/q\) ratio. Per-position agreement is still bounded by \(\sum\min(p,q)\), the ceiling rejection sampling reaches; the coupling through shared noise is what brings the lane close to that ceiling.

DSpark: anchor, noise and a Markov head

The DeepSeek-V4-Flash card defines DSpark like this: “it is not a new model; it is the same checkpoint with a speculative decoding module attached”. It's a heavy module: 3 stages in the legacy namespace mtp.0..2, loaded as virtual layers 43–45 (dspark_start_layer_idx = max(num_hidden_layers, max(target_ids)+1)). Each stage is a full V4 block, with mHC, 64-head × 512 SWA attention with a sink, and the entire 256-expert MoE (chapter 11). The router in these stages is the learned one, as in the target's deep layers: V4's hash routing only applies to layers 0–2 (extract_layer_index(prefix) < num_hash_layers = 3), and the stages are layers 43–45. The inventory estimates ≈19.8 B parameters, not counted in the card's 284 B.

In the overlay this breaks the hc_post_pre fusion.   2. Context. Stage 0 has main_proj (12288 → 4096, ≈50 M) + main_norm. RoPE has a catch here (chapter 8). In the V4 reference, the pure-window layers (0, 1 and the 3 stages) use plain RoPE with θ = 10000, and YaRN ×16 is applied only to the compressed layers. Anemll's vLLM promotes YaRN to all layers. A quality A/B at 128K+ is still missing before it becomes the default.
  3. Parallel backbone. The block [anchor, noise×4] goes through the 3 stages (DeepSeekV4DSparkLayer: mHC → attention → mHC → MoE → mHC) in a single non-causal forward. The residual is replicated across the hc_mult streams (x.unsqueeze(1).repeat(1, hc_mult, 1)).   4. Sequential refinement. This is where the piece DFlash doesn't have comes in.

Multimodal collision and the low-rank transition bias

Positions computed in parallel yield marginal distributions, an average over what the earlier positions could have been. With two plausible continuations, the marginals blend both, and the paper calls this multimodal collision. The fix is the Markov head (DeepSeekV4DSparkMarkovHead), a bias that depends on the token actually chosen before:

\[ \ell_i = \ell^{\text{base}}_i + W_2\,W_1[\,y_i\,], \qquad y_{i+1} = \arg\max \ell_i, \qquad y_0 = \text{anchor} \]
  • \(\ell^{\text{base}}_i\): logits at position \(i\), coming out of the parallel backbone
  • \(y_{i+1}\): token chosen by argmax (or sampled), one position at a time, left to right
  • \(W_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{V\times 256}\) (markov_w1): a lookup that maps the previous token to 256 dimensions
  • \(W_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{V\times 256}\) (markov_w2): back to the vocabulary (\(V = 129{,}280\)). \(W_2W_1\) is a rank-256 bigram (dspark_markov_rank)

Why low rank? A full \(V\times V\) bigram would have \(\approx 1.7\times10^{10}\) entries, ≈33 GB in BF16 (our calculation). Factored, it's two matrices of 129,280 × 256 × 2 B ≈ 66 MB each; together they take up 0.4% of the full bigram's area. Each step costs one lookup (read row \(y_i\) of \(W_1\), a 256-vector) and one GEMV (multiply \(W_2\) by that vector, which gives a bias for each of the 129,280 tokens). The price of factoring is expressiveness: \(W_2W_1\) can only represent bigrams that fit in 256 “directions”. The design bets that this is enough for a bias, since the backbone still does the heavy lifting and the head only needs to nudge the next position toward the right continuation. Toggle the head on and off in the figure and watch the final sentence; the bottom panel shows the lookup and the GEMV with the real shapes.

The confidence head

The confidence head (DeepSeekV4DSparkConfidenceHead) is a Linear(4096 + 256 → 1) in FP32 over the position's dense state and the previous token's Markov embedding. It estimates the chance that the draft will be accepted, with an analytic training target:

\[ \begin{aligned} c_i &= \sigma\!\left(w^\top[\,h_i\,;\,W_1[y_i]\,]\right) \\ c^\star_i &= \sum_x \min\big(p(x), q(x)\big) = 1 - \mathrm{TV}(p,q) = 1 - \tfrac12\lVert p-q\rVert_1 \end{aligned} \]
  • \(p, q\): target and draft distributions at the position; \(\mathrm{TV}\) is the total variation distance
  • \(\sum_x \min(p,q)\) is the exact probability that rejection sampling accepts (speculative_acceptance_confidence)
  • training (paper): \(L = 0.1\,L_{ce} + 0.9\,L_{tv} + 1.0\,L_{conf}\), weighted by \(e^{-(k-1)/\gamma}\) at position \(k\), with per-position temperature calibration

The weight on \(L_{tv}\) says it all: minimizing the TV distance to the target means maximizing expected acceptance. The confidences turn into prefix survival, \(a_j = \prod_{i\le j} c_i\) (cumulative_survival), the same \(a_j\) from the first section, now predicted before verification.

How much to verify? Fixed k, adaptive k and confidence-guided verification

Drafting got cheap; verifying didn't. Each request becomes \(1+k\) rows in the target's forward: 8 on GLM with DFLASH_TOKENS=7, and the CUDA graphs follow suit (1 2 4 8 16 24 32 = up to 4 sequences × 8). On JSON, acceptance is high (0.98…0.83) and it pays to verify everything. On prose (0.75…0.06), the last positions almost never pass and cost rows anyway.

1. Picking the right k for the hardware

GLM uses k = 7 because the trained block has 8 positions. On the 4-Spark kit (TP4) the optimum became k = 3: 53% acceptance versus 30% with k=7, and prose at 31–33 tok/s versus 26–28. k=2 and k=5 were worse, and without speculation the kit did 22.6. The repo only reports the measurement. The reading is our interpretation: on TP4 each verified row costs more communication among the 4 nodes, and the low acceptance of the late positions no longer pays that bill.

2. Adaptive k via a moving average (GLM)

Adaptive k (patch_adaptive_k.py, GLM53_ADAPTIVE_K=ema) keeps the drafter proposing 7, but the scheduler verifies only a prefix \(n \in\) {2, 4, 7}, chosen by an exponential moving average of each request's accepted counts:

\[ \begin{aligned} e &\leftarrow \alpha\cdot \text{obs} + (1-\alpha)\cdot e \\ n &= \max\{\,v \in \mathcal{S} : v \le \lceil e + m \rceil\,\} \\ \text{obs} &= \begin{cases} \max(7, n) & \text{accepted all } n \text{ (max)}\\ \text{accepted} & \text{otherwise}\end{cases} \end{aligned} \]
  • \(\alpha\) = GLM53_ADAPTIVE_K_ALPHA (0.25): weight of the new observation; larger reacts faster and oscillates more
  • obs: what the EMA receives; the “accepted all \(n\)” case follows GLM53_ADAPTIVE_K_SATURATE (max or n)
  • \(m\) = GLM53_ADAPTIVE_K_MARGIN (1.0): an optimistic margin that pulls toward verifying more
  • \(\mathcal{S}\) = GLM53_ADAPTIVE_K_SET; each \(n\) requires a CUDA graph of \(n+1\) rows (3, 5, 8)
  • \(e\) is initialized pulled toward 7 (\(\alpha\cdot\text{obs} + (1-\alpha)\cdot 7\)) and only cuts after GLM53_ADAPTIVE_K_MIN_STEPS = 4 observations; structured output always stays at 7

“Accepted all” is a censored observation: if only 2 were verified and passed, the true acceptance could be 7. With GLM53_ADAPTIVE_K_SATURATE=max the average receives 7 and can rise; with n, it receives 2 and turns into a ratchet that only goes down. The step's \(n\) is the batch minimum, because FLASHINFER_MLA_SPARSE_SM120 and the KDA backend only accept FULL CUDA graphs with a uniform batch (chapter 29). Measured on 2026-09-08, lossless at temperature 0: +21% on the “Silk Road” prompt, +13% on sky/sunset, +10% on hash-map and +5–15% on code.

To port the technique, you need to know where it plugs into vLLM. patch_adaptive_k.py touches two files. In v1/core/sched/scheduler.py, there are three spots. (1) Observe: in update_from_output, each request reports how many drafts were verified and how many passed, and that feeds the EMA. (2) Choose: in schedule(), the step's number of draft slots (num_spec_tokens_to_schedule) becomes the batch minimum; that's the number the AsyncScheduler (the path the kit uses) relies on to size the placeholders. (3) Trim: on the synchronous path, update_draft_token_ids trims spec_token_ids to \(n\). In v1/worker/gpu/cudagraph_utils.py, decode_query_lens gains the extra uniform-decode lengths (\(n+1\) = 3, 5, 8). Without them, steps with \(n = 2\) or \(4\) would have no FULL uniform-decode graph. For the same reason, the launcher builds the capture-size list on its own: the default list 1 2 4 8 16 24 32 lacks the shapes for 3 and 5 rows per request (3, 6, 9, 12 and 5, 10, 15, 20 for up to 4 requests). Boot confirms it with uniform decode graph query lens: [3, 5, 8].

3. Confidence-scheduled verification (DeepSeek, Stage-C lane)

DSpark knows in advance how likely each draft is to pass. The Stage-C overlay has two schedulers (VLLM_DSPARK_CONFIDENCE_SCHEDULER). The threshold one (confidence_threshold_prefix_length) verifies the longest prefix with \(a_j \ge\) VLLM_DSPARK_CONFIDENCE_THRESHOLD. The hardware-aware one (hardware_aware_prefix_schedule) pools the drafts from all requests, admits them in decreasing order of survival and keeps the point of maximum expected rate:

\[ \max_{\ell_1..\ell_R}\ \Big(R + \sum_{r}\sum_{j\le \ell_r} a_{r,j}\Big)\cdot \mathrm{sps}\Big(R + \sum_r \ell_r\Big) \]
  • \(\ell_r\): verified drafts of request \(r\); \(R\): requests, each with 1 bonus token
  • \(\mathrm{sps}(\cdot)\): steps per second as a function of the batch's tokens, the VLLM_DSPARK_SPS_CURVE curve; empty means 1.0

Both are non-anticipating: position \(n\) gets in by looking only at confidences up to \(n\), never at the target's verdict, and that's what keeps rejection sampling unbiased. The figure brings the two policies together: in GLM mode, a simulated request with the measured acceptances; in DeepSeek mode, the scheduler's algorithm over a toy batch.

In GLM mode, compare the readouts: on prose, adaptive-k delivers slightly fewer tokens per step but verifies far fewer rows, and the net tok/s comes out positive when each row is expensive; on structured text it stays at 7 and breaks even. Raise α and watch the staircase shake at every bad step; in the “prose → struct.” scenario, switch saturate to n and watch the ratchet: the staircase stays stuck at the bottom even when the text gets easy. In DeepSeek mode, with the “drops with batch” curve, the planner leaves out the hard-prose drafts; with the flat curve, it admits everything.

In the TensorFold lane, no EMA

The recipe's rule does not look at the request's past. TensorFold does have an adaptive rule, fa, which picks 1 to 3 drafts from a moving average of acceptance, but the recipe doesn't use it. Every round, DFlash2 walks the chain and a stop rule decides where to cut it from the chain's own confidences. It resembles DSpark's threshold scheduler, but without a trained head: the confidence comes from the selector's scores and the target's keyed noise. That removes both problems of the EMA, the censored observation and the lag in reacting when the text changes genre. Since that lane's CUDA graphs are captured per number of window rows (one for each count from 1 to 32 in the four-conversation batch), each conversation stops at its own depth, without the “batch minimum” that adaptive-k needs. And non-anticipation is no longer a correctness requirement: with equality verification, every rule returns the same reply, and only the speed changes. The recipe's rule is fnc7:0.3; the section Update: DFlash2 on TensorFold opens up the three families of rules and the simulator that tuned them.

In the recipe

Mia's default lane does not use scheduled verification. The VLLM_DSPARK_* knobs only exist in lane B (the Stage-C image); on the Anemll 0.1.1 image they produce “Unknown vLLM environment variable” and do nothing. The official Vision-Exp recipe uses "enable_adaptive_verification": true with num_speculative_tokens: 3, a flag that is absent from Mia's repo and requires FULL CUDA graphs.

Where the drafter's state lives

Every block drafter carries state between steps: the window's injected KV. In the vLLM lane, GLM put it inside vLLM's KV manager; GLM's TensorFold lane uses a third design, a ring per conversation with no paging at all, which is left for the next section. On DeepSeek there are two designs. In the default lane (Anemll 0.1.1), the drafter's 128-token window also lives in vLLM, as SlidingWindowMLASpec groups (blocks 4/8) filled by precompute_and_store_context_kv. In the Stage-C overlay (lane B, the readable reference implementation), it leaves vLLM and becomes a ring of its own. Each choice brought a different problem.

GLM: a KV group that didn't fit the page

The drafter's KV stays in BF16 (kv_cache_dtype: "auto") even with the target on fp8_ds_mla. Per the recipe, the fp8_ds_mla layout is MLA-only, and SM121 has no FA3/FA4 for plain FP8 KV. patch_dflash2.py forces that auto when the target uses fp8*/nvfp4. The math: 8 KV heads × 128 × {K,V} × 2 B = 4096 B per token per layer, or 2048 B per rank on TP2. A full window across the 5 layers takes ≈20 MiB.

The problem was geometric (chapter 31). GLM's hybrid manager uses a uniform physical page, sized by MLA: 3584 tokens × 656 B = 2,351,104 B. Inheriting that block made each drafter layer cost tens of MiB per block and pinned concurrency near 1×. patch_glm5_drafter_group.py creates an extra SlidingWindowSpec group, appended last to keep ids stable. If the drafter's page divides the MLA page, it's exact-fit; otherwise, it's padded slot-share: a block of 64, the page padded up to the MLA size, and drafter layer \(i\) co-owns MLA tensor \(i\) on disjoint ids. The pool reached 1,754,237 tokens at 1M, and a ~36k prompt dropped from 44.6% to ~16% occupancy.

With a block of 64, the window (2048) plus the in-flight tokens (2048) required 65 ids per request. GLM53_DRAFT_KV_COMPACT=1 (the default with SPEC_METHOD=dflash; an explicit empty value is an error) picks:

\[ B^\star = \max\{\,B : 64 \mid B,\ \ B \mid B_{\text{MLA}},\ \ B\cdot b_{\text{draft}} \le B_{\text{MLA}}\cdot b_{\text{MLA}}\,\} \]
  • \(B_{\text{MLA}}\): MLA block (3584 on TP2; 2560 on TP3); \(b_{\text{MLA}}\) = 656 B/token
  • \(b_{\text{draft}}\): 2048 B/token on TP2; 1536 on TP3 → \(B^\star\) = 896 and 640

The gain is in ids, not VRAM, and the repo insists on that. Worst-case reserved ids fell from 180 to 121 (−32.78%), and with 14 GiB of KV the logged pool went from 876,958 to 1,572,073 tokens (1.85×). Repeat TTFT for a 28,672-token prefix with a 64-token tail dropped 90% (2.623 → 0.302 s). In decode there was no universal gain: structured +1.15%, code +1.21%, prose −3.30%.

GLM: shrinking the drafter (EXL3 6 bpw without calibration)

The optional dense-h3 profile quantizes DFlash2 to 6 bpw in EXL3 (chapter 22) with tools/dflash2_exl3_quant.sh (BITS=6.0, fork exllamav3@63b32f0, ~25 min on a GB10). There is no calibration: the converter flags DFlash2 models with caps["uncalibrated_quantize"] = True, skips the calibration forwards and quantizes each linear against a synthetic Hessian.

Why does that work? Quantizers like GPTQ and EXL3 (chapter 20) don't minimize the weight error but the error at the layer's output, \(\operatorname{tr}\big((W-\hat W)\,H\,(W-\hat W)^\top\big)\). The Hessian \(H = \mathbb{E}[xx^\top]\), measured on real activations, says which input directions matter. Without data, init_H_data makes up an \(H\). If it's close to \(I\), the objective falls back to the weight error \(\lVert W-\hat W\rVert^2\), which treats all directions as equal (our interpretation, based on chapter 20). In a drafter that's acceptable, because an error there only costs acceptance, never output correctness: the target verifies every token.

The bit budget covers q_proj, o_proj, the MLP (gate/up/down), kernel_projection and fc; the last two can be left out with DRAFT_QUANT_KERNEL_PROJ=0 and DRAFT_QUANT_FC=0. These stay in BF16:

  • k_proj/v_proj (sentinel K=16), because the fused context-KV GEMM needs a plain tensor;
  • the selector's hidden_projection, 1 M parameters that steer the choice and would save only ~2 MB;
  • the selector's two codebooks (79 M, copied byte for byte from the snapshot);
  • the norms and base_kernel.

At serving time, patch_dflash2_exl3.py calls offset_draft_layer_prefixes, because the pack declares model.layers.0… while at runtime the drafter's layers are 45–49. The patch also guards the fused-KV assembly when qkv_proj carries only K/V (bf16_shards=[1,2]). The profile is opt-in, TP2-only and still without an A/B. With no calibration, the quality sensor is the per-position acceptance curve.

In the TensorFold lane, the drafter already runs in 4 bits

The TensorFold lane downloads the same DFlash2 in BF16 (~2.3 GB), but drafts with 4-bit copies made by the engine: the drafter's matrices and a copy of the target's head. In the engine's words, “draft steps use the quantized head; verification keeps the original head”: drafting uses the cheap copy, and verification the real head. The whole draft model takes 0.63 GiB per rank, counting the 4-bit copies and the selector's codebooks. Patch 0011 added TF_GLM_DRAFT_QUANT, which chooses how each group's range is computed: minmax (the default) or an mse search. The argument is the same as the synthetic Hessian's above, now as an engine rule: “Drafts only propose, so either keeps every reply; only acceptance can move”. The difference in stance between the lanes is clear: in vLLM, the quantized drafter is an optional profile still without an A/B; in TensorFold, 4 bits is the default.

DeepSeek, lane A (default): the window in vLLM's SWA groups

On the Anemll 0.1.1 image, DSpark does what GLM does: the 3 stages' 128-token window is KV managed by the hybrid allocator. Boot shows 1× MLAAttentionSpec (block 256) + 3× SlidingWindowMLASpec (64/4/8). The block-64 SWA group belongs to the target's window layers; the block-4 and block-8 groups belong to the drafter. As the hybrid allocator requires (chapter 31), they all have the same page size: 64 rows × 584 B = 37,376 B. The window is filled by precompute_and_store_context_kv, from the target's features, the same name as GLM's fused GEMM. The window lives in blocks of each request's block table, so it follows the request when the batch is condensed. This reading is our interpretation; the Keys patches in the next subsection belong to the Stage-C design. This lane's weak spot is the prefix cache.

Prefix cache × the drafter's window

On a prefix-cache hit, the target skips the prefix and the drafter never receives its features. The window stays empty, and the verifier ends up accepting degenerate drafts (e.g. ["json + stop). In DeepSeek's lane A, DSPARK_ENABLE_DSPARK_SWA_PREFIX=1 (port of Anemll/dspark-vllm-gx10#2) patches precisely kv_cache_manager.py, because there the window is managed KV. It caps the hit at num_tokens − 1 − 128 and recomputes the window; with the json60 prompt repeated, upstream went from 12.1 to 86.5 tok/s. On GLM, the [glm53-dflash-swa-replay] stages back the hit off until 2048 fresh tokens are rebuilt. Details in chapter 33.

In the TensorFold lane, the window travels with the state

TensorFold has no block-based prefix cache. It keeps whole prompt states at chosen points: the end of the prompt, the end of the system block and the point where two prompts stop matching. Each state carries a copy of the DFlash2 window, 20 MiB per rank (2,048 rows × 10 KiB), so a resumed prompt reaches the drafter with its context ready, with no empty window and no recomputation. Patch 0015 made shared-prefix states carry that window, and 0025 adapted the snapshot to the ring of the next section, which cannot be copied as a contiguous slice of positions. The price is fixed memory per kept state (each entry reserves ~45 MiB at startup, according to v1.3.2's README; patch 0097 later measured ~91 MiB on two Sparks) and reuse only at those points, not at any prefix. The full mechanism is in An exact engine.

DeepSeek (Stage-C overlay): a ring outside vLLM and the batch-row shuffle

In lane B, the DSparkProposer tells vLLM it needs no KV (block_size = 1) and keeps the window in main_kv_cache [max_num_seqs, 128, 512] BF16, one per stage. Writes go to a per-position ring, slot = pos % 128 (store_main_kv). When the target rejects part of the \(k+1\) rows, the rejected features would describe a future that never happened. The rejected mask (torch.where(valid, new, old)) keeps the old value. VLLM_DSPARK_GPU_REJECTED_CONTEXT_MASK=1 does this on the GPU, without syncing with the CPU. The legacy path _trim_rejected_target_context trims on the CPU and requires uniform lengths. VLLM_DSPARK_REFERENCE_KV_QUANT_DEQUANT simulates the training's FP8 quant/dequant there (UE8M0, groups of 64 on the NoPE dimensions).

State outside the managed KV exacts its price under continuous batching (chapter 32): nobody in vLLM knows that buffer belongs to a request. Look at the three scenes.

These are the fixes from the “Keys concurrency patch” (patches/keys-concurrency.patch, by drowzeys, a.k.a. “Keys”; nothing to do with API keys). With max_num_seqs > 1, acceptance went to ~0 without a crash: the ring was indexed by batch row, and batch condensation swapped the histories. Patch 1 creates _req_id_to_slot + _free_slots (lowest free slot first) and does gather/scatter by slot_index; with the identity permutation, the old path runs byte for byte. Patch 2 handles the chunked-prefill ragged batch: the [batch, seq, H] reshape gave HTTP 500 “got 41 rows for batch_size=2”. Now the anchor is computed per request and the ring is written by query_start_loc (_store_main_kv_ragged); mixed steps are never captured in a graph, so the Python loops there are safe. Patch 2b pulls the ragged detection out of if num_rejected_tokens_gpu is not None, because prefills without rejections still gave a 500. In validation with GSM8K N=8: 0 errors, 93.5% versus 95.0% sequential, 97.5% agreement.

Three defenses complete the proposer. The non-uniform batch guard makes the step skip speculation when rows % batch_size ≠ 0, instead of killing the WorkerProc. The slot clamp (DSPARK_SLOT_CLAMP) pins a stale slot to [0, rows−1] inside the graph, instead of triggering indexSelectSmallIndex, and only logs DSPARK_STALE_SLOT_INDEX outside capture. A wrong slot produces a bad draft that the target rejects, without corrupting the output. The warmup (make_dspark_warmup_draft_token_ids) returns a fixed block of noise_token_id on the first step, because stable shapes are a requirement of the drafter's PIECEWISE CUDA graph, which runs over persistent buffers.

Mind the order, which matters when you port this. In the current overlay, the guard runs before the ragged branch (dspark_proposer.py, right at the start of propose). The historical examples from Patches 2/2b (41 rows for 2 requests, 166 for 3) have a nonzero remainder and today skip speculation on that step, returning zeros. The query_start_loc path serves batches with unequal segments whose sum is divisible by the number of requests (e.g. 33 + 7 = 40 for 2). This comes from reading the code; the interpretation is ours.

Update: DFlash2 on TensorFold

The GLM recipe gained a second version . The checkpoint is the same, so are the two DGX Sparks, and the drafter is still incoai/GLM-5.3-Flash-DFlash2, pinned at a different revision (bf582e4e…). What changed is the engine: instead of vLLM, the recipe runs TensorFold v0.6.0 + 53 patches, an engine written for the GLM family. The whole engine is in An exact engine, and the recipe in Swapping the engine. What matters here is what changes for a block drafter when the engine treats exactness as a contract: a draft is accepted only when it equals the token the same engine would produce serially, and the reply with drafts is, bit for bit, the reply of serial decoding.

Three consequences change the design of DFlash2. The drafter's job becomes guessing the target's sample, rather than approximating its distribution. The rule that decides how many drafts to propose can be anything, because it only affects speed, and so it can be tuned offline. And each conversation's drafter state has to give the same bits it would give alone, even with four conversations in the same batch.

A round: copies first, DFlash2 second

In TensorFold, the unit of time of decode is the round. For one conversation, it has five steps:

  1. Copy drafts. If the last 8 tokens (counting the pending one) appeared before, in the prompt or in the reply, the tokens that followed the most recent occurrence become drafts, up to 15 per round. These are the copy drafts, a numpy prompt lookup that costs almost nothing. When they exist, they replace the DFlash2 chain in that round. Both ranks hold the same prompt and the same tokens, so they compute the same proposals without exchanging a message.
  2. DFlash2 block pass. Without a copy, the drafter runs one forward over [pending, mask×7]. The draft head is split by vocabulary between the two Sparks, so each rank sends its top-k (values and ids) in an all-gather, and the host merges the two lists by decreasing value, then by id.
  3. Walk and stop rule. On the host, in float64, the selector picks the chain with the target's noise (selector section), and a stop rule decides where to cut it.
  4. Verification. One target forward over the window [pending, drafts…], a keyed sample on each row, and acceptance while draft = sample. At the first disagreement, the target's sample enters as the correction; if every draft passes, it enters as the bonus.
  5. Commit. The kept rows update the target's KV, the KDA state and the drafter's context: the taps of the 5 target layers for those rows ([n, 5·4096]) become K/V in the DFlash2 ring. Rejected rows never get in.

The “copies first” order is a cost hierarchy. A copied round does not run the drafter's block and can carry 15 drafts at once, which turns a long quote into a single round. The recipe's numbers: COPY=1 took quote and edit replies from 80.3 to 84.4 tok/s, with prose and code unchanged, and COPY_MAX=15 took edits from 84 to 119 tok/s (two boots each). Code needed care, because it repeats short snippets (def __init__(self) whose continuations diverge. Over 39 real replies, copies taken from the reply itself kept 23% of the drafts, against 74% for copies taken from the prompt. That's why COPY_CODE=1 only copies from the reply after matching 16 tokens and captures 16-row windows as CUDA graphs: code went from 55.4 to 56.2 tok/s and edits from 120 to 125 (two boots each). A design detail: a copied round doesn't enter the acceptance average of DFlash2's adaptive rule (fa), because it would measure the copy, not the drafter.

Three stop rules

The DFlash2 chain has up to 7 drafts, but proposing all of them is wasteful when the last ones almost never pass. Each draft \(j\) gets a confidence, and the product of the confidences up to \(j\) estimates the chance that the first \(j\) all pass: it is the same prefix survival as DSpark's. TensorFold's rules differ in which confidence they use and how they cut:

\[ \begin{aligned} \texttt{fc}N{:}P:&\quad c_j = \mathrm{softmax}(s_j)[\text{pick}_j], \quad \text{cut before } j \text{ if } \textstyle\prod_{i\le j} c_i < P \\ \texttt{fnc}N{:}P:&\quad \tilde c_j = \mathrm{softmax}\!\big((s_j + g_j)/\beta\big)[\text{pick}_j], \quad \text{same cut} \\ \texttt{fcost}N:&\quad k^\star = \arg\max_{0\le k\le N}\ \frac{1 + \sum_{j\le k}\prod_{i\le j}\tilde c_i}{V(k+1) + B} \end{aligned} \]
  • \(s_j\): the candidates' scores at position \(j\), \((\ell + 0.6\cdot\text{edge})/T\); \(\text{pick}_j\): the candidate the walk chose there
  • \(g_j\): the target's keyed Gumbel draws for those candidates (in greedy, there is no noise); \(\beta = 2.0\)
  • \(N\): draft cap; \(P\): threshold on the product. The first draft is always kept
  • \(V(R)\): cost of verifying \(R\) rows; \(B\): cost of the DFlash2 block. In the engine, both come from a table measured at startup, which also counts updating one context row. The numerator is \(E[\text{tokens}\mid k]\), including the token the target always emits

The difference between fc and fnc is subtle and worth it. The walk picks with \(s + 0.7\,g\), so the target's noise has already pushed the choice. The fc confidence, \(\mathrm{softmax}(s)\), ignores that push and only asks “how much does the drafter like its own guess?”. If the noise handed the win to a candidate the drafter considered second-rate, fc sees a low confidence and cuts, even though the target, which receives the same push, tends to sample exactly that candidate. fnc asks the right question: according to the code, \(\tilde c_j\) is the chance that the target's keyed sample is the pick when the target's scores are the drafter's plus independent Gumbel(\(\beta\)) errors. \(\beta\) measures the size of that disagreement and was fitted by log loss on 9 dumps from 2026-09-30 (7 of sampled prose, 2 of greedy code). It came out at 2.0 on all of them, on the sampled ones alone and on the greedy ones alone; a finer grid found 1.8, with a log loss only 0.2% lower. In greedy, with no noise, \(\beta\) only softens the softmax of the scores (our interpretation: the drafter tends to be more confident than it is right).

Measured in the recipe against fc5:0.3, two boots each, fnc7:0.3 took prose from 47.2 to 50.6 tok/s and code from 51.7 to 55.5. The comparison mixes two changes (the cap also went from 5 to 7), and the recipe publishes no separate number for fcost7:noisy, which cuts at the maximum of expected tokens per millisecond instead of at a fixed threshold. The sentence that settles the matter is from config.sh: “Drafts only propose: replies are the same under every policy”.

The offline lab: stop rules without a GPU

How do you choose between fc5:0.3, fnc7:0.3 and fcost7? In the vLLM lane, adaptive-k was tuned with live measurements, boot after boot. The TensorFold lane used exactness as a research tool. With TF_GLM_DRAFT_DUMP=<dir> on both ranks, a request decodes one token per round (the reply is the serial one), but before each round it runs the DFlash2 block and records everything a stop rule could want to know: the merged candidates and their logits, the selector's edges, the request's keyed Gumbel draws, the chain the production rule would propose and, after verification, the target's top-32 and the committed token. Rank 0 writes one .npz per request. It is a teacher-forced record: the text is what the target actually wrote, position by position.

The trick is in one sentence of the simulator. Since sampling is keyed by (seed, position, token), a round that starts at position \(i\) and proposes a chain keeps exactly the drafts equal to the committed tokens \(i+1, i+2, \dots\), up to the first mismatch, plus one sampled token. The token sequence is fixed, whatever the rule, and any rule can be replayed offline, on the CPU, with numpy. You walk the tape: the rule picks \(k\), \(\min(k, L_i)\) drafts are kept (\(L_i\) is how many of the full chain match), and you jump to \(i + \min(k, L_i) + 1\). A cost table turns rounds into milliseconds, and milliseconds into tok/s. The code itself adds a caveat: the replay is exact “up to the drafter's numerics”, because in the dump the taps come from one-row verifies and, in a live round, from wider windows.

\[ T^\star(i) = \min_{0\le k\le N}\Big[\, V(k+1) + B\cdot\mathbb{1}[k>0] + T^\star\big(i + \min(k, L_i) + 1\big) \Big], \qquad T^\star(n) = 0 \]
  • \(T^\star(i)\): the least time to emit the rest of the reply from position \(i\); \(n\): the length of the reply
  • \(L_i\): how many drafts of the full chain proposed at \(i\) match the committed tokens, read from the dump
  • \(V, B\): the cost table; a round with no draft doesn't run the block

This is the simulator's oracle (oracle7), a dynamic program with hindsight that knows \(L_i\) before choosing \(k\). No real rule gets there, which is why it serves as a ceiling: it shows how much is still left to gain. The simulator also compares acceptance predictors (the current confidence, the noise-aware one, a constant rate per depth, a logistic regression) by log loss, Brier score and AUC, and that is how \(\beta\) came out. In the figure, you do the same with a synthetic dump.

Notice three things. The tape, the committed tokens, is the same under every rule; only the round brackets change. f7 wastes rows when the text gets hard and hits the jackpot on structured output, where almost everything passes. And fcost7 hardly ever cuts at the end of a good chain, because, according to the measured table, going from 7 to 8 rows costs only 1.5 ms, while going from 1 to 2 costs 6.4 ms. In the vLLM lane, this lab doesn't work the same way outside greedy. With rejection sampling, the token emitted after a rejection comes from the residual distribution \((p-q)_+\), which depends on what the drafter proposed; changing the rule changes the reply itself, and each rule has to be measured live (our interpretation).

One ring per conversation

DFlash2 only attends to a sliding window of 2,048 context positions. In TensorFold v0.6.0, that context lives in a ring of 2,176 rows (the window, plus the block, plus one read tile, rounded up to whole 64-row tiles), addressed by pos mod 2176. Each row holds K and V for the drafter's 5 layers, 10 KiB per rank. The ring costs 21 MiB per rank, whatever the request's window; before, the engine reserved a flat buffer the size of the whole window plus one block, 10 KiB per rank per token. TensorFold v0.6.0's CHANGELOG sums up the change (the block now reads only the window's tiles, and the context moved into the ring): 8% faster at 52k tokens and 3.8 GiB less per rank on the two Sparks. For the recipe, the arithmetic is ours: with windows of 1,048,576 tokens, a flat buffer would cost ~10 GiB per rank per conversation, and four conversations would not fit; four rings cost ~85 MiB.

Reading a window from a ring has one detail (our reading of the drafter's attention kernel; patch 0027's segmented kernel repeats the same loop). The 2,048-position window counts the pending token itself, so the kernel keeps window = 2047 positions behind it. It starts reading at \(\lfloor (s-2047)/64\rfloor\cdot 64\), rounded down to a 64-row tile, and goes up to the block's last row, \(s+7\), where \(s\) is the position of the pending token. In the worst case that is \(2047 + 63 + 8 = 2118\) rows. The ring has the fewest whole tiles that cover this, \(\lceil 2118/64\rceil = 34\) tiles, that is, \(34 \times 64 = 2176\) rows: it holds the whole read without overwriting anything still to be read. The block is also written into the ring during the draft pass, right after the context. On commit, the kept rows are rewritten from the target's taps, and the rejected ones stay as garbage that the next block overwrites.

With four conversations, patch 0027 builds a MultiDrafter: a single set of weights and a pool of rings per layer, [kv_heads, S·cap + trash, head_dim] in BF16. Conversation \(i\) occupies the rows from \(i\cdot\text{cap}\) on, and the last ones are trash rows, where the padding rows of launches captured as CUDA graphs go. The conversations' blocks run side by side in a single pass, and the declared invariant is that each conversation's candidates have the bits of its solo drafter. To get there, the pass separates what can be batched from what can't:

  • Batched: everything that is row-independent, such as the 4-bit matmuls, the norms and the rotary.
  • Batched, respecting boundaries: the 2-tap convolution gained the SEG parameter, so that each block's first row does not mix with the neighboring block's last one. It is the indicator \(\mathbb{1}[t \bmod B \ge 1]\) from the conv equation, now applied to several blocks. Attention became a segmented kernel that reads each segment's slot and position from a table, with the same loop and the same arithmetic as the solo kernel.
  • Per conversation: the selector's projection (cuBLAS) and torch.topk, which may choose kernels by row count and would therefore change the bits.

On commit, the kept rows of all conversations are stacked into launches of up to t_max rows, rounded to multiples of the block. Each row carries its own position and its own destination, \(\text{slot}\cdot\text{cap} + (\text{pos} \bmod 2176)\), and the padding goes to the trash. There is one block-pass CUDA graph for each number of conversations (1 to 4) and one per row bucket for the updates. Capture uses tables that send everything to the trash, so it never touches a live conversation.

Compare with the other two designs in this chapter. In GLM's vLLM lane, the drafter's context had to fit into the hybrid allocator's pages, with padded slot-share and compact blocks; TensorFold pages nothing, and that problem does not exist. In DeepSeek's Stage-C overlay, the ring indexed by batch row broke under condensation and needed Patch 1's req_id → slot map. In TensorFold, rank 0 decides each conversation's slot and announces it in each iteration's message, and both ranks apply it through the same code. A conversation only changes slot through an explicit operation, which copies its DFlash2 context along.

In the recipes: from checkpoint to --speculative-config

Start with the most confusing number: why does DeepSeek use k = 6 if the trained block has 5? The default lane (Anemll 0.1.1, vLLM 0.25.2.dev0+g752a3a504) maps DSpark onto the MTP machinery, with n_predict = num_nextn_predict_layers. In classic MTP one layer is re-executed at every step, and the rule “Ensure divisibility for MTP module reuse” requires \(k \bmod n_{\text{predict}} = 0\) when \(k > n_{\text{predict}}\). The 0731 declared 1 and booted with k=5. Vision-Exp declares 3; combined with \(k \ge 5\) (below that the block is truncated, silently on 0.25.2 and with a rejection on ≥ 0.26), the smallest legal value becomes 6. But DSpark's stages are stacked in one forward, never re-executed, and k=6 forces a noise query beyond what was trained. The Stage-C overlay (config/speculative.py) maps it correctly: deepseek_v4 → deepseek_v4_dspark with n_predict = dspark_block_size, dspark inside use_eagle() and the auxiliary states in compute_hash. In lane A, hotfix-vllm-dspark-block-k.py adds self.method != "dspark" to the rule (target file 3f1abd1c… → 7fffe035…) and relaxes the launcher to \(k \ge 1\).

With the hotfix, k=5 measured 56.6 tok/s versus 55.3 (greedy, 8K) and 56.6 versus 53.6 (32K), with the same per-position acceptance: the gain comes entirely from the cheaper step. Capture follows along: 6 × 6 = 36 → 40 instead of 6 × 7 = 42 → 48. When nobody rounded (capture 42), the 6-sequence step ran without a graph and the aggregate at c=6 fell from 139 to 122.

What about Qwen?

The Qwen3.8-Flash-Next recipe sticks with sequential MTP (num_speculative_tokens: 3, one re-executed layer, reduced vocabulary; chapter 36) and doesn't use a block drafter. The general lesson: the speculative method depends on what was trained for that target, not on a free choice made by the recipe.

Your recipe with a block drafter

  1. Read the drafter's config.json: block_size, mask/noise id, target_layer_ids, is_causal, sliding_window, Q/KV heads divisible by TP, optional scales. MiMo-V2.6-Flash, for example, ships a 5-layer SWA DFlash (1024 window), block 8, target_layer_ids [0,11,23,35,47] and mask 151675.
  2. Secure the features: does the target expose auxiliary states (SupportsEagle3)? If the residual is multi-stream, contract it first.
  3. Non-causal mask: check the drafter's backend and whether it honors is_causal: false.
  4. Drafter KV: dtype, group in the hybrid manager and page; or state outside the KV, with per-request slots.
  5. k from the trained block (block−1 in DFlash, block in DSpark), following your vLLM's validation rules, with CUDA graphs in multiples of \(k+1\).
  6. Measure per-position acceptance with real prompts (scripts/spec-accept-gate.sh on GLM, scripts/spec-acceptance.py on DS) and put a canary at boot.
  7. Only then optimize (adaptive k, confidence, drafter TP, replication), always in A/B/A and testing concurrency: max_num_seqs>1, mixed chunked prefill, repeated prefixes. If your engine verifies by equality with keyed sampling, as TensorFold does, tune the stop rule offline, from dumps, before spending boots.
