On September 27, 2026, MiMo-V2.6-Flash-MOPD showed up on Hugging Face: the variant of MiMo-V2.6-Flash with an extra post-training stage (the “MOPD”, explained in the first section). It has 309 billion parameters, 15 billion active per token, hybrid attention with a 128-token window, K and V of different sizes, experts already stored in MXFP4 and two built-in drafters. “Day-0” support in vLLM exists, and it hides three silent bugs. You have two DGX Sparks. This chapter is the complete roadmap, in eleven steps, for turning that release into a reproducible recipe up to the standard of the lab’s three recipes: every flag with a reason, every patch auditable, every number tagged as measured, estimate or to measure.

The method: eleven steps, one exit criterion each

In the previous chapters you took apart three finished recipes (GLM, Qwen and DeepSeek) and learned to measure what they deliver. Now the problem flips: the model came out yesterday, nobody has written the recipe, and the official instructions were designed for H200 and B200, never for a GB10. Where do you start?

The answer is a method. There are eleven steps with the same shape: goal → concept → how to do it → applied to the model → exit criterion. The exit criterion is the part that matters most. It is a verifiable artifact (a table, a log line, a green test), and without it you do not move on. That is what separates a reproducible recipe from a “works on my machine”.

Notice that four entire steps happen before any docker run. It is not bureaucracy: the download is ~175 GB and a cold boot takes about 11 minutes [measured: tonyd2wild]. Finding out on paper that something does not fit, or that a kernel does not exist for your hardware, is orders of magnitude cheaper than finding out in the log.

Three tags for every number

The lab’s recipes follow a hard rule: every published number is a measurement, not a guess. The tutorial this chapter is based on tags every value with one of three labels, and you should do the same in your README:

[measured: X]
read from a log or benchmark published by X, on the stated hardware;
[estimate]
computed from config.json and the safetensors headers. Good for planning, and it must be replaced by the value measured on your kit;
[to measure]
nobody has published it yet: that is your job.

Why this model is a good teaching case

The target is two GB10s (sm_121, 128 GB of unified LPDDR5X per node at ~273 GB/s), of which CUDA sees 121.69 GiB [measured: the lab’s Qwen3.8 README], linked by ConnectX-7 with 200 Gb/s RoCE. In practice ~160–170 GiB of weights fit in the pair. Among the August and September 2026 releases, MiMo-V2.6-Flash-MOPD was chosen for five reasons:

🧮

A tight fit

It has ~161 GiB of weights for 2 × 121.7 GiB. That leaves ~15–20 GiB of KV per rank, which forces you to do the memory math properly. A single Spark cannot run the native format.

🧩

It breaks assumptions

K and V with different dimensions (192 × 128), KV heads that change with the layer type (8 in the SWA layers, 4 in the global ones), sinks only in the SWA layers, QKV pre-sliced for TP=4 and MXFP4 experts with E8M0 scales.

🐛

Day-0 with silent bugs

--kv-cache-dtype fp8 ignored without a warning; QKV zeroed out by certain loaders; speculative verify on a kernel that is slow at long context. All of them with an open PR.

🔁

MOPD, not RL

MOPD and RL are two post-training variants of the same base model. RL is the checkpoint that came out of reinforcement learning; MOPD is that same checkpoint with one more stage of on-policy distillation from several specialized teachers (the card describes the new version as Multi-Prefix Multi-Teacher On-Policy Distillation). Architecture, config.json (byte-for-byte identical) and drafter are the same; only the weights change. The extra stage was done to mitigate repeated tool calls, the defect that shows up most with agents.

Step 0 — Triage: read the model before you download it

Goal. Before downloading 170 GB, find out what is different about the model and where the engine is going to break.

Concept. A checkpoint describes itself in three layers. (1) The card gives the intent and the official commands. (2) The config.json gives the geometry, which determines memory, parallelism and kernels. (3) The safetensors headers give the real dtypes and shapes, which sometimes contradict the config. In MiMo, for example, quant_method: fp8 coexists with MXFP4 experts.

The trick that makes triage cheap is the file format (seen in Checkpoints). A .safetensors file starts with 8 bytes forming an unsigned 64-bit little-endian integer holding the size \(N\) of the header. Next come \(N\) bytes of JSON with, for each tensor, dtype, shape and data_offsets. Only then comes the data. An HTTP range request asks for just one slice of the file, and with two of them you read the “table of contents” of a ~1.3 GB shard without downloading it.

# 0.1 Metadata: find the current commit and PIN IT for the rest of the triage
REPO=XiaomiMiMo/MiMo-V2.6-Flash-MOPD

  | jq '[.[]|.size] | add / 1e9'            # ~177.8 GB in the repo

# 0.2 Small files, at the same revision and with the path preserved
mkdir -p triage/dflash
for f in config.json generation_config.json chat_template.jinja tokenizer_config.json dflash/config.json; do
  curl -sfL "https://huggingface.co/$REPO/raw/$REV/$f" -o "triage/$f" || echo "FAILED: $f"
done
python3 -c "import json; [json.load(open(p)) for p in ('triage/config.json', 'triage/dflash/config.json')]; print('JSON ok')"

# 0.3 Safetensors headers (real dtypes/shapes), still at the same revision
python3 triage.py $REPO $REV

The first call only serves to discover the commit. From then on, everything uses $REV: 2479e2d0… is the MOPD commit from 2026-09-27, and a “main” checkpoint changes underneath you. Two details of the loop are worth their weight in gold. The -o "triage/$f" preserves the subdirectory; with basename, dflash/config.json would overwrite the model’s config.json, and Step 1’s mem_budget.py would die with a KeyError. And curl -f turns a 404 into an error, instead of saving the error page as if it were JSON. The validation reads both configs because the RL release’s dflash/config.json shipped with a stray comma [measured: tonyd2wild]. triage.py (tested against MOPD) does the rest:

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""Triage of a Hub checkpoint without downloading the weights: config, index and headers via range request."""
import collections, json, re, struct, sys, urllib.request
REPO = sys.argv[1]; REV = sys.argv[2] if len(sys.argv) > 2 else "main"
BASE = f"https://huggingface.co/{REPO}/resolve/{REV}"

def get(url, rng=None):
    req = urllib.request.Request(url, headers={"Range": f"bytes={rng}"} if rng else {})
    with urllib.request.urlopen(req, timeout=120) as r:
        return r.read()

def header(fname):
    n = struct.unpack("<Q", get(f"{BASE}/{fname}", "0-7"))[0]      # 8 bytes: size N of the JSON
    return json.loads(get(f"{BASE}/{fname}", f"8-{8 + n - 1}"))      # N bytes: the shard's table of contents

cfg = json.loads(get(f"{BASE}/config.json"))
idx = json.loads(get(f"{BASE}/model.safetensors.index.json")); wm = idx["weight_map"]
print("arch:", cfg["architectures"], "| index:", idx.get("metadata"), "| tensors:", len(wm))
pat = cfg.get("hybrid_layer_pattern") or []
print("global:", [i for i, x in enumerate(pat) if x == 0], "| SWA:", pat.count(1))
q = cfg.get("quantization_config", {})
print("quant:", {k: v for k, v in q.items() if k != "ignored_layers"},
      collections.Counter(re.sub(r"\.\d+\.", ".N.", x) for x in q.get("ignored_layers", [])))
wanted = ["model.layers.0.self_attn.qkv_proj.weight", "model.layers.1.self_attn.qkv_proj.weight",
          "model.layers.1.mlp.experts.0.gate_proj.weight", "model.mtp.layers.0.self_attn.qkv_proj.weight"]
for f in dict.fromkeys(wm[t] for t in wanted if t in wm):          # each shard only once
    for k, v in header(f).items():
        m = re.match(r"model\.(layers\.[01]|mtp\.layers\.0)\.", k)
        if k != "__metadata__" and m and (".experts." not in k or ".experts.0." in k):
            print(f"  {k:66s} {v['dtype']:8s} {v['shape']}")
  • get() sends the HTTP header Range: bytes=a-b; the Hub responds with just that slice.
  • header() reads in two stages: struct.unpack("<Q") decodes the first 8 bytes as a little-endian uint64 (< = little-endian, Q = unsigned 64-bit), and the second request brings back exactly the JSON.
  • The index’s weight_map says which file each tensor lives in. Four probe tensors are enough: layer 0 (global, dense MLP), layer 1 (SWA + MoE), one expert and the MTP.
  • The Counter with the regex \.\d+\. → .N. collapses the 49 ignored_layers into two patterns, instead of printing 49 lines.

Notice what the figure proves. The QKV of the global layers has 13,568 rows because it is the concatenation of four slices of \(16 \times 192 = 3072\) rows of Q, \(1 \times 192\) of K and \(1 \times 128\) of V. That is pre-sharding for TP=4 (the index’s tp_size: 4). The U8 shape [2048, 2048] of an expert only makes sense once you remember that each byte holds two 4-bit E2M1 values: the logical matrix is \(2048 \times 4096\), and the E8M0 scale every 32 values gives the 128 scale bytes per row (FP8, FP4 & microscaling). The MTP’s QKV has the shape of the SWA QKV, so the MTP is an SWA-type layer, with a sink.

Step 1 — The memory math

Goal. Know, before launching, how much is left for KV and, from that, how much context and how many concurrent requests fit.

Concept. On the GB10 memory is unified (Unified memory): the operating system, the page cache, Docker and vLLM all compete for the same 128 GB. --gpu-memory-utilization (GMU) is a fraction of the 121.69 GiB that CUDA sees, and vLLM splits that budget like this (PagedAttention & KV):

\[ \underbrace{\mathrm{GMU}\cdot M_{\text{vis}}}_{\text{budget}} \;=\; W_{\text{rank}} \;+\; O \;+\; P_{\text{KV}} \]
  • \(M_{\text{vis}}\): memory visible to CUDA per node (121.69 GiB on the GB10)
  • \(W_{\text{rank}}\): weights loaded per rank
  • \(O\): peak activations + “non-torch” memory + CUDA graphs
  • \(P_{\text{KV}}\): the KV pool, which is whatever is left, shared by all requests

KV: what grows and what is fixed

A hybrid model has two kinds of KV cost (Prefill, decode & KV, Sparse attention). The 9 global layers store K and V for the entire context; the 39 SWA layers store only the window. Per rank, with \(s\) bytes per element:

\[ b_{\text{GA}} = L_{\text{GA}}\cdot\tfrac{H^{\text{GA}}_{kv}}{TP}\cdot(d_k+d_v)\cdot s = 9\cdot 2\cdot 320\cdot s \qquad b_{\text{SWA}} = 39\cdot 4\cdot 320\cdot s \] \[ \text{req}(L) \;=\; L\cdot b_{\text{GA}}\cdot\rho \;+\; b_{\text{SWA}}\cdot B\!\left(\left\lceil \tfrac{W+k}{B}\right\rceil+1\right) \;+\; b_{\text{draft}}\cdot B\!\left(\left\lceil \tfrac{W_d+k}{B}\right\rceil+1\right), \qquad \rho=\frac{\lceil L_{\text{GA}}/g\rceil\,g}{L_{\text{GA}}} \]
  • \(b_{\text{GA}}\): bytes/token/rank that grow with the context: 11,520 B in BF16, 5,760 B in FP8
  • \(b_{\text{SWA}}\): 99,840 B/token (BF16) or 49,920 B (FP8), but only for the window
  • \(b_{\text{draft}}\): the KV of the DFlash drafter, 5 SWA layers with 8 KV heads (4 per rank) of 128 + 128: \(5\cdot 4\cdot 256\cdot 2 = 10{,}240\) B/token in BF16, with window \(W_d = 1024\)
  • \(L\): tokens in the request; \(W = 128\): window; \(k\): speculative tokens; \(B = 64\): tokens per block
  • the \(+1\) covers the partial block: a sliding window of \(W+k\) tokens rarely starts on a block boundary, so it can touch \(\lceil (W+k)/B\rceil + 1\) blocks at the same time
  • \(\rho\): padding of the hybrid allocator; groups of \(g=5\) layers → 9 global layers take up 10 “slots”: \(\rho = 10/9\), +11%

With k = 7, the SWA part costs \(49{,}920 \times 64 \times (\lceil 135/64\rceil + 1) = 49{,}920 \times 256\) ≈ 12.8 MB per request in FP8, fixed. The drafter adds another fixed cost: \(10{,}240 \times 64 \times (\lceil 1031/64\rceil + 1) = 10{,}240 \times 1152\) ≈ 11.8 MB [estimate], in BF16, as the source tutorial assumes for the GB10 (if your build puts the drafter’s KV in FP8, it is half that; check the log). Together, ~24.6 MB fixed per request: the drafter nearly doubles the fixed cost. The global part costs 6,400 B per token (5,760 × 10/9). Two consequences: the cost that grows with context is small (for comparison, 13.3 KB/token for Qwen3.8 at TP2 BF16, and 656 B per MLA layer for GLM), and the allocator wastes ~11% of the global pages. PR #58175 measured +10.57% capacity by switching to groups of 3 (1,606,781 → 1,776,588 tokens, MiMo BF16 TP2).

The mem_budget.py script does this math from the config.json itself:

#!/usr/bin/env python3
"""KV budget per rank. Usage:
python3 mem_budget.py triage/config.json --draft triage/dflash/config.json --tp 2 --kv fp8 --gmu 0.85"""
import argparse, json, math
ap = argparse.ArgumentParser(); ap.add_argument("config")
ap.add_argument("--draft", help="dflash/config.json: adds the drafter's fixed KV")
ap.add_argument("--draft-kv", choices=["bf16", "fp8"], default="bf16")
ap.add_argument("--tp", type=int, default=2); ap.add_argument("--kv", choices=["bf16", "fp8"], default="fp8")
ap.add_argument("--gmu", type=float, default=0.85); ap.add_argument("--visible-gib", type=float, default=121.69)
ap.add_argument("--weights-gib", type=float, default=82.7); ap.add_argument("--overhead-gib", type=float, default=4.0)
ap.add_argument("--group-size", type=int, default=5); ap.add_argument("--block", type=int, default=64)
ap.add_argument("--spec-k", type=int, default=7)
ap.add_argument("--ctx", type=int, nargs="*", default=[32768, 131072, 262144, 1048576])
a = ap.parse_args(); c = json.load(open(a.config)); b = 2 if a.kv == "bf16" else 1
blocks = lambda win: (math.ceil((win + a.spec_k) / a.block) + 1) * a.block   # window + k, with the partial block
pat = c["hybrid_layer_pattern"]; n_ga, n_swa = pat.count(0), pat.count(1)
ga_tok = n_ga * (c["num_key_value_heads"] // a.tp) * (c["head_dim"] + c["v_head_dim"]) * b
swa_tok = n_swa * (c["swa_num_key_value_heads"] // a.tp) * (c["swa_head_dim"] + c["swa_v_head_dim"]) * b
swa_req = swa_tok * blocks(c["sliding_window"]); draft_req = 0
if a.draft:
    d = json.load(open(a.draft)); bd = 2 if a.draft_kv == "bf16" else 1
    d_tok = d["num_hidden_layers"] * (d["num_key_value_heads"] // a.tp) * (d["head_dim"] + d.get("v_head_dim", d["head_dim"])) * bd
    draft_req = d_tok * blocks(d.get("sliding_window") or 1024)
pad = math.ceil(n_ga / a.group_size) * a.group_size / n_ga
kv_gib = a.visible_gib * a.gmu - a.weights_gib - a.overhead_gib
print(f"global KV {ga_tok} B/tok/rank (x{pad:.2f} padding) | fixed/req: SWA {swa_req/1e6:.1f} MB"
      f" + drafter {draft_req/1e6:.1f} MB | pool {kv_gib:.2f} GiB")
cap = kv_gib * 2**30 / (ga_tok * pad); print(f"capacity ~{cap/1e6:.2f} M tokens")
for L in a.ctx:
    print(f"  ctx {L:>8}: {kv_gib*2**30/(L*ga_tok*pad + swa_req + draft_req):6.2f} full requests")

Read the script as the equation above written in Python: ga_tok is \(b_{\text{GA}}\), blocks() is \(B(\lceil (W+k)/B\rceil+1)\), swa_req and draft_req are the two fixed terms, pad is \(\rho\) and kv_gib is \(P_{\text{KV}}\). --draft reads Step 0’s triage/dflash/config.json (which is why the path had to be preserved); since the drafter is a qwen3 with a single head_dim (128 for K and V) and no v_head_dim, the script reuses head_dim for V. The weights go in as measured (82.7 GiB) and the overhead as an initial guess (4 GiB) that you recalibrate from the log. In the calculator below, the same code runs live.

Three golden rules of the GB10

1. The log rules. Read Available KV cache memory and GPU KV cache size and recalibrate --overhead-gib.

2. Leave headroom for the host. The API process, the multimodal processor (the default cache is 4 GiB of RAM; use --mm-processor-cache-gb 1), the page cache and the OS all use the same memory. Keep MemAvailable ≥ 4–6 GiB at peak and release the checkpoint’s page cache with posix_fadvise(DONTNEED) before booting.

3. vLLM refuses to start if, at startup, free memory is less than GMU × total. With GMU 0.90 you need 109.5 GiB free. That is why the community uses 0.80–0.85 and, after the first boot, pins the pool with --kv-cache-memory <bytes>.

Step 2 — Quantization and checkpoint

Goal. Choose the weights file with the best balance of quality, memory and speed for this hardware, and have a way to measure the loss.

Concept. In an MoE whose decode is bandwidth-bound, two things matter (Quantization fundamentals): the bytes read per token (the active experts plus everything that is dense) and the error relative to the reference, measured as KLD between the next-token distributions. Quantizing it yourself, and measuring

If the file is not the official one (or if you produced one yourself with llm-compressor, nvidia-modelopt, exllamav3 or llama-quantize), you owe a KLD measurement. The crucial detail: the reference has to run on the same engine. There is no public BF16 of MiMo and HF cannot run 173 GB on a Spark, so serve the official one in vLLM and compare the candidate against it through prompt logprobs (Measuring quality):

\[ D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q) \;=\; \sum_{t\in \mathcal{T}_k} \tilde P(t)\,\big(\log \tilde P(t) - \log Q(t)\big), \qquad \tilde P(t) = \frac{P(t)}{\sum_{u\in\mathcal{T}_k} P(u)} \]
  • \(P\): the next-token distribution in the reference (official)
  • \(Q\): the candidate’s, at the same position of the same text
  • \(\mathcal{T}_k\): the reference’s top-\(k\) tokens (\(k=20\))
  • \(\tilde P\): \(P\) renormalized on that support; tokens missing from \(Q\) get a floor
# kld_topk.py — approximate KLD (top-k support) between two vLLM servers with the same tokenizer
import json, math, sys, requests
REF, CAND, MODEL_REF, MODEL_CAND, K = sys.argv[1], sys.argv[2], sys.argv[3], sys.argv[4], 20
docs = [json.loads(l)["text"] for l in open("kld_corpus.jsonl")]   # e.g. 64 windows of 2048 tokens

def plp(url, model, text):
    r = requests.post(f"{url}/v1/completions", json={"model": model, "prompt": text, "max_tokens": 1,
                      "temperature": 0, "prompt_logprobs": K}, timeout=600).json()
    return r["choices"][0]["prompt_logprobs"][1:]            # position 0 has no distribution

tot, n, top1 = 0.0, 0, 0
for d in docs:
    for p, q in zip(plp(REF, MODEL_REF, d), plp(CAND, MODEL_CAND, d)):
        P = {t: v["logprob"] for t, v in p.items()}; Q = {t: v["logprob"] for t, v in q.items()}
        zP = math.log(sum(math.exp(v) for v in P.values()))   # renormalize on the top-k support
        kl = sum(math.exp(lp - zP) * ((lp - zP) - Q.get(t, min(Q.values()) - 2.0)) for t, lp in P.items())
        tot += kl; n += 1; top1 += max(P, key=P.get) == max(Q, key=Q.get)
print(f"mean KLD ≈ {tot/n:.4f} nats | top-1 agreement {top1/n:.3%} | {n} positions")

The trick is prompt_logprobs: with max_tokens: 1, vLLM returns, for every position in the prompt, the \(k\) largest log-probabilities. Each document in the corpus turns into thousands of distribution samples. The kl line is the equation above; the floor min(Q) − 2 avoids \(\log 0\) when the candidate does not have that token in its top-k. Limits: top-k KLD underestimates the real one, the token key depends on the version (ID or string), and the corpus has to be exactly the same on both sides.

The reference scale

The GLM recipe’s KLD panel measured the served 4 bpw EXL3 at 0.0246 nats, practically the same as that stack’s official FP8 (0.0246), with 54% of the bytes; NVFP4 came in at 0.061. A reasonable heuristic: < 0.03 great, 0.03–0.06 acceptable, > 0.1 shows up in use. Always confirm with task evals (GSM8K, HumanEval+, needle).

Step 3 — Topology (TP, EP, PP)

Goal. Decide how to split the model across the two GB10s (Parallelism, NCCL & RDMA).

  • TP slices every matrix. It requires 2 all-reduces per layer (attention and MoE), each sensitive to link latency (8 KB per token in decode), and it demands divisibility: heads % TP, KV heads % TP (or replication), moe_intermediate/TP a multiple of the quantization block.
  • EP puts whole experts on each rank and exchanges tokens via all-to-all or allgather/reduce-scatter. Without a shared expert, the load depends on routing: with few tokens (decode, batch 1), one rank can get 6 of the 8 experts.
  • PP cuts the layers into slices (24/24). It only passes activations, but at batch 1 one node sits idle while the other works. It is for throughput with many streams, not for latency.

Everything divides. The only “almost ✘” is the pre-sharding: the checkpoint was saved sliced for TP=4, and you are going to run TP=2. The figure shows what that means, row by row, in the QKV.

Look at the amber blocks in the global layers. Each chunk has 3072 + 192 + 128 = 3392 rows, which is not a multiple of 128: scale block 25 covers the last 64 rows of K and the first 64 of V, and block 26 is half empty. A 128-row block has a single F32 factor, so when the rank’s [Q | K | V] layout joins rows from different source blocks, there is no checkpoint scale that works. The stock loader solves this the expensive way: it dequantizes and requantizes each rank’s QKV rows. In this checkpoint, at TP2, that changes 18% of the Q weights, ~51% of the K weights and 73–81% of the V weights in the global layers, with a relative L2 error of 1.3–2.5% [measured: diffbot]. Even Q, which aligns, changes. The likely explanation: requantizing recomputes each block’s scale from the dequantized values, and it need not match the one that was stored [interpretation].

The exact path (PR #59053, VLLM_MIMO_EXACT_QKV=1) skips that computation. In the global layers, it keeps each chunk’s K and V in the checkpoint’s scales, in a zero-padded layout [Q… | K V pad | …]: 192 + 128 + 64 = 384 rows, three exact blocks, which reuse the original blocks 24, 25 and 26. The forward splits K and V out of that layout. In the SWA layers, loading is a pure block permutation. The weights stay bit-for-bit identical to the checkpoint’s, and the price is ~12K fewer tokens in the KV pool because of the padding [measured: diffbot]. The SWA layers also have another problem, one of logic rather than scale: each chunk carries 2 KV heads, and the “1 chunk per KV head” assumption broke the sharding (#57508).

Decision: TP=2 (one GPU per node, --nnodes 2), with EP as an A/B variable in Step 9. tonyd2wild runs TP2 without EP, diffbot uses EP off as the default and Smilehyu (SGLang) runs TP2/EP2. PP stays out because it doubles decode latency at small batch sizes, and spec decode support under PP is new (#50514 in v0.30).

Communication cost [estimate]: there are 96 all-reduces per decode step (48 layers × 2), each ~15–30 µs over RoCE. That adds up to 1.5–3 ms in a ~25 ms step (~10%). If NCCL_DEBUG=INFO shows NET/Socket instead of NET/IB, RoCE is not being used and that cost explodes; fix NCCL_IB_HCA and NCCL_IB_GID_INDEX as in the Qwen recipe.

Three mechanisms behind the table

Why DiffKV means Triton (G1, G7). vLLM has two backends for K and V of different sizes. FLASH_ATTN_DIFFKV needs FlashAttention 3/4, which does not exist on sm_121, so all of the target’s attention falls to TRITON_ATTN_DIFFKV. That is why both attention patches are Triton files.

Why verify gets slow (G7). At long context, decode attention is split along the KV (split-KV): several thread blocks (CTAs) read different slices of the context in parallel and then combine the results. The stock kernel turns split-KV off when max_seqlen_q > 1, and an 8-token verify (1 + 7 drafts) ran on only 18 CTAs, with almost the whole GPU idle. diffbot’s patch keeps split-KV in verify and covers the whole verify in one program (BLOCK_M = q_len × 16): decode went from 87–94 to 156–175 tok/s at 46K [measured: diffbot, SM120].

Why DFlash needs a marker (G4). DFlash does not draft from tokens alone: it consumes the hidden states of 5 target layers (target_layer_ids [0, 11, 23, 35, 47]), and vLLM delivers those auxiliary states through the EAGLE3 interface. The SupportsEagle3 marker declares that the class knows how to expose them. In the omni class the hooks already delegated to the inner language model; only the marker was missing [measured: tonyd2wild, patch 02].

The lab’s pattern is the overlay: a bind-mount of a .py generated from the image’s own file, with no rebuild.

The generator is fail-closed (it aborts if the anchor does not match), idempotent and able to detect when the fix has already landed upstream:

#!/usr/bin/env python3
import re, sys
src, dst = sys.argv[1], sys.argv[2]
s = open(src).read()
MARK = "# [mimo-recipe] cache_config per layer"
if MARK in s:                                                   # idempotent
    open(dst, "w").write(s); print("[overlay] already applied"); sys.exit(0)
if re.search(r"cache_config\s*=\s*layer_cache_config", s):     # the new image already fixed it
    open(dst, "w").write(s); print("[overlay] already upstream"); sys.exit(0)
# Anchor 1: start of MiMoV2FlashDecoderLayer's __init__
a1 = "        quant_config = vllm_config.quant_config\n        layer_id = extract_layer_index(prefix)\n"
if s.count(a1) != 1:
    sys.exit("[overlay] anchor 1 not found exactly 1x — the image changed; review the patch")
s = s.replace(a1, a1 + f"        {MARK}\n        import copy\n"
    "        layer_cache_config = copy.copy(vllm_config.cache_config)\n"
    "        if layer_cache_config is not None:\n"
    "            layer_cache_config.sliding_window = None  # global layers stay global\n")
# Anchor 2: both MiMoV2Attention constructions receive cache_config
n = s.count("                quant_config=quant_config,\n                partial_rotary_factor=")
if n != 2:
    sys.exit(f"[overlay] expected 2 MiMoV2Attention constructions, found {n}")
s = s.replace("                quant_config=quant_config,\n                partial_rotary_factor=",
              "                quant_config=quant_config,\n                cache_config=layer_cache_config,\n"
              "                partial_rotary_factor=")
open(dst, "w").write(s); print("[overlay] cache_config forwarded (2 layer types)")

Read the defenses one by one. MARK makes the script idempotent: running it twice does not apply it twice. The “already upstream” regex makes the patch retire itself when the new image brings the fix. The anchors are exact strings with a required count (1 and 2): if the image changed, the script dies with a clear message instead of producing a half-patched file. And the fix itself is subtle: forwarding the shared cache_config would turn on FP8, but it would also carry the model-level sliding_window: 128 into the global layers. That is why the patch uses a copy with sliding_window=None. The figure shows the four possible states.

The four states tell a story. Upstream, FP8 is silently ignored: you think you have twice the KV and you do not. With the naive fix, FP8 works and the global layers start seeing only 128 tokens: the needle fails, and nothing in the log warns you. With overlay 1 alone, the boot fails, because the DiffKV Triton backend still rejects FP8. That is the right behavior: fail loudly. Only with overlay 2 (the port of #58128 to triton_attn_diffkv.py and triton_unified_attention_diffkv.py) do you reach the good state. To port it, download the diff (curl -L https://github.com/vllm-project/vllm/pull/58128.diff), run patch --dry-run against the files extracted from the image and, if it does not apply cleanly (the PR is against main, the image is dev449), redo it by hand with anchors. Never copy files from main into the image: mixed versions break imports in subtle ways.

Rules the three recipes follow

The overlay logs a marker at boot ([glm53-kpool-tail-seed-stride], [overlay] …) so you can check it with docker logs | grep; it exits with already upstream when the new image already has the fix; it is mounted on both ranks, with the same bytes; and it has a CPU test (tests/test_<patch>.py) that runs without a GPU, like ’s scripts/ci-validate.sh. The recipes go further than the example above: they pin the SHA-256 of the source file, validate the output with ast.parse, publish with os.replace (atomic) and keep a fixed order when several patches touch the same file (GLM53_OVERLAY_ORDER in ).

4.4 Truncated parity against HF

Running the whole model in HF is impossible on a Spark. The technique is truncated parity: comparing a model with the same first \(N\) layers on both sides.

  1. Choose N = 2. Layer 0 is global with a dense MLP and layer 1 is SWA with MoE: together they cover every block type. In BF16 that takes ~13 GB in HF (it is one layer’s 256 dequantized experts).
  2. vLLM: --hf-overrides '{"num_hidden_layers": 2}' --enforce-eager, without a speculator; TP=1 on one node and then TP=2 across nodes, because TP=2 is where G2 and G3 live. It needs the guard from #58184 (or an equivalent overlay) to skip the QKV of layers 2–47. The generated text is garbage, and that is expected: what you compare are the logprobs.
  3. HF: AutoModelForCausalLM.from_config(cfg_truncado, trust_remote_code=True, torch_dtype=bfloat16) with attn_implementation="eager" (HF falls back to eager when there are sinks) and a dequantized state dict for layers 0–1, using the formulas below.
  4. Metric: 32 prompts of 512 tokens, top-20 KLD per position and top-1 agreement. Reference [heuristic]: mean KLD < 1e-3 and top-1 > 99% at TP1; at TP2, the same value as at TP1. If TP2 diverges from TP1, the problem is in the sharding (G2/G3), not in the kernel.
  5. Kernel tests from the image, with the overlay mounted: pytest tests/kernels/attention/test_triton_unified_attention_diffkv.py (the cases that require FA3/FA4 show up as skipped on the GB10).
\[ W_{\text{FP8}}[i,j] = q[i,j]\cdot s_{\lfloor i/128\rfloor,\,\lfloor j/128\rfloor} \qquad\qquad W_{\text{MXFP4}}[i,j] = \mathrm{E2M1}\big(n[i,j]\big)\cdot 2^{\,e_{i,\lfloor j/32\rfloor}-127} \]
  • \(q\): stored E4M3 value; \(s\): F32 weight_scale_inv of the 128×128 block (respecting the QKV’s 4 chunks, 27 row blocks in GA and 29 in SWA)
  • \(n\): 4-bit nibble; E2M1 maps 0…15 to [0, .5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 6, −0, −.5, …, −6]
  • \(e\): the scale’s E8M0 byte, one every 32 values
  • The “low nibble = even element” convention (OCP/gpt-oss) has to be confirmed against the kernel’s GEMM
Exit criterion

The overlays pass the CPU tests; truncated parity TP1 = TP2 ≈ HF within the threshold; the full boot logs every marker; G1 is proven by the 2× pool and G2 by the nonzero QKV sum.

Step 6 — Speculative decoding

Goal. Choose the speculator and the k that maximize tok/s on your workload, measuring acceptance (Speculation: fundamentals, DFlash & DSpark).

Concept. The drafter proposes k tokens and the target verifies all of them in one pass. Each step emits the accepted drafts plus one token from the target itself (the correction, or the bonus when everything passes), and the gain is

\[ \text{tok/s} \;=\; \frac{1 + \bar a(k)}{t_{\text{step}}(k)}, \qquad \bar a(k) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \alpha^{i} \;\;\text{(geometric acceptance, simplified model)} \]
  • \(\bar a(k)\): accepted drafts per step, on average
  • \(\alpha\): probability of accepting each position, if positions were independent
  • \(t_{\text{step}}(k)\): cost of the step. It has a part that rises with k (bigger verify, bigger capture sizes) and, with DFlash, a nearly fixed part: the drafter drafts the whole block of 8 in one pass, whatever k is used

Acceptance depends on the type of text: very high for JSON, counting and code, low for prose; temperature 1.0 lowers it compared to 0. MiMo has two built-in options: DFlash (k from 1 to 7; the block of 8 is the one it was trained with) and MTP (k from 1 to 3). ngram is experimental. Measure with vLLM V1’s Prometheus counters, read as a difference before and after each arm:

m() { curl -s http://$HEAD_IP:8888/metrics | grep -E "^vllm:spec_decode_num_(drafts|draft_tokens|accepted_tokens)_total" ; }
m > before.txt; python3 bench/run_category.py --category code --n 20; m > after.txt
# accepted tokens per step = Δaccepted / Δdrafts ; per-token rate = Δaccepted / Δdraft_tokens
curl -s http://$HEAD_IP:8888/metrics | grep accepted_tokens_per_pos    # per-position curve (decays with position)

The published answer key [measured: tonyd2wild, vLLM TP2, DFlash k=7, FP8 KV, temperature 0, C=1] gives, in accepted tokens per step (out of 7): structured 6.85, code 5.23, math 5.00, JSON 4.00, reasoning 2.62, prose 1.23; and, in tok/s per stream, code 70.5, prose 25.9, average 53.3. The simulator below uses those points to calibrate a simple model and ask: what if k were different?

Change the category and watch the optimal k move. For code and structured text the model prefers k = 7: almost everything is accepted, and each expensive step yields 6 to 8 tokens. The measurement mostly agrees: on tonyd2wild’s TP4, k = 4 was worse than k = 7 in math (82 vs. 95 tok/s), structured text (78 vs. 115) and counting (84 vs. 136), and tied in code within the noise (86 vs. 83) [measured: tonyd2wild]. For prose, with \(c_d = 0\), the model says something else: it predicts a gain with small k (optimum at k ≈ 2). The measurement did not see that gain: on the same TP4, k = 4 did not improve prose (28 vs. 35 tok/s at k = 7). That is a sign that the step cost has a fixed part the linear model ignores, because DFlash drafts the block of 8 all at once. Raise \(c_d\) and watch the small-k gain for prose shrink until it disappears. Treat “k ≈ 2 for prose” as a hypothesis [to measure], not as a recommendation. SGLang’s MTP vs. DFlash comparison (MTP won on prose, 35.0 vs. 25.8 tok/s, and lost on code, 46.0 vs. 69.8 [measured: MiaAI-Lab]) uses a different drafter and does not validate the effect of k; it only shows that, for prose, the drafter matters more than k. The likely interpretation of the part that grows with k: in an MoE, verifying k + 1 tokens wakes up more distinct experts per layer than verifying 1.

Also look at the capture sizes band. The list 8 16 … 64 is perfect for k = 7 and bad for any other k: with k = 3, a batch of 3 sequences has 12 tokens and gets “padded” to 16. fell into the worse version of this mistake: with 6 sequences × 7 tokens = 42, the default cap truncated the grid at 40 and the 42-token step ran eager (−12% in the c=6 aggregate), until the cap was rounded up to 48.

Protocol: a no-spec baseline per category (code, JSON, prose, reasoning, summarization) at C=1; DFlash k ∈ {3, 5, 7} and MTP k ∈ {1, 2, 3} with the same prompt set, at temperature 0 and 1.0; repeat at 32K and 128K (the cost of verify at long context depends on G7); choose by the weighted average of your workload. A lab that serves coding agents weights code and JSON.

Goal. Reproducible numbers for TTFT and TPOT, and tok/s on the concurrency ladder and in prefill, with the method published alongside (Benchmarking). Before measuring, work out where you should land: prefill is compute-bound, and decode without spec is bandwidth-bound (Inside the GPU). Each decode step reads, per rank, the active experts and everything that is dense:

\[ \text{bytes/step/rank} \approx \underbrace{2.51}_{\text{8 experts}\times 47,\ \div 2} + \underbrace{1.44}_{\text{qkv FP8}} + \underbrace{1.61}_{o\_proj\ \text{BF16}} + \underbrace{0.63}_{lm\_head} + \underbrace{0.2}_{\text{router, dense MLP}} \approx 6.4\ \text{GB} \;\Rightarrow\; \text{tok/s} \le \frac{273\ \text{GB/s}}{6.4\ \text{GB}} \approx 43 \]
  • All [estimate] from the shapes; TP divides almost everything by 2
  • With o_proj in FP8 (#59083) the total drops to ~5.6 GB and the ceiling rises to ~49 tok/s
  • In practice expect 50–65% of the ceiling, and then multiply by the spec effect

The figure turns that math into a conveyor belt: a step’s bytes travel from the LPDDR5X to the GPU at the speed of the bandwidth, and the time the belt takes is the minimum time for the step.

Step 9 — Iterative tuning

Goal. Improve a declared target without breaking the rest, with a decision rule written before the experiment (Benchmarking). The rule, declared in docs/ before measuring:

  1. One variable at a time, in an A/B/A design (baseline → change → baseline, in the same boot or in paired boots), with ≥ 3 runs per arm and the median.
  2. Accept if the target improves ≥ 3% on both sides of the A/B/A, no secondary metric gets worse by more than 2%, the quality canary stays within the noise (GSM8K-200 ± 3 questions; needle 100%; tools suite 100%) and there is no OOM in the worst case.
  3. Reject and record the negative result in the CHANGELOG, as tonyd2wild did with k=4 and with MNBT 8192.
  4. Changes that affect memory (KV dtype, GMU, MNBT) rerun the OOM test.

Why “on both sides”? Because the system drifts: the page cache warms up, autotuning finishes, the chip’s temperature rises. In the figure, turn on drift and zero out the real effect: the naive A/B “discovers” an improvement that does not exist.

.env pitfalls

A .env is sourced by the shell: quotes vanish, and a JSON like {"image":8} arrives broken. That is why accepts image=N and builds the JSON inside the container, and the start.sh above builds --speculative-config from SPEC_K. also normalizes BOM and CRLF in a 0600 copy before reading it. Document the precedence, because each recipe decides it differently. In the .env beats the environment (KV_CACHE_DTYPE=fp8 ./start.sh is ignored if the .env defines the key), except for ABLIT and HF_TOKEN, and the start-fp8.sh and start-v030.sh lanes use OVERRIDE_* variables read after the .env. In it is the opposite: whatever the caller exports, even empty, beats the .env, except ABLIT, which is forced to 0 after the .env. Pick one rule, write it in the README and make the launch summary print the effective value of each key.

start.sh does, in this order: (1) doctor: ssh to the worker, the image on both nodes with the same digest, RoCE active, GPU idle, MemAvailable, disk, earlyoom off; (2) download + verify on the head and sync (rsync or read-only NFS); (3) extracts the files from the image, checks the sha256 and generates the overlays, aborting if an anchor fails; (4) refuses forbidden combinations before starting (KV_CACHE_DTYPE=fp8 without the overlay, SPEC_K > 7 with DFlash or > 3 with MTP, capture sizes that are not multiples of k+1, nonexistent flags); (5) releases the page cache, starts the worker, starts the head and waits for /health with a ~20 min timeout (the cold boot takes ~11 min with mmap [measured: tonyd2wild]); (6) prints the KV lines from the log and runs smoke.sh.

Finally, the documentation: a “Status” section as a table, with measurements only and an explicit “Not measured”; a “Why the overlay exists” section for each patch (symptom, cause, upstream PR, how to remove it once upstream absorbs it); and credits to every kit an idea came from and to the PR authors. Respect the licenses: MiMo is MIT and vLLM is Apache-2.0, but vendored third-party code has its own license (the Qwen recipe, for example, vendors AGPL-3.0 code and says so).

Beyond MiMo: the challenge, the Mac and the final checklist

Challenge: Naive-N0.5-Flash, a model vLLM does not know yet

Naive-N0.5-Flash (2026-09-27, MIT) has 309B parameters with 15.5B active and derives from the MiMo-V2.5 base: same backbone (48 layers, hidden 4096, 256 experts top-8, 4 KV heads, vocabulary of 152,576). But it replaces the global layers with DeepSeek Sparse Attention (DSA) in a ~5:1 SWA:DSA layout, with no dense global attention at all. The weights are BF16 (617.8 GB) and FP8 (~310 GB), with a Naive-N0.5-Flash-FP8-Draft. It is the “level 2” exercise: Step 4 stops being an overlay and becomes a full implementation.

  • Step 0: NaiveN05FlashForCausalLM, model_type: naive_n05_flash, remote code in modeling_naive_n05_flash.py, and no entry in vLLM’s registry.
  • Step 1: there is no dense global layer anymore. DSA still stores KV and a small indexer key for every token (storage grows with context), but each query reads only the top-k (Sparse attention). Redo the formula per DSA layer from the config: KV heads × dims × bytes + the indexer key.
  • Step 2: not even FP8 fits in 2 Sparks. Quantize the experts (NVFP4 with llm-compressor or ModelOpt, calibrated) and measure the KLD against the official FP8 running on ≥ 3 nodes, or against the truncated HF reference.
  • Step 4: write the model as an out-of-tree plugin, which registers the architecture without a fork. Reuse mimo_v2.py (MoE, SWA with sinks, QKV loader) and swap the global layers for a DSA block. vLLM’s DSA exists for MLA (DeepSeek-V3.2 and V4); DSA with GQA is new work, and on sm_121 the sparse-MLA backends do not work out of the box either. Validate with truncated parity at N = 6, to include at least one DSA layer.
  • Step 6: check whether the FP8-Draft follows a format vLLM already knows (EAGLE3/DFlash) or whether it also needs registering.

The central change is one of mechanism, not format, and it is worth a close look. In a MiMo global layer, each query reads the K and V of all previous tokens. In a DSA layer, a cheap indexer gives each past token a score, using small keys stored separately, and the real attention reads only the top-k.

Notice what does not change: storage. DSA still stores the KV of every token, because any of them might be chosen tomorrow, and it adds the indexer key on top. What it cuts is the read per query, from L to k (plus the scan of the small keys). That is why the Step 1 math has to be redone, not deleted.

The same model on the Mac, with MLX and an OpenAI contract

If you want MiMo running locally on a Mac, behind the same /v1/chat/completions, so that the client only changes its base_url:

  • Memory: the MLX conversions weigh 150–172 GB and load ~164–170 GB; you need a Mac with ≥ 192 GB of unified memory (in practice, a 256 GB Mac Studio M3 Ultra or an M5 Ultra). On a 256 GB M3 Ultra: decode at 59.4 tok/s, prompt processing at 563 tok/s at 2K and a 164.3 GB peak [measured: Vontra]. It is the same ceiling math as Step 8, but without TP: a single device reads all of the step’s active bytes, ~12.8 GB (twice the 6.4 GB per rank), and ~800 GB/s ÷ ~12.8 GB ≈ 60 tok/s [estimate], vs. ~43 on the pair of Sparks. The bandwidth is ~3× higher, but the bytes per device double, and the ceiling gain comes out at ~1.5×.
  • Engine: support is in mlx-lm PR #1219 (branch kernelpool/mlx-lm@add-mimo-v2), not merged as of 2026-09-28.
  • Contract differences: the mlx-community conversion is text-only and runs neither MTP nor DFlash; thinking comes on (pass enable_thinking=False via chat_template_kwargs, and give a generous max_tokens: the mlx_lm.server default is 512); tool calls use the Qwen3-Coder format (the qwen3_coder parser); the reasoning field in the stream is called reasoning, not reasoning_content. Test with the same suite as Step 7.
  • MOPD: there is no mlx-community conversion yet (only for RL); converting with mlx_lm.convert from the same branch is the way, [to measure]. 128 GB Macs only work with expert pruning (REAP50 MLX, ~95 GB), with quality loss [to measure]; for them, a smaller model is usually the more honest choice.
