There are two ways to remember a conversation. In the first, you write every word down in a notebook and, to answer, you reread the whole notebook. That is the KV cache of softmax attention: it gains one line per token and never stops growing. In the second, you keep a fixed-size whiteboard. At every word, you check what the board already said, erase a little, correct the error and write over it. You never reread anything; you only consult the board. This second way is linear attention, and it fills 34 of the 45 layers of GLM-5.3-Flash and 36 of the 48 of Qwen3.8-Flash-Next. In this chapter you'll build that whiteboard from scratch (a 128×128 matrix per head) and meet the delta rule that keeps it clean, the gates that decide what to forget and the two ways of doing the same math: token by token in decode, in blocks in prefill. By the end it will be clear why vLLM treats this state as a separate object, with blocks of 1,600 or 3,584 tokens and aligned checkpoints, and why TensorFold, the engine behind the second GLM recipe, does the same math with different choices: a hand-written chunkwise kernel, states kept on a 64-token grid and two copies of the state to undo drafts.

The notebook that never stops growing

Recall softmax attention. Each new token projects a query \(q_t\), compares that query with the keys \(k_s\) of all the previous tokens and returns a weighted average of the values \(v_s\):

\[ o_t = \sum_{s \le t} \mathrm{softmax}_s\!\left(\frac{q_t \cdot k_s}{\sqrt{d}}\right) v_s \]
  • \(q_t, k_s, v_s \in \mathbb{R}^{d}\): query of the current token, key and value of token \(s\) (one head)
  • \(\mathrm{softmax}_s\): normalizes the scores over all \(s \le t\) (causal mask)
  • \(o_t\): output of the head for token \(t\)

To compute this during decode, the model must have kept \(k_s\) and \(v_s\) for every \(s \le t\). That's the KV cache, with one line per token per layer, and every new token costs \(O(n\cdot d)\) operations and memory reads. In prefill, with \(n\) tokens at once, the score table has \(n \times n\) entries: cost \(O(n^2)\). The previous chapters already showed two ways out. One is to compress each line of the notebook (MLA stores a latent of 512 numbers instead of full K and V). The other is to read fewer lines (sparse attention picks 2,048 of them with an indexer). The third way out is more radical: store no lines at all.

Why this matters

At 1,048,576 tokens, a single GLM MLA layer stores 656 B × 1,048,576 ≈ 688 MB of KV, while the neighboring KDA layer stays at 2.34 MB, about 290 times less. With 34 layers of that kind, GLM carries 76 MiB of state per request per rank, whether the prompt has 10 thousand tokens or 1 million.

Without softmax, attention becomes an RNN

The whole trick fits in one line of algebra. Remove the softmax and keep just the dot product. The weighted sum becomes

\[ o_t = \sum_{s\le t} (q_t^\top k_s)\, v_s = \Big(\sum_{s\le t} k_s v_s^\top\Big)^{\!\top} q_t = S_t^\top q_t, \qquad S_t = S_{t-1} + k_t v_t^\top \]
  • \(S_t \in \mathbb{R}^{d_k \times d_v}\): the state, a sum of outer products \(k_s v_s^\top\); it has the same size for any \(t\)
  • \(k_t v_t^\top\): outer product (column × row), a rank-1 matrix
  • \(S_t^\top q_t\): the read, a matrix-vector product
  • \(d_k, d_v\): key and value dimensions per head (128 and 128 in both recipes)

The change is only one of order. Instead of first comparing the query with each key (the \(QK^\top\) table, \(n\times n\)) and then mixing the values, we first sum the key-value pairs into a \(d_k\times d_v\) matrix and then query it. The associativity of the matrix product, \((QK^\top)V = Q(K^\top V)\), only works because the softmax is no longer in the middle. Without it, attention becomes a recurrent network: a state that passes from token to token, receives a write and accepts a read.

It's worth looking at this state as an associative memory. Writing \(k v^\top\) records “when someone asks for \(k\), return \(v\)”, and reading \(S^\top q\) asks exactly that question. If the keys are orthonormal, reading with \(q = k_i\) returns \(v_i\) with no error. That's why the literature calls \(S\) fast weights: it is a weight matrix written by the data itself, during inference, while the “slow” weights (\(W_Q, W_K, W_V\)) have been frozen since training. The stepper below does these computations by hand, with \(d = 4\) and numbers you can check.

The last step shows the weakness. Since \(k_3\) has a piece in the direction of \(k_1\) (\(k_3 \cdot k_1 = 0.6\)), asking for \(k_1\) brings back \(v_1\) contaminated by \(0.6\,v_3\). A \(128\times128\) matrix has at most 128 orthogonal directions per head. Beyond that, every new write leaks into the old ones. That's the price of swapping an unbounded notebook for a fixed-size whiteboard, and the next two sections deal with it.

In exchange, the cost becomes constant. In decode, each token costs \(O(d_k d_v)\) per head, whether it's the tenth or the millionth. In prefill the total is \(O(n\, d_k d_v)\) instead of \(O(n^2 d)\), linear in the length, and that's where the name comes from.

Deep dive: so where did the softmax go?

The classic formulation of linear attention (2020) replaced \(\exp(q\cdot k)\) with a product of feature maps \(\phi(q)^\top\phi(k)\) (for example \(\phi = \mathrm{elu}+1\), always positive) and kept a denominator \(\sum_s \phi(q_t)^\top\phi(k_s)\) to imitate the normalization. The modern variants used here, DeltaNet, Gated DeltaNet and KDA, drop the denominator. They normalize \(q\) and \(k\) by their L2 norm and apply an RMSNorm to the output of each head. What is really lost is the softmax's selectivity, the ability to single out one token among millions. That's why real models aren't 100% linear, as the section on hybrids shows.

Forgetting to fit: decay gates

With the plain sum, the whiteboard only accumulates. After a million tokens, all 128 directions of every head are full of leftovers, and nothing that went in at the start ever comes out. The first fix is to let the board fade: before writing, multiply the state by a factor \(\alpha_t \in (0,1)\).

\[ S_t = \alpha_t\, S_{t-1} + k_t v_t^\top \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \text{weight of token } s \text{ at time } t = \prod_{r=s+1}^{t} \alpha_r, \qquad t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{-\ln \alpha} \]
  • \(\alpha_t\): forget factor of step \(t\) (1 = forgets nothing, close to 0 = wipes everything)
  • \(\prod \alpha_r\): how much of token \(s\)'s write is left after \(t - s\) steps
  • \(t_{1/2}\): half-life in tokens, when \(\alpha\) is constant

The idea gets powerful when \(\alpha_t\) depends on the data: it becomes a gate computed from the current token (the same kind of multiplication by a number between 0 and 1 you saw in the neural networks chapter). Faced with a “new document” or a change of subject, the model can wipe almost everything at once. Faced with continuous text, it can retain things for thousands of tokens. There are two granularities:

  • One scalar per head (Mamba-2, Gated DeltaNet): the whole 128×128 state fades together.
  • One value per channel (GLA, KDA): \(\mathrm{Diag}(\alpha_t)\) multiplies each row of the state (each key channel) by its own factor. Some channels become long-term memory, others very short-term memory, inside the same head.

Both recipes parameterize the gate with names inherited from state space models (SSM), the Mamba family. In a continuous SSM, the state decays as \(\dot S = -A\,S\). Discretized with step \(\Delta\), this becomes \(S_t = e^{-\Delta A} S_{t-1}\), and the gate is precisely \(\alpha = e^{-\Delta A}\). Hence the names A_log (the logarithm of \(A\), so that \(A = e^{A\_log}\) is always positive) and dt_bias (the bias of the step \(\Delta t\)). That's also why, in vLLM, these layers use the “Mamba” infrastructure.

Qwen · Gated DeltaNet (scalar per head)

\(\alpha_t = \exp\!\big(-e^{A\_log}\cdot \mathrm{softplus}(a_t + dt\_bias)\big)\), with \(a_t = W_a x_t \in \mathbb{R}^{48}\) (in_proj_a [48, 2560]) and A_log, dt_bias [48]. One factor per value head, with no floor: \(\alpha\) can get close to 0.

GLM · KDA (per channel, with a floor)

\(g_t = -5\cdot\sigma\!\big(e^{A\_log}\cdot(f_b(f_a(x_t)) + dt\_bias)\big) \in (-5, 0)^{128}\) per head and \(\alpha_t = e^{g_t} \in (0.0067,\ 1)\). A_log [64] FP32, dt_bias [8192] FP32; \(f_a\) [128, 4096] and \(f_b\) [8192, 128] form a rank-128 bottleneck.

The gate floor (“safe gate”)

In GLM, linear_attn_config.gate_lower_bound = −5.0 limits \(g\) to \((-5, 0)\). In a single step, the state loses at most \(1 - e^{-5} \approx 99.3\%\) of each channel, and there is no “infinite” forgetting. vLLM's code calls this path the safe gate. The FlashKDA kernel requires a bounded gate and refuses to run without lower_bound. Interpretation: the floor also limits the dynamic range of the products of \(\alpha\) accumulated inside a chunk (see the section on the two forms of computation), which helps the numerical stability of the parallel form.

The floor becomes a design number

In the GLM·TF recipe, served by the TensorFold engine, the floor shows up explicitly in the comments of the chunkwise kernel. Since each row's log-decay \(\ln\alpha\) lies between −5 and 0, a channel's cumulative sum \(G = \sum \ln\alpha\) never goes below −160 in a 32-row block, or below −320 in a 64-row one. That −320 is what made the first version of the kernel lose precision. The final version uses 32-row blocks and only multiplies decays (see the section on TensorFold). The interpretation above gets its numbers there.

The delta rule: write by correcting the error

Decay solves the build-up, but it's a general cleanup: it erases a bit of everything. Think of the fact “the capital is Rio” followed, later, by “the capital is Brasilia”. With the plain sum, the whiteboard starts answering “Rio + Brasilia”. With decay, it answers a mixture in which Rio weighs less, and it also fades everything else that had nothing to do with capitals. The right move would be to edit only the association of the key “capital”. That's what the delta rule (DeltaNet) does:

\[ \hat v_t = S_{t-1}^\top k_t, \qquad S_t = S_{t-1} + \beta_t\, k_t\,(v_t - \hat v_t)^\top = (I - \beta_t k_t k_t^\top)\,S_{t-1} + \beta_t\, k_t v_t^\top \]
  • \(\hat v_t\): what the whiteboard already answers for the key \(k_t\) (the prediction)
  • \(v_t - \hat v_t\): the error, and only the error gets written
  • \(\beta_t \in (0,1)\): write strength (0 = ignore the token, 1 = overwrite the whole association), \(\beta_t = \sigma(W_b x_t)\), one value per head
  • \(I - \beta_t k_t k_t^\top\): erases the old component in the direction \(k_t\) and leaves the orthogonal directions intact

There are two readings of this equation, and both help. The first is about optimization. The update is exactly one gradient-descent step (see the learning from data chapter) on the loss \(\mathcal{L}_t(S) = \tfrac12\lVert S^\top k_t - v_t\rVert^2\), with learning rate \(\beta_t\), because \(\nabla_S \mathcal{L}_t = k_t (S^\top k_t - v_t)^\top\). The state is “trained” token by token during inference to return the right values for the keys it has seen. (This reading, sometimes called test-time training, is a common interpretation in the literature, not something the recipes claim.) The second is geometric. With \(\lVert k_t\rVert = 1\), the matrix \(I - \beta k k^\top\) is a generalized Householder transformation. It shrinks the state in the direction \(k\) by the factor \(1-\beta\) and doesn't touch any perpendicular direction. With \(\beta = 1\), the old association of \(k\) disappears entirely and the new one takes its place.

This explains a detail that shows up in both recipes: \(q\) and \(k\) are normalized by their L2 norm (L2Norm in Qwen's GDN, use_qk_l2norm_in_kernel=True in GLM's KDA). The eigenvalue of \(I - \beta k k^\top\) in the direction \(k\) is \(1 - \beta\lVert k\rVert^2\). If the norm of \(k\) were free, \(\beta\lVert k\rVert^2 > 2\) would be enough for that factor to go past −1 and for the state to blow up over thousands of steps. With \(\lVert k\rVert = 1\) and \(\beta\in(0,1)\), the update is always a safe contraction.

In the lab below, four tokens record facts on a 4×4 whiteboard: “capital → Rio”, “currency → Real”, then the correction “capital → Brasilia” and, finally, “language → Portuguese”, whose key overlaps 60% with that of “capital”. Compare the plain sum with the delta rule, and play with \(\beta\).

With the delta rule and \(\beta = 1\), the capital correction is perfect: the whiteboard now answers Brasilia. The last token, however, spoils the capital a little, because the key of “language” isn't orthogonal to that of “capital”. The delta rule guarantees that the most recent association comes out exact and pushes the error onto the keys that overlap with it. With the plain sum, everything turns into a mixture. With a small \(\beta\), the whiteboard learns slowly: each fact has to show up more times.

Gated DeltaNet: general cleanup and precision eraser

The two mechanisms complement each other. Decay is good for switching context (forgetting everything that's old), and the delta rule is good for editing a specific fact. Gated DeltaNet combines both, and KDA (Kimi Delta Attention) swaps the scalar factor for a per-channel factor:

\[ \text{GDN:}\ \ S_t = \alpha_t\,(I - \beta_t k_t k_t^\top)\,S_{t-1} + \beta_t k_t v_t^\top \qquad\qquad \text{KDA:}\ \ S_t = (I - \beta_t k_t k_t^\top)\,\mathrm{Diag}(\alpha_t)\,S_{t-1} + \beta_t k_t v_t^\top \]
  • \(\alpha_t\) (GDN): scalar per value head; \(\alpha_t \in (0,1)^{d_k}\) (KDA): one factor per key channel, applied to the rows of \(S\)
  • read in both cases: \(o_t = S_t^\top q_t\)
Analogy

Decay is the board eraser swept lightly over the whole whiteboard after every class. The delta rule is the precision eraser: before writing “Brasilia”, you erase exactly the “Rio” that was in that spot. A good teacher uses both.

Qwen3.8: Gated DeltaNet with more value heads than key heads

Qwen's GDN has a curious asymmetry: 16 query/key heads and 48 value heads, all with 128 dimensions. Each QK head is repeated three times and serves three value heads, and each value head has its own 128×128 state. The inventory calls this GVA, the mirror image of GQA: there, several queries share one K/V; here, several states share one Q/K. The per-request state is sized by the 48 value heads: 48 × 128 × 128 × 4 B = 3 MiB per layer in FP32, 1.5 MiB per GPU on TP2. The output goes through \(W_o[\sigma(W_z x) \odot \mathrm{RMSNorm}(y)]\). The model's technical report showed that the sigmoid gate (output_gate_type: sigmoid) works better than the original GDN's SiLU.

The 48 × 128 × 128 state costs 3 MiB per layer in FP32 (1.5 MiB/GPU on TP2), and the CUDA decode kernel requires head_k_dim = 128. GLM-5.3: Kimi Delta Attention with a per-channel gate

GLM's KDA has 64 heads of 128 dimensions for \(q\), \(k\) and \(v\) (q_proj/k_proj/v_proj [8192, 4096] each), a per-head \(\beta\) (b_proj [64, 4096]), the per-channel forget gate in a rank-128 bottleneck (f_a_proj [128, 4096] → f_b_proj [8192, 128]) and a sigmoid output gate, also rank 128 (g_a_proj/g_b_proj), applied after a per-head RMSNorm (o_norm [128]). vLLM fuses six of these projections into a single GEMM, in_proj_qkvbfg_a [24896, 4096] = 3·8192 + 64 + 128 + 128, or [12576, 4096] per rank on TP2, with \(f_a\) and \(g_a\) replicated. That's 137,732,288 parameters per KDA layer. The recipe's repository doesn't expand the acronym. “Kimi Delta Attention” comes from vLLM's code, from the kernel names (chunk_kda_*, “the same kernel as Kimi-K3” in the FlashKDA patch) and from the Kimi Linear paper.

The short causal convolution: very short-term memory

Before entering the recurrence, \(q\), \(k\) and \(v\) go through a short causal convolution, one per channel (depthwise), with 4 weights, followed by SiLU:

\[ y_t[c] = \mathrm{SiLU}\Big(\sum_{j=0}^{K-1} w[c, j]\; x_{t - j\cdot \delta}[c]\Big), \qquad K = 4 \]
  • \(c\): channel (each channel has its own \(K\) weights, without mixing channels)
  • \(K\): kernel size (linear_conv_kernel_dim = 4 in Qwen, short_conv_kernel_size = 4 in GLM)
  • \(\delta\): dilation (1 in GDN/KDA; 3 in Qwen's N-gram conv)
  • causal: only \(x_t\) and the past come in, never the future

Why does this help? The recurrence sees one token at a time. The convolution lets each key “carry” a small piece of the three previous tokens, something like a local n-gram, which makes the associations more specific. Interpretation: it plays the same role as the “previous token” features that the induction heads of Transformers have to build. It also provides a sense of order. In GLM, whose MLA layers have no RoPE (NoPE), position information comes precisely from the causal recurrence and from this convolution, and from a small 4-slot absolute positional embedding (APE) inside the indexer's key pool (see position and RoPE).

The serving cost is a second per-sequence state, the conv_state. To process token \(t\) in decode, you have to remember the last \(K-1\) input values of each channel. With speculative decoding several tokens are verified at once, and the width grows to \(K - 1 + \text{num\_spec}\) (on rollback, num_accepted_tokens says how many columns are valid). On the conveyor belt below, the 4-tap window slides over the tokens and the “backpack” holds what needs to be remembered.

With the real numbers: in GLM (TP2, DFlash2 with k = 7), the conv_state of a KDA layer is [3·8192/2 = 12,288 channels, 4 − 1 + 7 = 10 columns] in BF16, or 245,760 B per request per rank. In Qwen's GDN (TP2, MTP 3) it's [5120, 6] in BF16, about 60 KiB per layer (inventory figure). The N-gram (PLE) conv has kernel 4 and dilation 3, state [10240, 9] plus 3 speculative columns, and registers in vLLM as a Mamba layer of type SHORT_CONV, replicated across ranks (details in N-gram and MTP). GLM's DFlash2 drafter uses another variant, a 2-tap dynamic convolution (DFlash2 and DSpark).

Two forms of the same computation: recurrent and chunkwise

The equation \(S_t = f(S_{t-1}, x_t)\) suggests a loop: token 1, token 2, token 3… That's the recurrent form, perfect for decode, where there is only one new token per sequence. Each step reads the state (128 × 128 × 4 B = 64 KiB per head in FP32), does a few matrix-vector products and writes the state back. The work is small and the bottleneck is moving the state. In Qwen on TP1 that's ~108 MiB of GDN state per sequence, read and written at every step, which the .env.sample sums up as “~0.23 GB per sequence per step”.

In prefill this form is a disaster. A 100-thousand-token prompt would become 100 thousand sequential steps, with no parallelism across tokens and no tensor cores (which thrive on multiplying matrices, not vectors). The chunkwise form solves this. The sequence is cut into blocks of \(C\) tokens (64 in the kernels used by Qwen, according to the inventory). Inside each block, everything is computed in parallel, with matrix multiplications, like a small masked attention. Between blocks, the state is passed once per block. For the sum with decay, the output of block \(c\) is the contribution of the incoming state plus a triangular \(C\times C\) “attention” among the block's tokens. For the delta rule there's a complication: the product of the block's \(C\) matrices \((I - \beta k k^\top)\). It is compacted with the WY representation:

\[ \prod_{i \in c}\big(I - \beta_i k_i k_i^\top\big) = I - K_c^\top W_c, \qquad W_c = \big(I + \mathrm{tril}(\mathrm{diag}(\beta)\,K_c K_c^\top,\,-1)\big)^{-1}\mathrm{diag}(\beta)\,K_c \]
  • \(K_c \in \mathbb{R}^{C\times d_k}\): the block's keys, one per row
  • \(\mathrm{tril}(\cdot, -1)\): strictly lower-triangular part (causality inside the block)
  • the inverse is of a triangular \(C\times C\) matrix with unit diagonal (the “UT transform”), cheap for \(C = 64\)
  • with this, the state leaving the block and the outputs of the \(C\) tokens become a few matrix multiplications

The total cost ends up at \(O(n\,C\,d + n\,d^2)\) per head, linear in \(n\) and with the tensor cores busy. With \(C = 64\), a 131,072-token prefill has 2,048 sequential stages instead of 131,072. In the figure, toggle between the two forms.

Notice that both forms give the same result (up to rounding). Only the order of the operations changes. That's why each linear layer in the recipes has two kernels: one for prefill, another for decode, and sometimes a third to verify speculative tokens.

3:1 hybrids: the best of both worlds

If linear attention is so cheap, why not use it in every layer? Because the whiteboard has 128 × 128 numbers per head, and there's no way to store in it, literally, a phone number that appeared 400 thousand tokens ago. Exact retrieval tasks (copying a passage, finding a needle in a haystack, following cross-references) need the notebook, that is, softmax attention over the original keys. The solution in both recipes is a hybrid model: most layers are linear, cheap and fixed-state, and one in every four layers is a “real” attention layer, which can look back precisely.

  • full_attention_interval: 4. layer_types has full_attention when \((i+1) \bmod 4 = 0\): layers 3, 7, …, 47. That's 36 GDN + 12 QSA, plus one MTP layer that is also QSA, i.e., 13 layers with per-token KV. The technical report shows the GDN hybrid beating the pure Transformer on 8 of 9 benchmarks.
  • the KDA, KDA, KDA, DSA pattern repeated 11 times (DSA on layers 3, 7, …, 43) plus layer 44 (KDA): 34 linear_attention + 11 deepseek_sparse_attention, and the MTP (layer 45) is also DSA. It's the Kimi Linear recipe (3 KDA : 1 MLA, MLA without position), whose paper reports up to 75% less KV and 6× faster decode at 1M compared with MLA on every layer.
  • DeepSeek-V4-Flash is the counterexample: it has no linear layer at all. Its “hybridness” is of another kind. Every layer has a 128-token sliding window, 21 of them add a 1:4 compressed branch with an indexer (C4A) and 20 a 1:128 branch (C128A). The windows are also a fixed cost per sequence (≈ 3.4 MB), but an exact, short one, whereas the linear whiteboard is approximate and covers the whole history.

The map helps you see the savings. If all 45 GLM layers were MLA, the KV would cost 29,520 B per token, 28.8 GiB at 1,048,576 tokens. With classic BF16 MHA it would be 2.95 MB per token, impossible. Served as a hybrid, GLM pays 7,579 B/token per rank (7.40 GiB at 1,048,576 tokens, the same value as the calculator at the end of the chapter) plus the fixed 76 MiB. In Qwen, the 13 attention layers store 26,624 B/token in BF16 (13,312 in FP8) across the whole model. On the same basis as GLM, per GPU on TP2, that's 13,312 B/token in BF16 and 6,656 in FP8. Deep dive: who takes care of position?

In GLM, the MLA layers are NoPE (qk_rope_head_dim: 0) and the config.json doesn't even have rope_theta. None of the text attention layers uses RoPE. According to the model's code, order comes from two places. The first is the KDA layers: the recurrence is causal by construction (the state after token 10 differs from the state after token 11), and the short convolution provides local order. The second is a small 4-slot absolute positional embedding (APE) inside the indexer's key pool. Interpretation, consistent with the Kimi Linear design: the attention layers only need to do lookup, without worrying about distance, which helps generalize to long contexts. Qwen, on the other hand, keeps partial RoPE (64 of 256 dims, θ = 10⁷) in the QSA layers.

Why this matters for serving

A hybrid model has at least two kinds of memory with opposite rules. KV grows with the context and can be cut at any token. State is fixed per request and only exists in the “now”. The inference engine has to allocate, share, free and reuse both at the same time. That's where the KV groups, the giant blocks and half of the recipes' cache patches come from.

Serving state: MambaSpec, align mode and a single page size

In vLLM, any layer that carries a fixed-size state per sequence implements the “Mamba” interface (MambaBase): Mamba, GDN, KDA and even the short conv of Qwen's N-gram. The state is described by a MambaSpec, with the shapes and dtypes of the conv_state and of the recurrent state, and it becomes one or more KV groups in the hybrid manager (the PagedAttention chapter details the allocator). In GLM, the boot log shows 4 MambaSpec groups for the 34 KDA layers, each with layers=9. The math 4 × 9 = 36 doesn't match 34, and it isn't a mistake. Interpretation: vLLM splits the layers into groups of equal size and the last one is left incomplete, so all it takes is 4 × 9 ≥ 34. (Documents from earlier versions of the overlay mention 3 groups, or up to 11 layers per group. Trust your own boot log.) In Qwen, the 36 GDN layers and the PLE conv come in as Mamba layers.

The single page size and the giant blocks

The hybrid allocator uses a single page size for all groups, because all pages come from the same pool. An attention block has page = tokens in the block × bytes per token. A Mamba “page” has to fit a request's entire state for one layer. vLLM then enlarges the attention block until the attention page is ≥ the state page, and rounds up to the kernel alignment. It's the crossover from the first figure, now as the uniform page rule:

\[ B_{\text{attn}} = \Big\lceil \frac{\text{state bytes per layer}}{\text{KV bytes per token per layer} \times a} \Big\rceil \cdot a \]
  • GLM (TP2, k = 7): \(\lceil 2{,}342{,}912 / 656 = 3{,}571.5 \rceil\) rounded up to a multiple of \(a = 256\) → 3,584, and mla_page = 3584 × 656 = 2351104 B (exactly the mla_page=2351104 in the log). On TP3: 1,610,752 / 656 = 2,455 → 2,560.
  • Qwen (TP2, MTP 3): 1,634,304 / 1,024 ≈ 1,596 → 1,600 (BF16 KV, FP32 state); / 512 → 3,200 (FP8 KV); 847,872 / 512 = 1,656 → 1,664 (FP8 KV, BF16 state)
  • \(a\): alignment. In GLM it's 256 (= index_kpool × 64, a requirement of the indexer on SM12x). For Qwen the sources don't give the value, and the data doesn't pin it down: alignments of 16, 32 or 64 tokens reproduce the three measured blocks equally well (1,596 → 1,600, 3,192 → 3,200, 1,656 → 1,664). The calculator uses 16, which is vLLM's default kernel alignment for non-MLA attention (interpretation).

In GLM three different “block sizes” show up, and it's worth telling them apart. The one that governs checkpoints is in the per-group log of the patch_kv_capacity_log.py overlay:

[glm53-kv-capacity-log] group 2: MambaSpec layers=9 block_size=3584 … prefix_caching=yes mamba_cache_mode=align

In align mode, the effective granularity of the state checkpoints is the block_size of the Mamba group itself, and vLLM raises it to the attention block: 3,584. The scheduler aligns the steps to the least common multiple of the blocks of the groups taking part in the cache: LCM(3,584 for MLA, 3,584 for the Mamba groups, 64 for the drafter) = 3,584. That's why every GLM prefix cache hit is a multiple of 3,584 tokens. The “GPU blocks” line in the boot log shows two other numbers. block_size=64 is the MLA kernels' block, not the manager's 3,584 block. mamba_block_size=16 is the configured base granularity of the Mamba cache (--mamba-block-size, which must be a multiple of 8). Interpretation: this base granularity only needs to divide the effective block (3,584 = 16 × 224) and doesn't enter the LCM math, because the group's block has already been raised to 3,584. Neither recipe passes --mamba-block-size. The model forces align and vLLM derives the rest, so this is a knob you only touch if the log shows a Mamba group with a block different from what you expected.

You can't go back to the middle

Now the most important point. The KV of a 1,000-token prefix is simply the first 1,000 lines of the notebook: any cut works. The state after 1,000 tokens is something else. It mixes everything that went in, with factors \(\alpha < 1\) and delta-rule erasures that can't be undone. There's no way to “subtract the last 500 tokens” from a whiteboard. To resume from token \(p\), the state must have been photographed at token \(p\). Hence the modes of --mamba-cache-mode: none (only the current state), all (a snapshot at every block, deprecated in v0.30) and align, which saves the state at the end of each scheduler step, with the steps cut so that they end on block boundaries. Qwen forces align (the "all" mode raises an error), and GLM uses align in its 4 Mamba groups. The stepper walks through the consequences, with GLM's numbers.

Three GLM patches live exactly in these steps:

  1. Chunks that stop at the boundary (patch_mamba_align_chunking.py, overlay #6, applied after fair scheduler v5). The state is only materialized at the end of a chunk. If the chunk crosses the boundary without stopping, the snapshot for that point never exists. The stock code got three things wrong, and v2 fixes all three.
    • Wrong granularity. Stock aligned to cache_config.block_size, which is the smallest page, the drafter's (64 tokens). v2 uses the scheduler's LCM (3,584) for the shared checkpoints and, if there's a private Mamba group with a smaller page, also stops at its next boundary.
    • EAGLE backoff applied to everyone. With EAGLE-style drafters (MTP, DFlash), vLLM deliberately discards the last block that hit in the prefix cache (the “EAGLE block drop”). The reason is that the drafter writes a lookahead token into its own KV, which may not be shared by every request with that prefix. Stock applied this backoff to the alignment of every group. In GLM, only the sliding-window (SWA) group of the DFlash2 drafter is EAGLE, and the MLA and Mamba groups lost a 3,584-token page for nothing. v2 only backs off if some participating group that is not SWA is EAGLE.
    • Grants rounded down to zero. A grant is the token quota the scheduler gives a prefill request in one step. The recipe's fair scheduler v5 is the mixed prefill policy that splits each step between decodes and prefills (details in scheduler and batching). When a prefill competes for the step with decodes, it gets small chunks, on a ladder from 128 to 2,048 tokens. Stock rounded every grant down to a full page boundary, and a 512-token grant with a 3,584 block became 0. The request never advanced. v2 only rounds grants of at least one block, and the smaller ones advance without a snapshot in the middle.
  2. Superseded states that have to go (patch_mamba_align_state_free.py, overlay #11). The manager kept the previous state's block in a single slot, last_state_block_idx. With asynchronous scheduling (the processed prefix lags one batch behind) and 7,168-token chunks crossing 3,584 blocks, the slot was overwritten before the old block could be freed, and the block stayed stuck until the end of the request. The fix replaces the slot with a per-request list, stale_state_block_idxs. The maximum reservation goes from 2 + num_speculative_blocks (9) to 1 + max_concurrent_batches + num_speculative_blocks (10, with 2 batches in flight and 7 speculative blocks).
  3. Hits that respect the state (patch_hybrid_prefix_hit.py and per-group retention, detailed in prefix caching). The hit of a hybrid model is the minimum across groups. Skipping a missing Mamba checkpoint would be a correctness bug, not an optimization. The [glm53-partial-replay-fallback-v1] fallback, when the drafter's window is missing, first tries the previous shared checkpoint. GLM53_APC_RETENTION_INTERVAL keeps a snapshot every N tokens (a multiple of 3,584), which fits more history in the pool and costs recomputation on edits in the middle.
The same problem on another engine

The GLM·TF recipe has no MambaSpec, no uniform page and no align mode, and none of these three patches applies. TensorFold keeps each request's state in its own slot, outside the per-token cache pool, and snapshots prompt states at a few chosen points: at the prompt's last multiple of 64 tokens (if the prompt ends exactly there, the snapshot also carries the logits row, and an identical resend redoes nothing), at the end of the system prompt and at the point where a new prompt stops matching a kept one. The rule “you can only resume where a snapshot was taken” is the same. What changes is the grain (64 instead of 3,584) and the reason for the grain, explained in the section on TensorFold.

State and speculation

In speculative decoding, the target verifies \(k+1\) tokens in one step and may accept only some of them. With KV this is easy: just ignore the rejected lines. With state, the kernel has to keep the states after each of the \(k+1\) tokens, and num_accepted_tokens says which one becomes the official state. In align mode, this shows up as num_speculative_blocks extra pages per request (7 in GLM with DFlash2), and the conv_state gets wider (\(K - 1 + k\) columns). In Qwen, fused_gdn_decode_post_conv_mtp accepts up to 8 speculative tokens, and the recipe's MTP = 3 fits with room to spare.

Two copies instead of k+1 states

TensorFold undoes drafts differently. Each stream keeps its KDA state in two copies, the parities. Verification reads one and writes the other. If a draft is rejected, the engine redoes only the recurrence of the accepted rows from the old copy, with the inputs the window saved (replay). No candidate state per token has to be kept. The figure in the section on TensorFold shows both cases.

The state dtype

Parametersmamba_ssm_dtype: float32 → --mamba-ssm-cache-dtype bfloat16

The checkpoint's config.json asks for the GDN state in FP32. The recipe overrides it with BF16 through the MAMBA_SSM_CACHE_DTYPE variable, which start.sh turns into the flag. This is only possible because the fused GDN kernel accepts BF16: FUSED_GDN_STATE_DTYPES in qwen_gdn_linear_attn.py includes bfloat16, and the fast path stays the same. Effects: the state drops from 1.5 to 0.75 MiB per layer per GPU, the Mamba page is halved and vLLM picks a smaller attention block (3,200 → 1,664 with FP8 KV), which also makes the prefix cache less coarse. Half of the ~0.23 GB/sequence/step no longer has to move. The same reasoning explains why FP8 KV yields 1.70× tokens per GiB, and not 2×. --kv-cache-dtype only shrinks QSA's main KV: QSA's indexer and compressor and the state part (GDN and PLE) stay as they were. On the sibling single-GPU recipe, this change measured +8.5% aggregate decode at 8 streams (151.6 → 164.5 tok/s), +6.8% at 1 stream and 15/15 needles at 32k. On the 2-node kit the gain wasn't measured in isolation. The .env.sample ships bfloat16, but the start.sh fallback is empty (FP32). There is also --mamba-cache-dtype, which changes conv and SSM together, and changing --kv-cache-dtype doesn't touch the state.

Pitfall: the precision of a state that accumulates

The state is updated up to 1 million times in one request, and every update rounds. BF16 has 8 bits of precision (7 of mantissa plus the implicit bit) versus 24 for FP32 (23 + 1) (see numbers in the computer), and rounding errors in a recurrence can accumulate. The checkpoint asks for FP32 for a reason. GLM keeps the recurrent state in FP32 and only the conv_state in BF16. Qwen's 15/15 needles were measured at 32k, not at 262k. If you lower the state dtype, measure at long context (quantized KV cache discusses this trade-off alongside FP8 KV).

For the 34 KDA layers, what changes comes from a contract the engine takes seriously. A reply with drafts must be identical, bit for bit, to serial decoding. That contract reopens three questions the previous section answered the vLLM way.

🧮

How to compute the prefill?

With its own CUDA kernel in WY/UT form, 32-row sub-chunks and the 128×128 state in registers. It stays close to the serial chain, but not on the same bits.

📍

Where to keep the state?

On a 64-token grid. When the prompt ends on the grid, the end snapshot also carries the logits row. vLLM keeps one every 3,584.

↩️

How to undo drafts?

With two copies of the state per stream (parities) and a replay of the accepted rows, instead of one candidate state per verified token.

The chunkwise form, written out

With KDA_CHUNKED=1, the recipe's default, the prefill of the KDA layers swaps the serial chain (one token at a time, the same kernel as decode) for the chunkwise form from the section on the two forms of the computation. The teaching bonus is that every computation is visible, because the kernel (kda_chunk.cu, patch 0039) documents them one by one. The sequence is cut into 32-row sub-chunks at absolute positions (tokens 0–31, 32–63, …). Let \(S_0\) be the state entering a sub-chunk and \(E_c(t\to s) = \alpha_{t+1,c}\cdots\alpha_{s,c}\) the accumulated decay of key channel \(c\) between rows \(t\) and \(s\). In this chapter's convention (\(S\) is \(d_k\times d_v\) and the read is \(o = S^\top q\)), the computations are:

\[ L_{st} = \beta_s \sum_c k_{s,c}\,k_{t,c}\,E_c(t\to s)\ \ (t < s), \qquad M_{st} = \sum_c q_{s,c}\,k_{t,c}\,E_c(t\to s)\ \ (t \le s), \qquad T = (I + L)^{-1} \] \[ V' = T\,\mathrm{diag}(\beta)\big(V - (K\odot E)\,S_0\big), \qquad O = (Q\odot E)\,S_0 + M\,V', \qquad S_{\text{end}} = \mathrm{Diag}(E_{\text{end}})\,S_0 + (K\odot E^{\to})^{\top}\,V' \]
  • \(Q, K, V\): the sub-chunk's rows stacked, one per matrix row (\(q\) and \(k\) already L2-normalized); \(\beta\): each row's write strength
  • \(E\): in row \(s\), the decay from the start of the sub-chunk up to \(s\), inclusive; \(E^{\to}\): from the next row to the end; \(E_{\text{end}}\): the whole sub-chunk
  • \(L\): how much row \(t\)'s write changes what row \(s\)'s key reads (interference between keys, strictly triangular)
  • \(M\): the causal “attention” inside the sub-chunk, already weighted by the decays
  • \(T\): solves the chain of delta-rule corrections (each row's error depends on the corrections of the earlier ones)
  • \(V'\): the errors the delta rule actually writes (the code's “v_new”); \(O\): the rows' outputs; \(S_{\text{end}}\): the state that moves on to the next sub-chunk

It is the WY representation from the section on the two forms, now with the per-channel decays inside the sums. It is worth reading backwards. A row's output has two parts: what the incoming state answers, \((Q\odot E)\,S_0\), and what the sub-chunk's own writes add, \(M V'\). Those writes are not the raw \(v\). The delta rule writes only the error, and row \(s\)'s error depends on what the earlier rows have already written. That is why a triangular system shows up, \((I+L)\,V' = Y\) with \(Y = \mathrm{diag}(\beta)\big(V - (K\odot E)S_0\big)\). Since \(I + L\) is lower triangular with a unit diagonal, the inverse comes out by forward substitution, row by row, with no pivoting and no division. The kernel does it in 16-row blocks.

Only the hand-off of \(S\) from one sub-chunk to the next remains sequential, and the kernel is organized around that. It launches one block of 256 threads per head, which walks the prompt chunk's sub-chunks in order and keeps the 128×128 FP32 state in registers, 64 numbers per thread. While one sub-chunk is being computed, the next one's rows are already being brought into L2, and they move into shared memory as soon as the current sub-chunk frees it. The figure does the same computations with toy numbers (\(d_k = d_v = 4\), 4 to 8 rows) and checks the result against the serial chain.

Measured in the recipe, with patches 0012, 0014 and 0039 together and one boot per configuration: the 50k-token prefill went from 29.3 to 26.4 s, and the 149k one from 91.1 to 84.5 s, 7% to 10% less. Interpretation: the gain is modest for the reason the section on the two forms already pointed out, since the KDA layers are only part of the prefill, next to the MoE, the sparse attention and the dense projections. Decode stays on the serial chain. The chain already had a three-kernel path (prep, step and output, with 8 blocks per head instead of one and the same bits), reserved for windows of 64 rows or more. A separate patch (0016) moved every window to that path, and decode windows of 1 to 8 rows measured 4% to 36% faster on the GB10.

Close to the serial chain, but not the same bits

The serial chain (kda.cu) is the engine's reference. It rounds the output of the convolution with SiLU to BF16, computes the L2 norms in FP32, computes \(\beta = \mathrm{bf16}(\sigma(b))\) and is compiled without FMA contraction, with CUDA's expf and IEEE division. The chunkwise kernel receives exactly those per-row inputs and is compiled the same way. What changes is the order of the sums: matrix products instead of a token-by-token chain. In floating point, addition is not associative (chapter numbers in the computer), so the result stays close to the chain, but not on the same bits. The error against the delta rule computed in float64 is of the same order as the serial chain's own error. The recipe says so. KDA_CHUNKED=1 is one of three defaults that are not exact with respect to the BF16 checkpoint, next to DENSE=q4 and KV=fp8, and DENSE=bf16 KV=bf16 KDA_CHUNKED=0 serves the checkpoint as it is, with a smaller window. The engine's contract still holds because it compares the engine with itself: the drafted and serial versions, or the concurrent and solo ones, run the same prefill and the same decode and come out equal to each other.

Why products of decays, and not \(e^{G_s - G_t}\)

The obvious way to compute \(E(t\to s)\) is to keep the cumulative sum of the log-decays, \(G_s = \sum_{r\le s}\ln\alpha_r\), and take \(e^{G_s - G_t}\). The first version of the kernel, in Triton (patch 0012, 64-row sub-chunks), did exactly that and reached 10 times the error of the serial chain. The culprit is the gate floor. With \(\ln\alpha \in (-5, 0)\), \(G\) reaches −320 in a 64-row sub-chunk, and in FP32 the distance between two neighboring numbers near 320 is \(2^{-15} \approx 3\cdot10^{-5}\). The difference of two large \(G\)'s carries an absolute error of that order into decays close to 1, while each multiplication in the serial chain is off by at most half a unit in the 24th bit (\(\approx 6\cdot10^{-8}\), relative). That version already factored each exponent around a reference row per 16-row block, so that no factor exceeded \(e^{75}\), below FP32's ceiling of \(e^{88.7}\). Patch 0014 fixed the precision: each exponent started being summed outward from the reference, never as the difference of two large \(G\)'s. The final CUDA kernel (0039) went further and only multiplies decays, in row order, the way the serial chain does with the state. Every factor is \(\le 1\) (never \(e^{+x}\)), each decay carries the same rounding as the chain and, with 32 rows, \(G\) only reaches −160. Two hardware details complete the story. The Triton version did the matrix products in tf32x3, about \(2\cdot10^{-6}\) relative error against float64, because plain TF32 (\(\sim 5\cdot10^{-4}\)) would be “half a BF16 step”. And the GB10 gives each block 99 KiB (101,376 B) of shared memory, less than the up to 154 KiB a load pipeline would ask for, so that version's loops ran with a single stage (see inside the GPU).

Where to keep the state: a 64-token grid

The rule from the previous section still holds: a recurrent state can only be resumed where it was snapshotted. What changes is where the snapshots go, and why. In the chunkwise kernel, a row's bits depend only on the rows of its sub-chunk and on the state entering it. If a resumed prefill started at token 10,001, the sub-chunk that runs from 9,984 to 10,015 would be computed from a different incoming state, with different rows, and the bits would change. “Resumed = fresh” would no longer hold. That is why every prompt chunk starts on a multiple of the prompt grid \(G\) (TF_GLM_PROMPT_GRID, which accepts 32, 64 or 128 when KDA_CHUNKED=1: the kernel only needs a multiple of 32, and when unset the engine uses 64), and the prompt state is kept at the grid's last multiple, \(B = \lfloor n/G\rfloor\cdot G\). When the prompt does not end on the grid, the request prefills up to \(B\), keeps the state and resumes from it up to \(n\). That way, any prompt that extends prompt[:B] goes through the same state, fresh or resumed.

Two pieces complete the picture. When the prompt ends exactly on the grid (\(B = n\)), the end snapshot also carries the head's logits row, before any grammar mask. An identical prompt sent again samples its first token from that row and does no prefill at all: a 64k-token prompt goes from 34 s to under 0.07 s. Off the grid, the resend resumes from \(B\) and recomputes only the fewer than 64 final tokens. And, with SHARED_PREFIX=1, a request keeps up to two extra states, at the point where it stops matching a kept prompt and at the end of the system block (the first <|user|>), each on the grid and only if it adds at least 256 tokens. A new conversation with the same 7.9k-token system prompt prefills in 0.13 s instead of 4.24 s. The price is that these states are few and whole. The recipe keeps at most 32 (TF_GLM_CACHE_ENTRIES; plain TensorFold keeps 8): the oldest leaves when the limit is exceeded, and kept states give way first when a live stream needs the pool. vLLM reuses any aligned prefix, from any request, but only every 3,584 tokens.

Undoing drafts: two parities and replay

In speculative decoding the target verifies at once a window with the pending token and the drafts: up to 16 rows per stream, and up to 32 in the batch of up to 4 streams. The previous section showed vLLM's solution, which keeps the state after each verified token and picks one with num_accepted_tokens. TensorFold keeps each stream's KDA state in a slot with two parities, a [count, 2, layers, H, 128, 128] FP32 tensor. The window's chain reads the current parity and writes the other one, so the drafts never touch the already committed state. At commit there are two cases. If all rows were accepted, flipping the parity is enough. If only the first keep rows were accepted, the engine does a replay of them: it redoes the recurrence of those rows, in every layer, from the old parity, using the \(k\), \(v\), \(g\) and \(\beta\) the window saved per row, without rerunning any projection. The convolution window is shifted with the kept rows' projections.

The same machinery serves several requests. A segment table [nseg, 6] (first row, rows, slot, parity, conv slot, keep) cuts the window into pieces, one per stream, and each segment runs from the state of its own slot. According to the code, a segment's bits are those of a solo run of its rows, which is what upholds “concurrent = solo”. Segments with 0 rows are skipped, so a table of the maximum size, captured in a CUDA graph, serves any smaller number of streams.

The trade-off is clear in the figure. vLLM writes one candidate state per verified token at every step (GLM's 7 extra pages per request) and, on rollback, just picks one of them. TensorFold keeps two copies and pays a short replay when some draft is rejected. Interpretation: the replay is cheap because it only redoes the recurrence of a few rows, with no projections or MoE, using inputs the verification window had already saved. Memory per stream is predictable. By the engine's formula, one more slot costs about 177 MiB per rank, counting the two parities of the 34 layers, the convolution window and the scratch of a 16-row window (a computation over the code, not a measurement).

vLLM (GLM recipe)

  • Prefill: Triton chunk_kda_with_fused_gate or FlashKDA on TP3
  • State as a MambaSpec: uniform page, 3,584-token block
  • align checkpoints every 3,584; the hit rounds down
  • Speculation: one state per verified token (7 extra pages)
  • 2, 3 or 4 Sparks (TP4 experimental)

TensorFold (GLM·TF recipe)

  • Prefill: its own CUDA kernel, 32-row sub-chunks, state in registers, sums in a fixed order
  • State in one slot per stream, outside the per-token cache pool
  • Prompt states on the 64 grid; the end one, when it falls on the grid, carries the logits row (replay)
  • Speculation: two parities and replay of the accepted rows
  • Only 2 Sparks (TP=2) and up to 4 streams

Checklist for your next hybrid model

  1. Read the layer pattern. Look in config.json for layer_types, full_attention_interval, linear_attn_config or something like hybrid_layer_pattern. Count the linear and the attention layers, without forgetting MTP and drafters.
  2. Identify the variant. Scalar or per-channel gate? Is there a delta rule (\(\beta\), b_proj/in_proj_b)? Does the gate have a floor? What kernel size and dilation does the short conv have?
  3. Compute the state per rank: (state heads / TP) × \(d_k\) × \(d_v\) × bytes + channels / TP × (\(K-1\) + num_spec) × 2. Multiply by the number of linear layers.
  4. Predict the block: state ÷ KV bytes per token of one attention layer, rounded to the alignment. This is also the prefix cache granularity.
  5. Check the kernels: supported head_dim (128), supported state dtypes, speculative token limit (8 in GDN), prefill backends for your GPU architecture. On sm_121, expect to fall back to Triton.
  6. Divisibility: linear heads (and their per-head vectors, such as A_log) must be divisible by the TP. See TP3 and TP4.
  7. State and cache: confirm the mamba_cache_mode (almost always align), the freeing of states under asynchronous scheduling and the prefix cache behavior before promising “second turn” TTFT.
  8. Look at the engine too. The same questions have different answers on different engines. On a purpose-built engine like TensorFold, ask on which grid the prompt states are kept, whether the prefill kernel reproduces the serial chain's bits and how verification undoes drafts.
