An LLM never writes a whole sentence. It writes one token, rereads everything that exists so far and writes another, dozens of times per second, until it decides to stop. Between the network's last layer and the text you read sits a small, decisive piece: the sampler. It is what turns 154,880 scores (one for each token in GLM's vocabulary) into a single choice. Temperature, top-p, penalties, the seed and “thinking before answering” all live there, along with a good share of the pitfalls the recipes document. In this chapter you will build that sampler piece by piece. Along the way you will understand why the DeepSeek recipe ignores the checkpoint's sampling defaults, why Qwen offers an opt-in mode that costs ~4% in speed to give bit-for-bit identical answers and why a small max_tokens returns content: null. And, since the GLM recipe gained a second version on another engine, TensorFold , you will also meet a sampler built so that the same question always gets the same answer, with or without drafts, alone or in company.

The autoregressive loop: one token at a time

So far we have treated the Transformer as a function: a sequence of tokens goes in, one vector per position comes out. A language model uses only the vector at the last position, and uses it to answer one question: what is the next token? The answer is not a token. It is a probability distribution over the entire vocabulary. Generating text means repeating the question: pick a token from that distribution, append it to the end of the sequence and ask again. This back-and-forth is called autoregressive generation, because each output becomes the input of the next step.

\[ p_\theta(x_1,\dots,x_T \mid \text{prompt}) \;=\; \prod_{t=1}^{T} p_\theta\!\big(x_t \,\big|\, \text{prompt},\, x_{<t}\big) \]
  • \(x_t\): token generated at position \(t\)
  • \(x_{<t}\): all tokens before \(t\) (prompt + whatever has already been generated)
  • \(p_\theta\): the distribution that the network with parameters \(\theta\) assigns to the next token
  • \(T\): how many tokens came out before stopping

The formula is the chain rule of probability, and it is exactly the training objective from What learning is: the model was tuned to maximize \(\log p_\theta(x_t\mid x_{<t})\) over trillions of tokens. At inference the roles flip. Instead of measuring the probability of a given text, we sample a text from it, one token at a time. Notice three things. First, the cost of a step is one forward pass through GLM's 45 layers (48 in Qwen, 43 in DeepSeek), whether the chosen token turns out to be a comma or a hard word. Second, the network itself is deterministic: given the same context, it produces the same logits (with a numerical caveat we will see in the determinism section). All the randomness enters in a single place, the sampler. Third, the loop is sequential by nature, because token \(t+1\) can only be computed after token \(t\) has been chosen.

Why this matters: the bottleneck behind half the recipes

At each step the engine has to read every active weight from memory to produce a single token. The computation over the old tokens is reused through the KV cache (next chapter, Prefill, decode and the KV cache), but the weight reads are not. That is why decode is bound by memory bandwidth, and why speculative decoding (Part VII) is worth so much: it breaks the sequential chain by guessing several tokens and verifying them all in a single forward pass. We will see at the end of this chapter that this verification depends on how we sample.

The loop stops for one of four reasons: the model generates an end token (EOS), the text matches a client stop string, the max_tokens limit is reached or the context runs out. Each model has its own EOS tokens. GLM declares three (<|endoftext|> 154820, <|user|> 154827 and <|observation|> 154829): generating <|user|> is how the assistant “hands the floor back”, and <|observation|> closes a tool call. Qwen uses <|im_end|> (248046) and <|endoftext|> (248044), and DeepSeek uses <｜end▁of▁sentence｜> (id 1).

Analogy: the storyteller with a loaded die

Picture a storyteller who, at every word, rolls a 154,880-sided die whose weights depend on everything they have said so far. They cannot erase anything, only keep going. Everything that follows in this chapter is about how to roll that die: trusting the weights more or less (temperature), taping over the faces that are too unlikely (top-k, top-p, min-p), discouraging repeated faces (penalties) and letting the storyteller draft in a notebook before speaking out loud (reasoning).

From vector to vocabulary: lm_head, logits and softmax

After the last layer and the final RMSNorm, each position carries a vector \(h\in\mathbb{R}^d\), the model's “thought” at that point. It has d = 4096 in GLM and DeepSeek and d = 2560 in Qwen. To turn it into one score per token, the network's last matrix steps in: the lm_head (or unembedding) \(W_U\in\mathbb{R}^{V\times d}\), with one row for each vocabulary token. You already met it in Tokens, vocabulary and embeddings, where we saw its shape and how much it costs to read. Here it goes into action inside the loop, and what matters is what happens to the scores it produces.

\[ z \;=\; W_U\,h \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad z_v \;=\; \langle w_v,\, h\rangle \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^{d} W_U[v,k]\; h_k \]
  • \(z\in\mathbb{R}^V\): the logits, one raw score per token
  • \(w_v\): row \(v\) of the lm_head, the “output vector” of token \(v\)
  • \(h\): final hidden state of the last position
  • \(V\): vocabulary size; \(d\): model width

Each logit is a dot product, a measure of alignment between the thought \(h\) and the token's output vector. A high logit means the model “points” at that token. Logits are real numbers with no fixed scale, and they can be negative. Only the differences between them matter. Notice that nothing in the lm_head “knows” which token is right. The context “2 + 2 =” produces an \(h\) that aligns with the row for “ 4” and barely with the others. In “Once upon a time, a”, “ king” and “ dragon” end up close, and that is where the way we sample will make a difference.

Softmax: from scores to probabilities

To sample we need probabilities: positive numbers that add up to 1. The softmax (the same one used in attention) gets there by exponentiating and normalizing:

\[ p_v = \frac{e^{z_v}}{\sum_{j=1}^{V} e^{z_j}}, \qquad \log p_v = z_v - \underbrace{\log\sum_j e^{z_j}}_{\text{LSE}(z)}, \qquad H(p) = -\sum_v p_v \log p_v \]
  • \(p_v\): probability of token \(v\)
  • \(\text{LSE}\): log-sum-exp, computed by subtracting the largest logit so it does not overflow
  • \(\log p_v\): the logprob, in nats (natural log)
  • \(H\): entropy, the uncertainty of the step; \(e^{H}\) is the “effective number of choices”

Three properties of the softmax will keep coming back throughout the chapter. It preserves order: the largest logit is always the most likely. It is shift-invariant: adding the same constant to every logit changes nothing, which is why kernels subtract the maximum before exponentiating. And it is exponential: a 1-nat difference between two logits becomes a ratio of \(e\approx 2.72\) between their probabilities, and a difference of 5 becomes almost 150×.

In the TensorFold lane: candidates instead of logits

In TensorFold the GLM lm_head is also split by vocabulary between the two Sparks, and each one computes the scores of half of the 154,880 tokens. The difference is what crosses the cable: the ranks do not gather whole logit rows. Each one sends only its best candidates, the top_k + 8 largest (28 by default, because the TensorFold server uses top_k=20 when the request omits the field) or, with top-k off (top_k ≤ 0 in the request), up to 1,024 per row, with the fixed-point masses that the section Update: keyed sampling explains. There are also two heads: drafting uses a 4-bit copy of the lm_head, and verification uses the target's head (FP8 under the default DENSE=q4). Since in this lane drafts only propose, the cheap copy can change acceptance, but never the answer.

Temperature, scale and softcap: shaping the distribution

The sampler's first knob is the temperature \(T\). It divides every logit before the softmax:

\[ p_v(T) = \frac{e^{z_v/T}}{\sum_j e^{z_j/T}} \qquad\quad T\to 0:\; p \to \text{one-hot}(\arg\max z) \qquad T=1:\; p = \text{softmax}(z) \qquad T\to\infty:\; p \to \text{uniform} \]
  • \(T\): temperature (\(T>0\)); in vLLM, \(T=0\) is treated as greedy, with no division
  • \(z_v/T\): dividing by \(T<1\) widens the differences; \(T>1\) shrinks them

Temperature never changes the order of the tokens. It only decides how peaked the distribution is. With a low \(T\), the favorite takes almost everything: the text becomes coherent and predictable, and also repetitive. With a high \(T\) the tail gets a voice, which brings variety and, past a certain point, incoherence. \(T=1\) is special because it is the distribution the model learned: the training cross-entropy fits exactly \(\text{softmax}(z)\). Sampling at \(T=1\) reproduces the model as it was trained, tail included. Play with the figure.

Note how the entropy rises continuously with \(T\) and how the effective number of choices \(e^H\) (“exp H” in the readout) goes from ~1 (greedy) to almost all 10 tokens at \(T=2\). The checkpoints' recommended defaults sit near \(T=1\) and cut the tail with top-p (next section). GLM's generation_config.json ships \(T=1.0\) and top_p=0.95. Qwen's ships \(T=1.0\), top_p=0.95 and top_k=20, and the Qwen card suggests a different profile for non-thinking mode (\(T=0.7\), top_p=0.8, top_k=20, presence_penalty=1.5). The examples in DeepSeek's README use \(T=0.6\) and top_p=0.95. The benchmarks do not all follow the same profile. GLM measures in greedy (tests/bench_decode.py, \(T=0\)). Qwen runs decodebench at \(T=0\) and at \(T=0.8\), but the MTP numbers the recipe quotes are greedy. Greedy is reproducible and, with speculative decoding, gives the highest possible acceptance (last section). DeepSeek measures decode at \(T=0.6\) and top_p 0.95 (scripts/bench-miaai.py and benchmark-0731.py), so the drafter acceptance baked into those numbers already reflects sampling.

Pitfall: temperature applies to every token, syntax included

vLLM samples every token at the request's temperature, including the structural tokens of a tool call. The DeepSeek recipe documents the consequence: DeepSeek-V4's tool calls are DSML markup (<｜DSML｜invoke name=…>, braces, quotes), and at \(T=1\) each syntax token is a full draw that can come out wrong. A single corrupted structural token turns the call into plain text, and a tool benchmark counts it as “no call”. Another engine (ds4) forces \(T=0\) on syntax tokens and samples only the values. In vLLM, the available mitigations are running at \(T=0\), using grammars (structural tags) or writing a logits processor. Details in The API: chat, tools and structured outputs.

Scale and softcap: two cousins of temperature

Some models apply fixed transformations to the logits before the sampler. The two most common ones are an output multiplier \(s\) and a softcap \(c\):

\[ z' \;=\; s\cdot c\,\tanh\!\left(\frac{z}{c}\right) \qquad\qquad \frac{s\,z}{T} = \frac{z}{T/s} \]
  • \(c\): soft ceiling; for \(|z|\ll c\), \(c\tanh(z/c)\approx z\), but no logit goes past \(\pm c\)
  • \(s\): fixed scale; multiplying by \(s\) is the same as using temperature \(T/s\)
  • \(\tanh\): hyperbolic tangent, smooth and monotonic, so it never changes the order of the tokens

The softcap was born in training: it keeps any logit from growing without bound and making the softmax too sharp. Gemma 2, for example, trains with a softcap of 30 on the final logits. The golden rule is that inference must apply exactly the transformations used in training. If the checkpoint was trained with a softcap and the engine ignores it, the distribution being served is a different one, and the model gets worse without any warning.

Cutting the tail: greedy, top-k, top-p and min-p

The vocabulary is huge and almost every token has a tiny probability, but “tiny” times 150 thousand can add up to a lot. If 150 thousand irrelevant tokens have 10−6 each (an illustrative value), the tail adds up to 15%, and one draw in seven lands on something meaningless. Truncation methods cut that tail before the draw and renormalize what is left:

\[ \underbrace{\{\arg\max_v p_v\}}_{\text{greedy}} \quad \underbrace{\text{the } k \text{ largest}}_{\text{top-}k} \quad \underbrace{\min\Big\{S : \textstyle\sum_{v\in S} p_{(v)} \ge p\Big\}}_{\text{top-}p\ (\text{nucleus})} \quad \underbrace{\{v : p_v \ge p_{\min}\cdot \max_j p_j\}}_{\text{min-}p} \]
  • \(p_{(v)}\): probabilities in decreasing order
  • \(k\): fixed size of the set
  • \(p\): minimum cumulative mass (top-p)
  • \(p_{\min}\): fraction of the favorite below which a token is dropped
  • Greedy (\(T=0\)) always picks the favorite. It is deterministic and great for comparisons, but prone to repetition loops. In Qwen's decode bench, the entropy task degenerates into repetition at \(T=0\), and the recipe says to read only the result at \(T=0.8\).
  • Top-k keeps a fixed number of candidates. It is simple, but blind to context: when the model is sure, \(k=20\) still lets 19 bad tokens through, and when it is in doubt the same \(k\) can cut good options.
  • Top-p (nucleus) keeps the smallest set that adds up to \(p\). Its size adapts: 1 or 2 tokens in an obvious context and dozens in an open one.
  • Min-p keeps the tokens with at least \(p_{\min}\) times the probability of the favorite. It adapts too, and it is cheap because it needs no sorting. The Qwen card recommends min_p = 0 (off).

Compare the four in the figure, in two contexts: one where the model is sure and another where it is in doubt. The hatched bar at the end stands for the tail, the remaining ~154 thousand tokens added together.

The central point is that top-p and min-p measure the confidence of the step and top-k does not. That is why modern profiles combine \(T\approx1\) with top-p (and, in Qwen, a top-k of 20 as a safety ceiling). Filters also compose, and the order matters. vLLM currently has two model runners, the part that prepares each step's tensors and calls the sampler (details in Inside vLLM). In the old one, Model Runner V1, the order is: penalties and masks on the logits, then temperature, then min-p, then top-k and top-p, and finally the draw. Model Runner V2, the default since v0.29, is what the DeepSeek recipe and the GLM vLLM lane run. In it, the stretch of code that DeepSeek's hotfix #31 edits shows the sequence min-p, then the reasoning budget (which we will see later), then top-k/top-p and, at the end, the Gumbel-max draw from the next section.

In the TensorFold lane, another order of cuts

TensorFold's sampler cuts top-k, divides by \(T\), cuts top-p and only then applies min-p, as SGLang does (the engine's documentation notes that vLLM applies min-p first). With both cuts on, the same top_p/min_p pair can produce different nuclei in the two engines. In vLLM, top-p measures the mass of a distribution already pruned and renormalized by min-p, so it keeps the same number of tokens or fewer (our own math). With the lane's default (\(T=1\), top_k=20, top_p=0.95, no min-p) the difference does not show up. Ties are also broken explicitly: candidates with the same value are sorted by token id, so that the choice does not depend on the order in which the GPU delivered them.

On the GPU: sorting 150 thousand numbers per step is not free

Top-k and top-p require knowing which tokens are at the top, and a full sort of 154,880 numbers per sequence, at every step, would be expensive. GPU samplers avoid the full sort. FlashInfer's uses rejection-based draws over the probabilities, and vLLM has a Triton kernel, _topk_topp_kernel, that handles only the cuts requested. That kernel takes “top-k on?” and “top-p on?” as constexpr parameters, and each combination compiles a different binary: k only, p only, or k+p. Requesting top_k=0 or top_p=1.0 is how the sampler “turns off” each cut. A greedy request never even calls the kernel. Which of the two paths runs depends on the build: VLLM_USE_FLASHINFER_SAMPLER=1 requests FlashInfer's top-k/top-p path when it is supported (the V2 sampler tests this at boot, with flashinfer_sampler_supported()). In the DeepSeek recipe, which pins that variable to 1, what showed up compiling in the middle of serving was the Triton _topk_topp_kernel, which dispatches whenever the request carries top_k/top_p. That is why it is the kernel the warmup exercises. When in doubt, do not trust the variable's name: look at what compiles.

Where the defaults come from: generation_config

When the client does not send temperature or top_p, someone has to decide. The checkpoint may ship a generation_config.json with the values its authors recommend. vLLM offers three controls. --generation-config auto (the default) reads that file and uses its values as server defaults. --generation-config vllm ignores it and uses vLLM's own neutral defaults (\(T=1\), no top-k or top-p). And --override-generation-config '{…}' overrides specific fields. The precedence is always the same: request field > override > checkpoint generation_config > vLLM default.

GLM · auto

\(T=1.0\) and top_p=0.95 are inherited from the Hub. The README asks the client not to send top_p or temperature: sending top_p=1.0 explicitly overrides the 0.95 and, in the recipe's words, “is worse”. The injected top_p also changes which kernel variant runs, hence the warmup arms.

Qwen · auto

\(T=1.0\), top_p=0.95, top_k=20 come from the checkpoint. The recipe's audit suggests --override-generation-config '{"temperature":0.7}' as a product option (not used), because \(T=1\) lowers speculative acceptance and real chat throughput ends up below what is measured in greedy.

DeepSeek · vllm

--generation-config vllm makes the server not inherit hidden defaults from the checkpoint, and with no override the client controls sampling. The recipe's examples send \(T=0.6\) and top_p=0.95 explicitly. The compose file also pins VLLM_USE_FLASHINFER_SAMPLER=1, which requests FlashInfer's top-k/top-p sampler when supported (see above for why the warmup still warms up the Triton kernel).

Sampling without sampling: the Gumbel-max trick

We have a distribution. How do we draw a token from it on a GPU, for hundreds of sequences at once, without stalling? The textbook way is to compute the cumulative sum of the probabilities, draw a uniform number in [0, 1) and look up where it falls. That requires normalizing (a full softmax), doing a sequential scan and, in PyTorch, torch.multinomial also forces a CPU-GPU synchronization. There is an elegant shortcut, the Gumbel-max trick:

\[ g_v = -\log\!\big(-\log u_v\big),\quad u_v\sim\mathcal{U}(0,1)\ \text{independent} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \arg\max_v\Big(\frac{z_v}{T} + g_v\Big) \;\sim\; \text{softmax}\!\Big(\frac{z}{T}\Big) \]
  • \(g_v\): Gumbel noise, one per token
  • \(u_v\): uniform number in (0, 1)
  • \(\arg\max\): a single reduction, embarrassingly parallel
  • \(\sim\): “has the same distribution as”

In words: add to each logit a random push of the right shape and take the largest. No normalization is needed (the softmax denominator is never computed), no cumulative sum is needed, and it all fits in a kernel that only does “add noise and find the max”. With \(T=0\) the noise disappears and plain argmax is what remains, so greedy is a special case of the same code. Watch the race below. Each round draws new pushes, and the histogram of winners converges to the softmax.

Deep dive: why it works

The standard Gumbel has cumulative distribution \(F(x)=e^{-e^{-x}}\). Token \(i\) wins if \(z_i+g_i\) beats all the others: \(P(i)=\int f(x-z_i)\prod_{j\ne i}F(x-z_j)\,dx\). Substituting \(F\) and integrating, the product of double exponentials merges into a single one and what is left is \(e^{z_i}/\sum_j e^{z_j}\), the softmax. There is an equivalent form without double logarithms: if \(E_v\sim\text{Exp}(1)\), then \(-\log E_v\) is Gumbel, and \(\arg\max_v p_v/E_v\) draws from the same distribution.

The two forms in vLLM. The two model runners use the same math in different clothes. Model Runner V1 samples with the exponential form: its random_sample function generates one \(E_v\sim\text{Exp}(1)\) per token and takes \(\arg\max_v p_v/E_v\) instead of calling torch.multinomial, precisely to avoid synchronizing with the CPU. Model Runner V2, which the DeepSeek recipe and the GLM vLLM lane run, uses the original form: Gumbel-max in a Triton kernel (vllm/v1/worker/gpu/sample/gumbel.py), adding \(g_v\) to the logits already divided by \(T\). In V2, the detail that matters most for reproducibility is where the noise comes from.

Seeds and reproducibility. The noise does not have to come from a sequential generator, which hands out numbers one after another and whose output depends on how many numbers have already been requested. It can be a pure function of (seed, position, token), which is called a counter-based generator. That is how V2 works (the helper GLM vendors, just below, shows the code): each row's seed is derived from (the request's seed, position) with tl.randint(seed, pos), and each token's uniform \(u_v\) comes from tl_rand32 (or tl_rand64) indexed by the token id. There is also an option to compute the noise in FP64, model_config.use_fp64_gumbel, which the runner passes on to the sampler. With noise indexed this way, the same request produces the same noise no matter which other sequences share its batch. That only guarantees identical text if the logits are also bit-for-bit identical, the subject of the determinism section.

In the TensorFold lane: the seed is born from the prompt

TensorFold uses the same Gumbel-max with noise indexed by (seed, position, token), but changes two details that change everything. Without a seed, the seed is not drawn anew for each request: it comes from a SHA-256 hash of the prompt ids, so the same request gets the same answer. That holds for TensorFold v0.6.0, which the GLM·TF recipe still runs; the native engine (1.x) has drawn a fresh seed per request since 1.1.1, records it for replay, and --prompt-seed brings back the prompt key. And the draw runs on the host, in float64, in a fixed order of operations. The section Update: keyed sampling shows why this turns speculative verification into a simple equality test.

With \(T>0\) the seed comes from tl.randint(seed, pos), per request and position, and the uniform is indexed by the candidate id. There is one more numerical detail: in FP32 the noise is computed as −log(−log1p(−u)), and the model's same FP64 option (use_fp64_gumbel) applies here. Next, _cache_draft_logits_kernel stores the realized scores as the “draft logits” \(q\) (−∞ outside the 16 candidates), which rejection sampling needs in order to be lossless. In DeepSeek, besides the V2 sampler, the DSpark speculator calls gumbel_sample in each of the 6 draft iterations, and DeepSeek-V4's own reference implementation (the HF model.py) also samples with Gumbel-max.

Repetition, penalties and knowing when to stop

Left in greedy or at a low temperature, a model can fall into a repetition loop: a sentence repeats, and each repetition makes the next one even more likely, because the context starts to “prove” that this is the text's pattern. Penalties fight this by adjusting the logits of tokens that have already appeared. vLLM implements three:

\[ z_v \leftarrow \begin{cases} z_v / r & z_v>0 \\ z_v\cdot r & z_v\le 0\end{cases}\ \ (\text{if } v \text{ has already appeared}) \qquad z_v \leftarrow z_v - \lambda_{\text{pres}}\,\mathbb{1}[c_v>0] - \lambda_{\text{freq}}\,c_v \]
  • \(r\): repetition_penalty (neutral = 1); applies to tokens from the prompt and from the output
  • \(\lambda_{\text{pres}}\): presence_penalty (neutral = 0), a fixed discount for having appeared at all
  • \(\lambda_{\text{freq}}\): frequency_penalty (neutral = 0), a discount proportional to the count
  • \(c_v\): how many times \(v\) has already come out in the generation

Notice the asymmetry of the repetition penalty. It is multiplicative, so it divides positive logits and multiplies negative ones, pushing both down, but on a scale that depends on the sign. Presence and frequency are additive and look only at the output. In the figure, the model is one step away from repeating “the cat saw”. Raise the penalties and watch the favorite change.

Penalties come at a price. Code repeats variable names, JSON repeats keys, tables repeat digits and tool calls repeat syntax. Punishing every repetition corrupts exactly what needs to be repeated. That is why none of the three recipes turns penalties on at the server, and the Qwen card keeps them at 0 in thinking mode (suggesting presence_penalty=1.5 only in non-thinking mode). A real loop is usually a symptom of something else: a temperature that is too low, a wrong template or a numerical bug. Before any other suspicion, the DeepSeek recipe says to test directly against the server at \(T=0\) and check that both nodes run the same image digest and the same snapshot.

In the TensorFold lane, penalties do not exist

The TensorFold server accepts presence_penalty, frequency_penalty, repetition_penalty and logit_bias and ignores them, without returning an error. For the recipes' defaults nothing changes, because none of them turns penalties on at the server. But a client that counts on a penalty to get out of a loop will get no effect at all in this lane, and the diagnosis has to go straight to the cause: temperature, template or numerics.

Reasoning models: thinking before answering

All three recipe models are reasoning models. Models of this kind are trained (typically with reinforcement learning) to write a draft of their thinking between <think> and </think> before the answer. From the loop's point of view there is nothing new: thinking tokens are ordinary tokens, drawn by the same sampler at the same cost per token. The difference is what they buy. More thinking tokens mean more computation spent on the question (test-time compute), and on hard problems that turns into better answers. The price shows up in latency and in max_tokens.

Markers, parser and the thinking switch

  • The markers are single tokens. In GLM, <think> = 154841 and </think> = 154842. DeepSeek also has single-token delimiters, and that will matter for the reasoning budget.
  • The template opens the thinking. The chat template, the rule that turns the list of messages into the text the model reads (Tokens, vocabulary and embeddings), decides how the prompt ends. With thinking on, the prompt ends with the generation trigger followed by <think>. With thinking off, the template already writes an empty <think></think> and the model starts straight on the answer. In GLM and Qwen the switch is chat_template_kwargs.enable_thinking (GLM also accepts thinking); in DeepSeek it is thinking.
  • The reasoning parser separates the two voices. --reasoning-parser glm45 (GLM), qwen3 (Qwen) and deepseek_v4 (DeepSeek) split the stream at </think>: the thinking goes into the reasoning field and the answer into content. A client pitfall documented in GLM's README: read reasoning, not reasoning_content. vLLM accepts the old name on input (and renames it), but never emits it in the response. A client that reads reasoning_content gets nothing and concludes that the thinking “leaked” into content, which did not happen. In the TensorFold lane it is the other way around: the server returns the thinking in reasoning_content and the answer in content. Whoever switches lanes has to switch the field they read.

Reasoning effort: an instruction, not a parameter

The reasoning effort looks like a sampler knob, but it is not one. It does not touch the logits. It is text: the chat template reads the value in chat_template_kwargs and injects a directive into the prompt, and the model, trained with those directives, thinks more or less. Three practical consequences:

  1. Each family accepts different values and handles invalid values its own way: it may ignore them, round them to another level or reject them with HTTP 400.
  2. Changing the effort changes the beginning of the prompt. Anything that changes early in the prompt breaks the prefix cache from that point on.
  3. The effort decides the length of the reasoning, and therefore how much of max_tokens is left for the answer.

GLM

  • low and high are accepted; any other value, or none at all, becomes max (chat_template.jinja:7). The directive is the line <|system|>Reasoning Effort: …, only with thinking on, and the | capitalize filter writes the level with an initial capital: Low, High or Max.
  • An empty GLM53_DEFAULT_REASONING_EFFORT omits --default-chat-template-kwargs (template → Max). It accepts low/high/max, and the launcher rejects medium, which the template would turn into Max.
  • The effort word lands at character 39 of the prompt, so varying the effort per request within a conversation is a total prefix cache miss.

Qwen

  • Accepts low, medium and xhigh (default xhigh).
  • high and max give HTTP 400 (“Unexpected reasoning effort max…”) before reaching the engine.
  • The check lives inside the thinking branch. With enable_thinking:false, an invalid value is silently ignored: the same client config fails in one mode and passes in the other.
  • chat_template_kwargs.thinking_budget is not honored by this build, and only max_tokens limits reasoning.

DeepSeek

  • DEFAULT_THINKING = low (default), off, high or max becomes --default-chat-template-kwargs: off→{"thinking":false}, the others →{"thinking":true,"reasoning_effort":"…"}. The request's kwargs always win, and only the field inside chat_template_kwargs reaches the encoder (a reasoning_effort at the top level of the JSON is ignored).
  • Inline patch in the compose file: max/xhigh→max, high→high, anything else→low. The old wrapper sent low (and everything else) to high, spending reasoning the client never asked for.
  • BOS + directive add up to 93 tokens at max, 80 at high and 0 at low. At max the checkpoint's directive says, in short, “do not stop reasoning until there is no error left to find”.
  • Because the directive comes right after the BOS, different efforts shared 0 blocks of prefix cache (#144). The opt-in DSPARK_ENABLE_ISSUE144_EFFORT_ALIGN=1 moves it to the end of the system region (details in Prefix caching).

max_tokens versus the reasoning budget

max_tokens counts everything, and reasoning comes first. If the ceiling runs out before the </think>, the response comes back with empty content (or null) and finish_reason: "length", and it looks as if the model “did not answer”. A reasoning budget solves this a different way: it limits only the thinking and, at the boundary, forces a </think>, leaving the rest of max_tokens for the answer. A budget that never asks the CPU anything

Forcing </think> is easy. At the exact step, just set every logit to −∞ and the one for </think> to 109, and the sampler picks it whatever the temperature (this is logit masking). It is the same mechanism GLM uses to enforce tool_choice:"none": the parser puts <tool_call> into bad_words, and the mask applies at every position, including the draft positions of speculative verification (The API). The hard part, for the budget, is knowing when to apply the mask without stalling the GPU. The first implementation (hotfix #31/#34, now removed) did the bookkeeping in Python: at every step it copied tokens from the GPU to the CPU and scanned the generated list, O(n) work multiplied by the speculation's draft rows. At long context this turned into a ~4.7× decode cliff (about 4.5 tok/s, issue #39). The implementation was reverted entirely, along with the defaults it injected (DEFAULT_THINKING_TOKEN_BUDGET=32768, DEFAULT_MAX_TOKENS=131072), and the recipe's CI now forbids reintroducing patterns such as .cpu( and .tolist( on that path.

Version 2 (hotfix-dsv4-issue31-v2-thinking-budget-gpu.py) starts from where the state lives. Each request's budget and prompt length sit in UVA tensors: page-locked (pinned) host memory mapped into the GPU's address space, which kernels read directly, with no explicit copy (Inside the GPU). These values are only written when the request arrives (add_request, followed by copy_to_uva). The “still thinking” flag lives in a tensor on the GPU itself. At each step, a NumPy check on the host, np.any(use_budget[idx_mapping_np]), decides whether any request in the batch asked for a budget. Because that check uses an array that is already on the CPU, it synchronizes nothing. If no request asked, no kernel is launched, and traffic that does not use the field pays nothing. If one did, two tiny Triton kernels come in. _force_thinking_end_kernel computes, for each batch row, \(\text{generated} = \text{pos} + 1 - \text{len(prompt)}\) and exits immediately if this is not the limit step. At the exact step, it rewrites the logit row. Before forcing, it looks back num_speculative_tokens rows: if </think> has already appeared on the speculative path, it forces nothing. _observe_accepted_end_kernel deactivates the request when a </think> is accepted, even if it came from an MTP draft token. No GPU→CPU copy happens on the hot path.

What the recipe measured

In an A/B from 1 to 6 concurrent sequences (256-token prompt, max_tokens 4096, budget 1024 versus no budget), the median per-sequence decode was equal to or above the baseline at every concurrency level (from −0.0% to +19.4%). On a fresh 65K-token prefix, decode with a budget measured 59.3 tok/s (256 reasoning tokens + answer, 769 in total), versus 37.2 tok/s without a budget (cut by length at 1024). The outputs have different compositions, so read the pair as “the ~4.5 tok/s cliff did not come back”, not as a gain. At short context, with a budget: 67.2 tok/s.

Loop or long tail?

When a reasoning request comes back with finish_reason: length and empty content, there are two different diseases with the same symptom. In the long tail, the model keeps saying new things, just too many of them, and raising max_tokens (or the budget) fixes it. In the loop, the model recycles what it has already said forever, and raising max_tokens only raises the bill. The DeepSeek recipe ships a one-screen instrument to tell them apart, scripts/loop_detector.py, which measures 8-gram novelty:

\[ \text{novelty}(W) = \frac{\big|\{\text{word 8-grams in } W\} \setminus \{\text{8-grams seen before } W\}\big|}{\big|\{\text{word 8-grams in } W\}\big|} \]
  • \(W\): a 700-word window of the reasoning (the reasoning field, not content)
  • threshold: 2%; loop verdict: 3 consecutive windows below it, with no recovery until the end
  • a collapse that recovers: “transient repetition”, not a loop
  • position in tokens ≈ characters / 4 (for the report only)

The calibration details teach a lot. In three real ~100-thousand-token traces that never finished, novelty collapsed and never recovered: it oscillated between 0 and 0.6%, without sitting exactly at zero (hence a 2% threshold instead of “equal to zero”). With the current algorithm, based on 700-word windows, the collapses begin at tokens ~7.2k, ~13.7k and ~30.2k. The first calibration, with windows over the character stream, marked ~7.9k, ~13.8k and ~29.7k, each less than one window away from the new value. The switch to word windows came because cutting the text mid-token created “new” fragments at every boundary, which held the novelty of a short, literal loop above the threshold. Two long, healthy traces (~31k and ~34k tokens), cut by length, never crossed the threshold in any window. The choice of word 8-grams instead of “unique 120-character chunks” came from an instructive mistake: real loops are templated, a handful of stock phrases recombined with a variable element on each lap. On the same three traces, unique chunks scored 22%/92%/66% (it looked like new text), and unique 8-grams scored 3.4%/4.0%/2.8%. The script also documents its limitation: a fragmented loop, with recycling interleaved with fresh filler, goes undetected.

Determinism: same question, same answer?

Fix \(T=0\). Greedy has no draw, so two identical runs should produce identical texts. In practice they do not always, because there is a second source of variation hidden in the logits. Floating-point addition is not associative: \((a+b)+c\) can differ from \(a+(b+c)\), because each sum is rounded to the format (How the computer stores numbers). GPU kernels sum in parallel, and the order of the sum can depend on how the work was split among blocks (split-K), on who arrived first, on the batch size. Differences in the last bit are usually harmless, until two logits are almost tied. Then the argmax flips, the token changes, and from there on the whole text diverges. And accumulating in FP32 helps, but does not solve it: with eight terms FP32 absorbs this example, but with thousands of terms and varied magnitudes the order differences reappear in the last bits. That is why bit-for-bit determinism in parallel inference requires fixing the order of the reductions, not just the seed.

Symptom. At TP2 + EP, identical requests gave different prompt logprobs, with a median spread of 0.25 nat and a maximum of 6.07 nats. Greedy outputs came out 5 of 5 distinct.

Two causes, two fixes. (1) The indexer of the QSA sparse attention (Sparse attention) uses a top-k to choose which blocks of the past the attention will read. The kernel returns the same set in a varying order, and the split-K sum over those blocks changes order along with it. With VLLM_QSA_DET_TOPK=1, each row is sorted after topk_op (the −1 entries become INT32_MAX so they go to the end, and then turn back into −1), and the same selection is always visited in the same order. (2) The fused MoE finalize, the weighted sum of the outputs of each token's 10 experts inside FlashInfer CUTLASS MoE, also varied between runs. With VLLM_MOE_DET_FINALIZE=1, the patched copy files/determinism/flashinfer_cutlass_moe.py passes use_fused_finalize = False, a non-fused, fixed-order reduction, and start.sh also injects VLLM_FLASHINFER_MOE_FUSED_FINALIZE=0 and a separate autotune directory (…/flashinfer_autotune_cache_unfused).

Result (2026-09-25). Spread 0/0 nats. Greedy outputs go to 1 of 5 distinct, that is, all identical, also when cold and after another prompt. Cost: ~−4% on decode (prose) and on prefill, which is why it is opt-in. It was not ported to the vLLM 0.30 lane and is incompatible with QSA_PROFILE.

When is it worth paying that 4%? When the goal is to compare: A/B tests of kernels, quantizations and patches, regression tests and numerical parity (Measuring the damage). If two runs of the same configuration already disagree, you cannot attribute a difference to the change you are testing. In production at \(T>0\) the draw already introduces much larger variation, and determinism is rarely worth its cost.

In the TensorFold lane, determinism is not optional

TensorFold flips the question “when is it worth paying?”. There, batch invariance is not an opt-in ~4% patch: it is the engine's contract, built into the design of every kernel, and it also holds at \(T>0\). The recipe publishes the tests: replies served 4 at a time are identical to the same requests served alone (11 of 11 staggered and 11 of 11 in a burst). Since there is no “inexact” TensorFold to compare against, there is no number for the cost of this choice. The next section shows how the engine gets there and what it gains.

Seed × determinism

These are two different guarantees. The seed makes the noise reproducible. The fixed order of the reductions makes the logits reproducible. Identical text at \(T>0\) needs both. At \(T=0\) the second one is enough.

Update: keyed sampling in TensorFold

The GLM recipe gained a second version . The checkpoint is the same (routed experts in EXL3 at 4 bits, the rest in BF16), so are the two DGX Sparks, but the engine changed: instead of vLLM, it runs TensorFold v0.6.0 + 53 patches, an engine written for the GLM family. The whole engine is in An exact engine, and the recipe in Swapping the engine. What matters here is a design decision that reaches straight into the sampler. TensorFold treats exactness as a contract: a draft is accepted only when it equals the token the same engine would produce serially. Three guarantees follow that vLLM does not give. The reply with drafts is, bit for bit, the reply of serial decoding. Four requests served together get exactly what they would get alone. And the same request, without a seed, always returns the same reply (in v0.6.0; the native engine draws a fresh seed by default since 1.1.1). This section shows how the sampler makes that possible and what it costs.

The draw as a pure function of (key, position, token)

You have already seen that vLLM's Model Runner V2 takes its Gumbel noise from a counter-based generator, indexed by (seed, position) and by the token id. TensorFold takes the idea all the way and turns the entire draw into keyed sampling:

\[ k = \begin{cases} \texttt{seed} & \text{if the request sends one}\\ \text{SHA-256}(\text{prompt ids})_{\text{8 bytes}} \bmod 2^{63} & \text{if it does not} \end{cases} \qquad u^{(t)}_v = H(k,\,t,\,v) \in (0,1) \qquad x_t = \arg\max_{v\in S_t}\Big(\frac{z^{(t)}_v}{T} - \log\!\big(-\log u^{(t)}_v\big)\Big) \]
  • \(k\): the request's key; without a seed, the first 8 bytes of the SHA-256 of the prompt ids, with 63 useful bits (“the same conversation samples the same reply”, says the code)
  • \(t\): the token's absolute position in the sequence; \(v\): the token id
  • \(H\): three rounds of splitmix64 mixing over (k, t, v), converted into a double in (0, 1)
  • \(S_t\): the candidates that survive the cuts (top-k, top-p, min-p) at position \(t\)

Notice what does not appear in the formula: how many other requests are in the batch, how many numbers were drawn before, whether the row sits in a verification window or alone. The push for token \(v\) at position \(t\) is always the same, as if there were a giant noise table that nobody needs to store. It is a die that, instead of being rolled, is looked up: whoever asks “which face comes up at position 12 for token 4410?” always gets the same answer. The figure runs the same request three times, with a sequential generator (a single stream of random numbers, like a global torch.Generator) and with the keyed generator.

The cuts also have a fixed order and fixed tie-breaking. Candidates are sorted by decreasing value and, on ties, by increasing id. Then come top-k, the division by \(T\), top-p (by cumulative sum) and, last, min-p. The whole choice runs in float64 on the host (NumPy), over the few candidates the GPU delivers: the top_k + 8 largest (the margin of 8 exists to break ties by id on the CPU) or, with top-k off, up to 1,024 per GPU. Greedy (\(T=0\)) skips the draw: each GPU finds its largest logit, and the host compares the two, with the same tie-break by id.

Why on the host, in float64?

The function that samples a whole verification window promises, in the code itself, “the same operation order and bits” as sampling each row alone. A GPU kernel whose reduction is split according to the number of rows would not give that guarantee for free, and a NumPy loop in double precision, with the order written by hand, does. The price is one GPU→CPU copy per round, exactly the kind of synchronization that vLLM's Gumbel-max avoids and that the DeepSeek recipe's CI forbids on the budget path. TensorFold amortizes it: a round verifies up to 16 rows of one stream (the pending token and up to 15 drafts), so one synchronization serves several tokens (interpretation).

Exactness needs both halves

The key settles the noise. But the “Seed × determinism” callout from the previous section still holds: identical text at \(T>0\) also requires identical logits, and this is where the whole engine comes in. The verification kernels keep each row's arithmetic independent of the other rows in the call, so split-K has a partition fixed by the weight's shape and the tiles do not change with the batch. Operations that could pick an algorithm by the number of rows (an F.linear through cuBLAS, DFlash2's torch.topk) run per stream, only on that stream's rows. Sums with atomics only happen on integers, which give the same result in any order. And tensor parallelism does not use all-reduce: each rank receives both ranks' fp32 partials and adds them in rank order, rank 0 first, so the two Sparks reach the same bits and sample the same token without having to exchange it. This set of rules is called batch invariance.

The tests the recipe publishes are end to end: replies served 4 at a time are identical to the same requests served alone (11 of 11 staggered, 11 of 11 in a burst), and v1.3 records “drafted = serial” in 6 of 6 cases and concurrency in 22 of 22. There is an important caveat: exact means equal to the same engine with the same settings, not equal to the checkpoint in BF16. Three defaults trade bits for speed and for the 1M window: DENSE=q4 and KV=fp8 are lossy, and KDA_CHUNKED=1 is close to the serial kernel, but not its bits. DENSE=bf16 KV=bf16 KDA_CHUNKED=0 serves the checkpoint as it is, with a smaller window: 163,840 tokens with DFlash2 and vision on (the default), or 196,608 with vision off.

Verifying becomes comparing

Now the payoff for speculation. A TensorFold round runs one forward over the window [pending token, drafts…], samples each row with the key at its absolute position and accepts drafts while draft = sample. At the first disagreement, the target's sample on that row is emitted as the correction, and if all pass, the last row's sample is the bonus token. Since the sample at position \(t\) is a deterministic function of the logits and the key, and the logits do not depend on the other rows, the reply is that of serial decoding whatever the drafter and whatever the stop rule. This is equality verification. In the next section you will see vLLM's rule, rejection sampling, which accepts with probability \(\min(1, p/q)\): it preserves the target's distribution, not the sequence, and it needs the drafter's probabilities.

Equality, though, is demanding. A drafter that proposes its own favorite only hits when the target's draw lands on it, with probability \(p(\text{favorite})\). One that samples with its own independent noise hits \(\sum_v p_v q_v\) of the time, and even a perfect drafter (\(q=p\)) would hit only \(\sum_v p_v^2\). The way out is TensorFold's clever move: the target's noise is known before verification, because it is a function of the key and the position. The drafter can then use the same push. TensorFold's DFlash2 picks each candidate by \(\arg\max\big((\ell + 0.6\cdot\text{edge})/T + 0.7\cdot g\big)\), where \(\ell\) are the drafter's scores, the edge is the selector's score between neighboring candidates and \(g\) are the target's keyed Gumbel draws for those candidates at those positions. With weight 1 and \(q\approx p\), the two races get the same push and almost always end with the same winner. This is a shared-noise coupling.

Play with the modes. With zero error, the drafter that uses the target's noise with weight 1 agrees every time, while the one that ignores the noise is stuck at the favorite's probability and the one with its own noise sits even lower. As the error grows, they all drop, but the coupling stays well above independent noise. The dashed line is the ceiling for the two modes that sample from \(q\), “own noise” and “target noise ×1”: \(\sum\min(p,q)\) bounds any way of making a sample of \(p\) and a sample of \(q\) agree, and rejection sampling reaches it (the next section shows where this common area comes from; with 3,000 rounds, a bar can cross it by a point, which is sampling noise). That is the honest trade-off: equality gives up a little acceptance relative to the rejection rule and, in exchange, the reply stops depending on the drafter. The other two modes do not sample from \(q\). Since \(\arg\max(s + 0.7\,g) = \arg\max(s/0.7 + g)\), the 0.7 weight amounts to sampling from \(q\) at a temperature of 0.7·\(T\), which is sharper, and “ignores the noise” is the extreme case, temperature zero (our own math). Their ceiling is a different one, and they can cross the line: with \(\delta=0.3\) and \(T=0.2\), \(q\) has the same favorite as \(p\) but is much more spread out, and the drafter that only bets on the favorite agrees 92% of the time, against a ceiling of 77% for a drafter that samples from \(q\). Near \(T=1\), in this toy, weight 1 agrees more often than 0.7. The 0.7 is an engine tuning for the real drafter, and the repository does not document how it was chosen.

Noise-aware stop ruleDRAFT_POLICY=fnc7:0.3

Knowing the noise also helps decide how many drafts to propose. The old rule, fc5:0.3, stopped the chain when the product of the drafter's confidences, without noise, fell below 0.3. The recipe's rule, fnc7:0.3, proposes up to 7 drafts and measures each confidence under the request's own noise: \(\text{softmax}\big((\text{score} + g)/\beta\big)\) at the chosen candidate, the chance that the target's keyed sample lands on it if the target's scores are the drafter's plus Gumbel errors of scale \(\beta\). The \(\beta = 2.0\) was fitted by log loss on 9 dumps from 2026-09-30 (7 of sampled prose, 2 of greedy code); a finer grid gave 1.8, with a log loss only 0.2% lower. Measured against fc5:0.3, with two boots each: prose from 47.2 to 50.6 tok/s and code from 51.7 to 55.5 tok/s. These were measured at an earlier stage of development, with a different workload, so the baseline is not comparable to sparkDash's 60.4 tok/s. And the reply is the same under any rule, because drafts only propose. Exactness even became a research tool: since the draw is keyed, a dump of one request decoded one token per round makes it possible to replay exactly, on the CPU, what any other stop rule would have accepted, and that is how the rules were tuned (details in DFlash2 and DSpark).

Exact top-p with the vocabulary split across two GPUs

With the lm_head split by vocabulary, each Spark only knows the scores of half of the tokens, but top-p needs the global cumulative mass. The DeepSeek recipe solves this by gathering whole logit rows with an all-gather (section 2). TensorFold exchanges only candidates and, even so, has to guarantee that one GPU and two GPUs draw the same token. The path, when top-k is off:

  1. Global maximum. Each rank divides its scores by \(T\) in float64 and finds its own maximum; an all-gather gives the global maximum \(m\).
  2. Integer masses. Each token's mass becomes a fixed-point integer, \(\mu_v = \lfloor e^{z_v/T - m}\cdot 2^{40}\rfloor\). Integer sums are exact and associative, so the total of the two slices gives the same bits in any order, on both ranks, and, given the same scores, equals that of a single GPU.
  3. Candidates. Each rank sends its 1,024 best (value, id, mass), plus the total mass and the width of its slice. The nucleus target is \(\lceil \texttt{top\_p}\cdot\text{total}\rceil\), computed as an exact fraction.
  4. Coverage test. Each rank's share of the nucleus must end inside the candidates it sent. The test is conservative: the masses of that rank's candidates, on their own, must reach the target at a value strictly above its last candidate. If any rank fails, a token it did not send could be in the nucleus, and the ranks read their whole vocabulary slices.
  5. Draw. Both ranks' candidates are merged by (decreasing value, increasing id), the nucleus is cut by the integer sum of the masses, min-p is applied and the keyed Gumbel chooses.

The byte count shows why it pays off (our own math from the code). With the GLM vocabulary split in two, each rank holds 77,440 tokens. The candidate packet carries three 8-byte integers per candidate, about 24 KB per row, against about 1.86 MB if the whole slice went along. Patch 0034 (TENSORFOLD_NUCLEUS_UNION=1, opt-in and off in the recipe) loosens the conservative test. It walks both ranks' candidates together up to the target and stops at a value \(v^*\); if \(v^*\) sits above the last candidate of each rank, every token not sent is below \(v^*\), and the nucleus is the same as over the whole vocabulary. If that fails, it first tries 16 × 1,024 candidates before reading whole slices. Result: the same draw with fewer full reads, with no published number.

What the TensorFold lane leaves out

GLM · vLLM

  • Gumbel-max in a GPU kernel (Model Runner V2), with no CPU synchronization.
  • Noise by (seed, position, token); without a seed, fresh noise for every request.
  • Rejection verification: exact in distribution; batching and speculation can change the bits.
  • logprobs, n>1, penalties and logit_bias available.
  • Reasoning in reasoning; an unknown effort becomes Max in the template.

GLM · TensorFold

  • Candidates from the GPU, keyed choice in float64 on the host: one copy per round.
  • Key = seed or the prompt's SHA-256: the same request gives the same reply.
  • Equality verification: drafted = serial, concurrent = alone, bit for bit.
  • logprobs/top_logprobs and n≠1 get HTTP 400 (“this engine returns no token probabilities”) in the recipe's v0.6.0 engine; the native engine has returned logprobs since 1.0.8. Penalties and logit_bias are accepted and ignored.
  • Reasoning in reasoning_content; effort normalized at the server.

Three practical consequences. First, parity tests in this lane do not use logprobs, as the Qwen determinism patch did, but hashes: the reply carries a tensorfold block with the token_sha of the generated tokens, and "draft": false requests the serial reference, a yes-or-no question (“did the hash change?”). Second, without a seed a retry returns the same reply; a client that wants variety when retrying has to vary the seed (interpretation). Third, the published speed numbers are greedy (sparkDash: “temp 0, thinking off”), like those of the vLLM lane, and the next section explains why greedy is speculation's best case.

In the recipes: sampling × speculation and the full panel

What is left is stitching sampling to speculative decoding, which all three recipes use. A cheap drafter proposes tokens with its own distribution \(q\), and the target model, with distribution \(p\), verifies them all at once. For the final text to have exactly the target's distribution, verification uses rejection sampling: it accepts draft \(x\) with probability \(\min(1, p(x)/q(x))\) and, at the first rejection, samples from the residual. The chance of accepting at a given position is the overlap area of the two distributions:

\[ \alpha \;=\; \sum_v \min\big(p_v,\, q_v\big) \;=\; 1 - \text{TV}(p, q), \qquad \text{TV}(p,q) = \tfrac12\sum_v |p_v - q_v| \]
  • \(\alpha\): probability of accepting the draft at this position
  • \(p\), \(q\): target and drafter distributions, at the served temperature
  • TV: total variation distance

Temperature enters on both sides. At \(T=0\), \(p\) and \(q\) become one-hot: acceptance is 1 if the favorites match and 0 if they do not. Since good drafters hit the favorite very often, greedy gives the highest acceptance. With \(T\) around 1 the distributions spread out, the small differences in the tail start to count and the overlap shrinks. See it in the figure.

Notice that, with matching favorites, acceptance falls from 100% at \(T=0\) to much less at \(T\approx1\). If you push the slider all the way to \(T=2\), you will see the curve climb back up. It is not a bug: as \(T\to\infty\), \(p\) and \(q\) both tend to the uniform distribution and \(\alpha\to1\), but that just means sampling garbage with a lot of agreement. The regime that matters is \(T\in[0,1]\).

One detail of the “tokens/step” readout is still missing: the bonus token. The verification forward pass computes the target's distribution at all \(k+1\) positions. If a draft is rejected, the target samples the correction right there, from the residual. If all \(k\) drafts are accepted, the distribution at position \(k+1\) is already available, and the target samples one more token from it for free. Each step therefore emits from 1 to \(k+1\) tokens, and with the same \(\alpha\) at every position the mean is \(1+\alpha+\dots+\alpha^k = (1-\alpha^{k+1})/(1-\alpha)\), the readout's formula. Two metrics are often confused here. The “acceptance rate” vLLM reports is accepted ÷ proposed, summed over all positions (in Qwen, 823 of 1,131 = 72.8%). The \(\alpha\) in the equation, on the other hand, is the probability of accepting at one position, given that the previous ones passed. vLLM also reports per-position acceptance, the fraction of steps in which the draft at position \(i\) was accepted (which requires all the previous ones to have been accepted too): in the same Qwen run, 89%, 74.5% and 60% at positions 1, 2 and 3. With a constant \(\alpha\) that sequence would be \(\alpha, \alpha^2, \alpha^3\), about 89%, 79% and 70% (our own arithmetic). The faster drop suggests that more distant drafts hit less often, and this last part is interpretation.

The recipes record the effect of temperature. Qwen's audit notes that the benchmark numbers (MTP=3, 24.5 → 52.1 tok/s at batch 1, 72.8% acceptance) are greedy, that the \(T=1.0\) from generation_config makes rejection sampling accept fewer drafts and that real chat throughput will be lower. GLM's adaptive k is described as “lossless at temperature 0”. Acceptance also depends on the kind of text: in GLM's bench, with 7 drafts per step, structured output accepted 0.959 (6.71 drafts accepted per step, ≈7.7 tokens with the bonus) and prose, 0.341 (2.39 accepted, ≈3.4 tokens). DeepSeek provides the contrasting profile: because it measures decode at \(T=0.6\)/top_p 0.95 (bench-miaai.py), its numbers sit closer to served throughput than GLM's numbers and Qwen's MTP numbers, which are measured in greedy. DeepSeek tested DRAFT_SAMPLE_METHOD=greedy against probabilistic in the drafter and got a tie, and the recipe kept probabilistic. The GLM vLLM lane also uses "draft_sample_method":"probabilistic","rejection_sample_method":"standard". The full math is in Speculative decoding.

In the TensorFold lane there is no rejection sampling

Everything this section says about \(\min(1, p/q)\) applies to vLLM. In TensorFold, verification is by equality with the target's keyed sample (previous section): per-position agreement comes from the shared-noise coupling, never exceeds the common area between \(p\) and the distribution of the drafter's proposals, and the reply is identical to the serial one whatever the drafter. Temperature acts the same way: in greedy the two rules coincide (accept if the favorites match), and at \(T\approx 1\) agreement drops. This lane's speed numbers are greedy too (sparkDash, “temp 0, thinking off”, GPU clocks capped at 2,200 MHz): prose at 60.4 tok/s with 1 request and 108.8 tok/s aggregate with 4; structured output at 114.7 and 227.9 tok/s. The tool, dates and settings differ from the vLLM lane's measurements, so do not read the two sets as a controlled A/B between engines.

Roadmap for your recipe

  1. Read the checkpoint. generation_config.json, EOS and the kwargs the template accepts (effort values, name of the thinking switch). Test each value and write down which ones give HTTP 400 and which are ignored. Also check which field the client reads the thinking from: in the vLLM lane, reasoning, not reasoning_content; in the TensorFold lane, the other way around.
  2. Decide the server defaults. auto, vllm or an override, plus a fixed default effort per route, so as not to break the prefix cache. Treat the choice as a hypothesis to be measured: the MiMo case shows that vllm can produce tool-call loops that auto avoids.
  3. Warm up the sampler. Include requests at \(T>0\) with top-k, top-p and both in the warmup, so that no kernel compiles in the middle of serving.
  4. Size max_tokens for the reasoning. Measure the thinking length at the effort you will serve and, if the engine offers one, use a budget implemented on the GPU.
  5. Measure in the served profile. Greedy benchmarks say little about throughput at \(T=1\) with speculation. Run both, or measure directly at the temperature the client will use, as DeepSeek's bench does at \(T=0.6\).
  6. Keep a deterministic mode for A/B tests and regressions, even if it costs a few percent. In the TensorFold lane it comes built in: compare the reply's token_sha with and without "draft": false. And, when something gets out of hand, tell a loop from a long tail before touching max_tokens.

Every step of this loop paid for a full forward pass, and we pretended the past was “reread” for free.
