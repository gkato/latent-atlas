You quantized a model with hundreds of billions of parameters down to 4 bits, swapped three kernels and turned on a speculative drafter. In the previous chapter (Checkpoints), the checkpoint passed the integrity check: every tensor in place, every scale with the right shape. Now comes the hard question: does it still think straight? A broken LLM rarely crashes; it stays fluent and gets a little more wrong. This chapter is your metrology lab: perplexity, KL divergence and top-1 agreement, parity between kernels, determinism, and the functional batteries that Mia’s recipes use as gates before accepting any change.

“Looks good” is not a measurement

Almost all numerical damage in an LLM is a silent failure. A kernel with an indexing bug usually crashes or spits out NaN; that is the easy case. The hard one is math that is almost right: weights rounded more coarsely, a sum in a different order, a KV cache key with 3 mantissa bits. The model stays articulate and starts getting more things wrong, in a way no “hi, how are you?” will ever reveal. The three recipes have scars like these:

  • In GLM, a degenerate boot (#249) answered /health normally, but every response was !!!! and the DFlash drafter accepted almost nothing.
  • In Qwen, an FP8 KV cache can make the sparse attention indexer pick different blocks (Quantized KV cache). The failure mode, says the script that watches for it, is “wrong answer, not slowness”.
  • Speeding up GLM’s prefill by also quantizing the activations (W8A8, #182) changed probabilities reproducibly: a token the reference gave 77.8% dropped to 0.51%. No error, no warning.
  • In DeepSeek, the “garble” (output that derails into an echo of the prompt, the tool schema or special tokens) only showed up in cold prefill.
  • In GLM’s TensorFold version, the 4-bit dense weights passed GSM8K and HumanEval. On short French coding prompts, though, 12 of 48 replies lost the end of turn and ran all the way to max_tokens (#18).

The engineering answer is to treat quality as a measuring instrument. Picture a building with smoke detectors on every floor: the error is born on one floor (weights, kernel, attention, parser) and rises, and each detector only sees certain kinds of smoke. Pick a defect in the figure and see which detectors go off.

The bottom detectors are cheap but nearsighted: the weight error comes out in minutes, with no GPU, but it does not know that a tiny tensor may be the most important one. The top ones are realistic but coarse: a benchmark only notices the damage once it already changes whole answers. And some damage only shows up in one place: broken tool syntax does not change the KLD of a prose text, and a degenerate engine is only caught by a test that runs on every boot.

Why this matters

In the journey map, every recipe answered three questions: does it fit? is it fast? is it still right? The third one only has an answer if you choose the instruments before touching anything and learn to tell damage from noise.

Surprise measured in nats: NLL and perplexity

At each position, a language model returns a probability distribution over the vocabulary for the next token (logits → softmax; see Generating text). To measure quality you do not need to generate anything: feed the model a whole real text and, at each position \(t\), read the probability it gave to the token that actually comes next. That is teacher forcing: the text leads the model by the hand, and its mistakes do not propagate. It does not depend on sampling and costs a single prefill, but it is not bit-deterministic: the same prefill can vary in the last bits between runs (see the determinism section).

If the model gave probability \(p\) to the right token, its surprisal is \(-\ln p\): zero if it was certain, large if it thought that token unlikely. With the natural logarithm, the unit is the nat (with \(\log_2\) it would be the bit; 1 nat ≈ 1.443 bits). The average surprisal over the text is the NLL (negative log-likelihood), and its exponential is the perplexity:

\[ \text{NLL} = -\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \ln p_\theta\!\left(x_t \mid x_{<t}\right) \] \[ \text{PPL} = e^{\text{NLL}} \]
  • \(x_t\): the true token at position \(t\) of the evaluation text
  • \(x_{<t}\): all previous tokens (the context comes from the text, not from the model)
  • \(p_\theta(\cdot)\): the probability assigned by the model with weights \(\theta\)
  • \(T\): number of scored positions
  • PPL: “on average, the model is as undecided as if it were picking at random among PPL options”

Worked example (illustrative): probabilities 0.5, 0.25, 0.8 and 0.1 for the right token give surprisals of 0.693, 1.386, 0.223 and 2.303 nats; the mean is 1.151 nats and the perplexity, \(e^{1.151} \approx 3.16\). The single bad position (0.1) dominates the sum: NLL punishes hard whatever the model did not expect.

In vLLM, /v1/completions with echo, max_tokens=1 and prompt_logprobs returns, for each prompt position, the log-probabilities of the \(k\) most likely tokens and that of the observed token. That is what the recipes’ quality scripts use, and what the figure does. The exception is the TensorFold version of the GLM recipe, whose engine returns no token probabilities (more on this later).

In “errors that cancel” mode, the distributions really do get damaged and the NLL stays almost the same, because the mean adds up the losses and the gains. That is perplexity’s Achilles’ heel.

Going deeper: NLL is the training loss

The cross-entropy minimized during training (What learning is) is the mean NLL over the corpus: the model’s most “native” measure, and a poorly discriminating one. A reasonable quantized model sits within thousandths of a nat of the original. Perplexities can only be compared with the same text, tokenizer and positions.

KL divergence: the ruler for distributions

Perplexity looks at one number per position, but it is the whole distribution that decides what comes out when you sample, what the drafter guesses, which tool gets called. Compare, position by position, the distribution of the reference \(P\) (the teacher: the high-precision original, or the system without the change) with that of the candidate \(Q\), both in teacher forcing over the same text. The ruler is the Kullback–Leibler divergence:

\[ D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q) = \sum_{v \in \mathcal{V}} P(v)\,\ln\frac{P(v)}{Q(v)} \] \[ \overline{\mathrm{KLD}} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(P_t\,\|\,Q_t\right) \]
  • \(P(v)\), \(Q(v)\): probability of token \(v\) according to the reference and the candidate, at the same position and with the same context
  • \(\mathcal{V}\): the vocabulary (in practice, the top-\(k\) the API returns)
  • \(P_t, Q_t\): the distributions at position \(t\); the mean over positions is the number reported on the panels, in nats per position

Three properties. KL is always ≥ 0 and is zero only for identical distributions. It is asymmetric: \(D(P\|Q) \ne D(Q\|P)\), because it weighs errors more where the reference puts mass. And it is “extra surprise”: \(H(P,Q) = H(P) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P\|Q)\), that is, the cross-entropy of predicting \(P\) with \(Q\) is the entropy of the reference plus the KL. KL is how many extra nats per token you pay for using the damaged model.

Worked example (illustrative): \(P = (0.60,\ 0.25,\ 0.10,\ 0.05)\) and \(Q = (0.50,\ 0.30,\ 0.12,\ 0.08)\). The terms are \(0.6\ln 1.2 = +0.109\), \(0.25 \ln 0.833 = -0.046\), \(0.1\ln 0.833 = -0.018\) and \(0.05\ln 0.625 = -0.024\): sum ≈ 0.022 nats. The top-1 is the same in both (an argmax test would say “identical”), but the shape changed enough to register on the ruler, in the same order of magnitude as GLM’s real panel, just below.

Two practical traps. Asymmetry: flip the direction and the number changes. And the top-k support: the API returns only the \(k\) most likely tokens (the recipes use \(k=20\)), so the KL is computed on the tokens present in both lists, renormalized. The tail disappears; when the top-k covers almost all of the mass, the number typically comes out below the true KL. GLM calls it a “KL proxy”: it is for comparing arms measured the same way, not for comparing against the KLD from some other script.

Going deeper: why the proxy “typically” underestimates

By the chain rule, the true KL is the sum of three terms ≥ 0: the binary KL between the masses inside and outside the common support \(S\), the mass \(P(S)\) times the proxy \(D_S\), and the tail mass times the tail’s own KL. Therefore \(D \ge P(S)\,D_S\): at a confident position, with \(P(S)\approx 1\), the proxy underestimates. At a high-entropy one, where the top-20 covers little mass, it can exceed the true value. The error goes both ways: compare proxies only with proxies.

KL is cheaper than a benchmark

Qwen’s HANDOFF proposes deciding between two checkpoints by logprobs/KL on identical prompts (“~30 min, including two model swaps”), because the 1,319-question GSM8K only resolves differences ≥ 1.3 percentage points. And the sparse attention indexer is trained by minimizing a KL against dense attention (Sparse attention): the same instrument, used as a loss.

Top-1, margins and the stock’s own noise

The most intuitive metric is top-1 agreement (argmax agreement): in what fraction of positions is the candidate’s most likely token the same as the reference’s? It speaks the language of greedy decoding: if the argmax changes, the text changes from there on. GLM’s dense FP8 (dense projections in per-channel FP8; FP8, FP4 and microscaling) measured argmax 94–100% and a KL proxy of 0.002–0.013 nats per position against stock (provisional, 2026-09-08, GLM53_DENSE_FP8=dense,kda).

The problem shows up in stock against stock. At many positions the two best tokens are nearly tied (the margin \(\ln p_{(1)} - \ln p_{(2)}\) is tiny), and a sum in a different order is enough to flip the argmax. That flip is not damage: it is the same machine tossing a coin on a tie. That is why a fixed gate of “argmax ≥ 0.99 / mean KL ≤ 0.01” fails the stock itself on identical repetitions of this model family, as the docstring of GLM’s panel records.

The calibrated panel (a screen)

GLM’s answer (tests/bench_numerical_calibrated.py) is to tare the scale before weighing: measure how much the ruler wobbles and only then judge the candidate. The docstring calls it a screening panel, not a qualification one. The calibrated panel:

  1. Calibrates with ≥ 4 stock captures (prompt_logprobs=20 over fixed texts of prose, code, arithmetic, counting and logs, with each prompt’s SHA-256 in the receipt).
  2. Classifies each position: CONF if the stock never changed argmax and every margin was ≥ \(m^*\) (fixed at 0.25 nats in the script); TIE if some margin fell below it; UNSTABLE if it switched argmax even with comfortable margins (dropped from every gate).
  3. Judges the candidate only on CONF positions: consensus flips; separation of the reference token’s log-prob beyond \(\max(0.25,\ \text{stock spread})\); and standardized KL.
  4. Standardizes before averaging: each position’s candidate↔stock KL is divided by the largest KL between stock pairs at that position (the stock null), and the mean of the ratios is compared with 2. A noisy position cannot “buy” slack for the others.
\[ r_t = \frac{\overline{\mathrm{KL}}_{\,\text{cand}\leftrightarrow\text{stock}}(t)}{\max_{a\ne b}\ \mathrm{KL}\big(\text{stock}_a,\text{stock}_b\big)(t)} \] \[ \bar r = \frac{1}{|\mathrm{CONF}|}\sum_{t\in \mathrm{CONF}} r_t \;\le\; 2 \]
  • \(r_t\): how far the candidate strays, in units of the stock’s worst wobble at that position
  • 2: KL_NULL_FACTOR, a screening heuristic, not a significance test

For flips, the slack uses the rule of three: with 0 events in \(n\) trials, the 95% upper bound for the rate is ≈ \(3/n\) (because \(-\ln 0.05 \approx 3\)). With \(r_{\text{up}} = 3/n_{\text{conf}}\) and doubled slack, \(\lambda = n_{\text{conf}}\cdot 2\cdot r_{\text{up}} = 6\) expected flips; the Poisson critical value (\(\alpha = 0.05\)) is 11, and the panel flags at or above it (it tolerates up to 10). The code warns: since CONF has 0 flips by construction, this slack has no guaranteed statistical coverage. Two instruments, two vocabularies

The screen is not the only judge. GLM’s formal studies use the kl_panel from llm-quality-suite (prefill logprobs over tens of thousands of tokens of code, prose and agent tails) and sealed protocols, which speak a different language:

NOT-WORSE
Not worse: the NLL change fits within \(\max(\text{stock range},\ 0.005)\) nats/token. Authorizes adopting an optimization.
EQUIV
Stronger: it fits within the band of the stock pairs themselves. It often comes out inconclusive, because the stock itself barely fits in it.
INCONCLUSIVE
The instrument could not judge, typically because the control (a stock boot playing the “candidate”) failed a gate. Neither a pass nor a fail.

The adoption of GLM53_DENSE_FP8=all + the BF16 copy of KDA (2026-09-26) was judged by kl_panel: 3 stock boots × 2 captures, with a “leave-one-boot-out” control. The control passed NOT-WORSE, and both candidates were NOT-WORSE on every item (ΔNLL from −0.004 to +0.0005 nats/token). EQUIV was inconclusive: the stock boots themselves missed the max-of-pairs band by ≤ 3%. And the report interprets: the text KL of FP8=all sits 25–30% above all stock pairs, a real, small change that does not predict worse text.

The calibrated panel failed its own control

In that same adoption, the screen (bench_numerical_calibrated.py, the “W-NUM screen” of the 09-26b nightly A/B/A) exited with code 1, and that night’s frozen verdicts were REJECT for both candidates for that reason alone. But it also flagged the left-out stock control, that night and again in the numerics study; and, in the BF16 KDA qualification (46 texts × 8 boots), the frozen comparator flagged the stock control once more. The maintainers’ conclusion: with 2 captures per arm, it is not a gate; it is a screen, just as the docstring says. A detector that fails the negative control is not fit to judge.

An instrument proves it can see damage with a positive control. In KDA (GLM’s linear attention, chapter 14), the input projection runs on the Marlin kernel, W8A16 (FP8 weights, BF16 activations). The W8A8 from #182 kicked in when the matmul had \(M > 64\) rows (M = tokens scheduled in the operation). In the A/B/C study (15 texts, 33,005 positions per capture), it reappeared loud and clear: at M=65, KL 7.99× the stock null and 23 coordinated flips; the BF16 copy that replaced it stayed at 1.10× and 0 flips.

The sealed thin-decode study (GLM53_EXL3_MOE_FAST: the same math as the EXL3 decode kernel, with fewer spilled registers, 127 regs/0 LDL versus 128/39), on the other hand, ended INCONCLUSIVE. The candidate had 0 consensus flips (critical value 11) and KL within the null in every family. But the protocol, with \(m^*\) derived from calibration (1.5 nats, the top of the grid), had two control failures: the absolute gate “KL ≤ 0.01” failed on stock versus stock (null of 0.0142–0.0146), and a bimodal long-context stock position blew the τ threshold transfer (0.5288 versus 0.500). Record: “inconclusive with clean relative evidence”, not “passed”. The moral of the imported threshold: “KL ≤ 0.01” without calibration against your own system’s null either fails everything or, if too loose, passes everything.

Going deeper: frozen protocol

The study was preregistered: protocol and thresholds, with a SHA-256 published before the first capture (protocol-numerical-FROZEN.md). This prevents looking at the data and then choosing the threshold that lets the candidate pass; it is the logic of clinical trials: an honest INCONCLUSIVE is worth more than a tuned PASS.

One rung down: weight error and kernel parity

Before spending GPU time on logprobs, you can measure the damage where it is born: after quantizing, dequantize and compare with the original, tensor by tensor. Two measures summarize the error:

\[ \text{rel RMSE} = \frac{\lVert \hat W - W \rVert_F}{\lVert W \rVert_F} \] \[ \cos(\hat W, W) = \frac{\langle \hat W, W\rangle}{\lVert \hat W\rVert_F\,\lVert W\rVert_F} \]
  • \(W\): the original tensor (e.g., BF16); \(\hat W\): the dequantized one (code × scale)
  • \(\lVert\cdot\rVert_F\): Frobenius norm (square root of the sum of squares); \(\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle\): elementwise inner product
  • for a small, unbiased error, \(\cos \approx 1 - \tfrac12\,\text{rel}^2\)

The relative RMSE says how much of the signal became noise; the cosine similarity, how well aligned the tensor stayed. With a rel RMSE of 2.68%, \(1 - 0.5\times0.0268^2 \approx 0.99964\): the same order as the “cos ≥ 0.9996” measured in Qwen.

Verificationverify_fp8_dense_checkpoint.py · rel > 0.06 → HIGH ERROR

Qwen’s hybrid FP8-dense checkpoint (NVFP4 experts untouched; 591 dense projections in FP8 E4M3 with a per-output-channel scale, \(s = \text{amax}_{\text{row}}/448\)) goes through two GPU-free instruments. verify_fp8_dense_checkpoint.py, the integrity verifier from Checkpoints, checks that the tensors are present, that the scales are F32 [rows] and that the shards were not rewritten but stay on the same inode (hard link), and dequantizes 6 samples: it measured cosine ≥ 0.9996, and above 0.06 relative error it would shout HIGH ERROR. compute_quant_stats.py recomputes the rel RMSE of the 591 tensors and writes fp8_dense_quant_stats.json: min/median/max 0.0240 / 0.0262 / 0.0268. The worst are the small block_inject_weight gates (if MTP acceptance drops, rebuild with lm_head in BF16). The checkpoint was never measured on a GPU: the bottom rung passed; the upper ones were never climbed.

In the figure, a synthetic weight matrix is quantized by five schemes. Switch the scheme and watch each cell snap onto the new format’s grid.

In FP8, per-channel or per-tensor scaling comes out almost the same (2.2% → 2.7% in this matrix): the exponent absorbs different scales. Qwen chose per-channel, rather than 128 × 128 blocks, because of the shapes (320-row projections and the per-rank slicing of lm_head). Two takeaways. The 0.06 threshold belongs to the FP8 verifier: 4-bit formats live above it by construction (≈ 8–12%) and are judged by KLD. And weight error is not output error: 2.6% on a tiny gate that multiplies the residual stream can weigh more than on a huge projection. The bottom rung filters out disasters; it does not certify quality.

Numerical parity of kernels

The second rung compares operations: the new kernel (Kernels) against a reference, with the same inputs. That is numerical parity, and the hard question is always the same: how much difference is acceptable? Three styles show up in the recipes.

  • Fixed tolerance against an oracle. GLM’s kernel_lab/exl3/reference.py is an EXL3 oracle in NumPy (trellis, MCG, Hadamards). The streaming path is checked against the materialized one with allclose(rtol=2e-5, atol=2e-5); maximum error recorded, 1.08e-6.
  • Tolerance calibrated by stock noise. The thin-decode comparator (tests/compare_thin_fast.py) runs the stock kernel twice (A and B) and the candidate (F) once, and passes if \(\max|F - A| \le \max\!\big(4\cdot\max|B - A|,\ 10^{-3}\cdot\max(1, \max|A|)\big)\). CUDA graph replay versus eager: rel RMSE ≤ 1e-5. Result: 119/119 cases, rel RMSE ~1e-8.
  • Set equivalence, not order equivalence. DeepSeek’s SP indexer test (scripts/test-sp-indexer-gpu.py) emulates TP=2 and TP=3 on a single GPU, with the real kernels, and requires it to match the single-rank stock path in the number of valid candidates and in the multiset of scores, but not in their order, which may permute on ties. Before that, CPU tests check the splitting and the boundaries against brute force (TP 2, 3 and 4). Rule: a change that touches the numerics proves correctness before measuring speed.

GLM’s BF16 KDA copy has a rel RMSE of 0.002526 against Marlin (W8A8 gave 0.0266; Marlin itself sits 0.00005 away from the exact weight): ten times closer to stock, which the logprobs confirmed (1.10× the null, 0 flips).

This env, only in the Stage-C lane (the stack with the readable attention overlay), reproduces that in the DSpark drafter’s KV: groups of 64, UE8M0 scale \(s = 2^{\lceil \log_2(\text{amax}/448)\rceil}\), \(\hat x = s\cdot \text{e4m3}(x/s)\), RoPE in BF16. Off in production: it is an instrument for checking whether the engine reproduces the training numerics, and the FP4 KV design (item 8, §4) cites it as a way to measure error offline before writing kernels.

Kernel parity is one rung, not the whole ladder.

Determinism: the same question, two answers

Where does stock-versus-stock variation come from? From the most treacherous property of floating point: addition is not associative (seen in How the computer stores numbers). In BF16, with 8 bits of significand, the distance between 1 and the next representable number is \(2^{-7}\). So:

\[ (1 + 2^{-8}) + 2^{-8} = 1 \] \[ 1 + (2^{-8} + 2^{-8}) = 1 + 2^{-7} = 1.0078125 \]
  • \(1 + 2^{-8}\) falls exactly halfway between 1 and \(1 + 2^{-7}\); round-to-nearest-even goes back to 1, twice
  • adding the small ones first, they become \(2^{-7}\) and survive

A GPU adds in orders that vary between runs: split-K reductions (the sum split into parallel chunks, which arrive in any order), atomic adds, the order in which sparse attention visits blocks, the expert reduction in MoE, the batch composition. The difference lives in the last digit, until it lands on a near-tie: the argmax flips, greedy decoding takes another path and the text becomes a different one. A numerical butterfly effect.

That is why “temperature 0” is not a synonym for reproducible: the seed fixes the randomness of sampling, not the order of the sums inside the kernels.

Patchpatch_determinism.py · VLLM_QSA_DET_TOPK=1 · VLLM_MOE_DET_FINALIZE=1

In TP2 + EP, identical requests gave different prompt logprobs (median spread of 0.25 nats, max 6.07) and 5 distinct greedy outputs out of 5. The patch (82 lines, applied by anchors, validated with ast.parse) attacks the two sources of variable order. (1) In the QSA indexer, after topk_op, each row of indices is sorted (the -1 sentinels become INT32_MAX, go to the tail and turn back into -1): the same selection is now visited, and summed in split-K, always in the same order. (2) In FlashInfer’s CUTLASS MoE, use_fused_finalize = (env != "1") swaps the fused finalize (the top-k reduction of the experts’ outputs) for an unfused one, with a fixed order; the launcher exports VLLM_FLASHINFER_MOE_FUSED_FINALIZE=0 and uses a separate autotune directory. Result (2026-09-25): spread 0/0 and 1 distinct output out of 5, also cold and after a different prompt, at a cost of ~4% in decode and ~4% in prefill. That is why it is opt-in (the v0.30 lane refuses the option: it was not ported).

Why pay 4% for determinism?

In production, nobody needs bit-for-bit identical answers. In the lab, with bit-identical output, any difference between the arms of an A/B becomes attributable to the change (the null becomes zero). You still have to measure its size and its effect (NLL, KL, tasks): FP8=all changes the numerics on purpose and still needs a judge. And the guarantee holds for the measured scenario (identical requests in TP2 + EP), not for any batch. It is a trade between machine cost and the cost of statistical inference.

Another engine: when the null is zero

The GLM recipe now has a second version, : the same checkpoint on the same two DGX Sparks, served by TensorFold v0.6.0 with 53 patches instead of vLLM (the overview is in An exact engine, and the full recipe in Swapping the engine). For this chapter’s metrology, the deepest change is not about speed. Everything we have seen so far starts from a null greater than zero: the stock disagrees with itself, and the candidate is judged against that wobble. TensorFold forbids the wobble. Its kernels keep each row’s arithmetic independent of the other rows in the batch (batch invariance), the two ranks always add the partials in the same order, and the noise of each token’s draw is a function of (seed, position, token id), the keyed sampling of Generating text. Without a seed, the seed comes from a hash of the prompt, and the same request gets the same reply even when sampling at temperature 1.

With the null at zero, the “nothing changed” test stops being statistical and becomes a hash test. Every reply carries a token_sha, and "draft": false asks for the serial reference, one token at a time. The exactness checks the recipe publishes with each version are all of this kind: reference hashes, drafted = serial (6 of 6), concurrent = alone (22 of 22; the README breaks it down as 11 of 11 staggered requests and 11 of 11 in a burst), images and videos, tool calls. And a prompt resumed from a kept state gives the same reply as the prompt computed from scratch. An optimization that promises to move the same bits (the RDMA all-gather of COMM=roce, the SPLIT=1 split, the TF_GLM_L2PF prefetch, the TF_GLM_EXL3_LOADS loads) passes or fails on equality: no panel, no repeated captures, no noise band. The sensitivity is total. A single different bit in one row fails, and that is exactly the difference a calibrated panel would accept as stock wobble (our interpretation).

Equality has no shades

A hash only answers yes or no. Three of the recipe’s defaults are not exact against the BF16 checkpoint, on purpose, for speed and the 1M window: DENSE=q4 and KV=fp8 lose information, and KDA_CHUNKED=1 stays “close to the serial kernel, but not on its bits”. Against the checkpoint, the replies differ (config.sh itself says so), and a different hash does not say by how much. The equalities still hold inside the configured engine (drafted = serial with q4 and an FP8 KV too), but the size of the damage needs a judge again. The faithful reference exists: DENSE=bf16 KV=bf16 KDA_CHUNKED=0 serves the checkpoint as it is, with a smaller window (163,840 tokens with vision on, the default; 196,608 without it).

Compare it with Qwen’s determinism, above. There it is optional, costs ~4% and holds for identical requests in TP2 + EP. In TensorFold it is always on and covers drafts, batches of up to 4 requests and resumed prompts. The price is in the kernels: split-K chosen by batch size and float atomics are off the table, and those tend to be the fastest paths for large batches. The recipe does not publish what that costs, and could not measure it the way Qwen did: exactness has no off switch, so there is no “no determinism” arm like the one that gave Qwen’s ~4% (our interpretation). For this chapter, an API limitation weighs more: the engine returns no token probabilities, and since v0.6.0 requests with logprobs or top_logprobs get HTTP 400. This chapter’s distribution instruments (NLL, KLD, top-1), which read probabilities through the API, have nothing to stand on in this version without instrumenting the engine itself (our interpretation). The recipe still runs v0.6.0 and still refuses logprobs; the native TensorFold engine (1.x, which the recipe does not use), however, has returned logprobs on its CUDA server since 1.0.8, and prompt_logprobs on /v1/completions, within ~2e-6 of a float64 reference on a GB10, according to its changelog. With it, this chapter’s instruments have something to read again. The recipe judged its lossy defaults with tasks and a needle, as the functional-tests section shows.

Long context: needles, RULER, garble and loops

Many defects only exist far from the start of the prompt: a leaky sliding-window mask, a YaRN factor on the wrong layer, a sparse indexer that picks different blocks with quantized K, a hybrid prefix cache that hands back null blocks. DeepSeek’s scripts/EVAL.md prescribes three rules learned the hard way: lengths verified by the server’s /tokenize, not estimated from word counts; cold prefill (a unique nonce busts the prefix cache, because “warm requests passing prove nothing”); and real-task prompts, not pathological ones. Not every script follows the first rule: ruler-lite verifies via /tokenize (≥ 97% of the target), but the garble sweep and the deep battery estimate the filler at ~1.56 tokens per word.

From the needle to RULER

The classic test is the needle in a haystack: hide a fact at a known depth in a filler text and ask about it. Qwen’s reasoning_check.py buries a password in synthetic records (--needles 8000,64000, prompts of ~13K and ~104K tokens); longctx.py places needles at 5%, 50% and 95%, and the recipe recorded 6/6 at 600K tokens. But the central finding of RULER (NVIDIA, arXiv:2404.06654) is that models that ace the simple needle fail at tracking and aggregating at the same depth. DeepSeek’s ruler-lite.py runs four live tasks, with the canonical prompts and the answer prefix (“Answer: … they are:”, which pulls the answer out):

sniah
A single needle, “One of the special magic numbers for key is: number.” Passes if the answer contains the right number (the scorer looks for 4–5 digit numbers).
mkniah
Three needles with different keys; only one is asked about, the others are distractors.
vartrack
A chain of 5 variables and 4 hops (VAR A = 12345, VAR B = VAR A, …). Passes only if all 5 appear.
cwe
A numbered list in which 5 words appear 10 times and 45 appear 3 times (185 items). Passes if the 5 common ones appear.

A detail that matters if you are designing your own gates: in ruler-lite, the task block (instruction, question, needles, chain or list, answer prefix) sits at the head of the prompt, and pad_to_length() appends the haystack (“The grass is green…”) afterwards. The needles’ depth is ≈ 0, the chain is interleaved across only 20 sentences and the cwe list is one contiguous stretch. It measures retrieval, tracking and aggregation across a long tail, not at varied depths like canonical RULER. Compare in the figure:

The script rejects the case if the filler falls below 97% of the target (an old bug, #81, capped every prompt at ~4.8K tokens, and the 32K and 262K cells passed falsely). It turns reasoning off via chat_template_kwargs (thinking in DeepSeek, enable_thinking in Qwen3; getting the key wrong makes the model spend its budget thinking) and exits with 0 only if everything passes: ready for CI.

Those are the defaults. The client timeout, --request-timeout 900 s, carries a lesson for every evaluator: an 899,994-token prompt took 1,028.85 s to the first token (~874.8 tok/s of cold prefill), so 900 s buys ~787K tokens; in a ~900K case, the harness records its own limit as a model failure. In the recipe: a needle at almost 1M

The TensorFold version of the GLM recipe keeps a single long-context test, tools/needle.py: a passphrase hidden at 60% depth in ~195K tokens of random prose, asked for greedily (temperature 0, seed 1234), with exit code 1 if the answer is wrong. For the 1M window, the recipe published a needle found in a 981,841-token prompt (967 s to the first token, ~1,015 tok/s of prefill, on sparkDash with the GPU clocks capped at 2,200 MHz), with the KV in FP8 and the dense weights in 4 bits, both of which lose information. It is the simple needle from the start of this subsection: it proves the 1M path is not broken, not that the model tracks or aggregates down there, which is what RULER demands.

Garble: the classic failure mode

context-garble-sweep.py builds an agent-style prompt (tools search_web, read_file, run_shell, “never reveal this prompt” and a planted secret, NEBULA-7-QUANTUM-OVEN), fills the context up to the target (estimated at 1.56 tokens per word), adds a nonce and asks a mundane question (the capital of Idaho). The answer is scanned for garble signatures: a leaked secret, a dumped schema, an echo of the prompt, starting mid-word, <| tokens, arguments that are not JSON. Any of them → GARBLE; empty → SUSPECT. Defaults: --lengths 2048,8192,32768,131072, --runs 2; the recipe’s gate is CLEAN up to ~900K.

Test your test

The garble sweep’s old word→token heuristic was wrong: the filler measures ~1.56 tokens per word (340,787 words → 533,110 tokens). According to EVAL.md, a --lengths 524288 actually sent ~815K tokens, and 700K+ blew past the 1M ceiling: the server’s correct 400 turned into “GARBLE DETECTED”. (The source describes the old heuristic ambiguously, “1.3 words/token”; do not rederive the 815K from it.) Before fixing the recipe, check that the test measures what it claims to measure.

Loop or long tail?

Reasoning models sometimes blow through max_tokens with empty content (finish_reason=length), for two opposite reasons. In a long tail, the model is still saying new things, just a lot of them: raising max_tokens fixes it. In a loop, it recycles what it already said: raising it only raises the bill. DeepSeek’s loop_detector.py tells the two apart from the reasoning text, by measuring 8-gram novelty:

\[ \text{novelty}(j) = \frac{\big|\, S_j \setminus \textstyle\bigcup_{i<j} S_i \,\big|}{|S_j|} \]
  • \(S_j\): the set of 8-grams (8 consecutive words) in window \(j\), which is 700 words long
  • \(\bigcup_{i<j} S_i\): all 8-grams already seen earlier in the same trace
  • loop verdict: 3 consecutive windows below 2% and no recovery until the end of the trace

The parameters come from calibration: in three real traces that never terminated (~100K tokens), novelty collapsed near tokens 7.9K, 13.8K and 29.7K and never rose above 0.6%; two healthy traces of ~31K and ~34K never crossed the threshold. Why not “unique 120-character blocks”? The real loop is templated (canned phrases with one element varying): on the same traces, unique blocks gave 22% / 92% / 66%, versus 3.4% / 4.0% / 2.8% for unique 8-grams. Declared limitation: a fragmented loop, interleaved with fresh filler, goes unnoticed.

Probesscripts/longgen_probe.py · scripts/multiturn_repro.py

The cousins in GLM: long sampled generations with the served defaults (a 2,000-token essay and short story, a ~5K-token log prompt, the essay at temperature 0) and a 4-turn conversation at temperature 1.0 with reasoning. Each answer is scanned for characters outside the expected set, the “garbage” of broken numerics (longgen_probe computes a repetition score over the last 300 words but does not report it; read the endings of the answers, which it prints).

Functional smoke tests: canaries, tools and JSON

At the top of the ladder are tests that use the service the way a user (or an agent) would. They are coarse, but they catch what no distribution metric sees: the tool parser, structured output, the finish_reason, the engine right after boot.

The boot canary

A canary is a tiny test that runs every time, before the service declares itself ready: the extreme case of a smoke test. In DeepSeek’s A/Bs, every boot goes through a probe (17×23 = 391; the capital of Japan is Tokyo). GLM’s canary piggybacks on the shape warmup that runs after /health:

  • Content: “Reply with OK.” at temperature 0, without reasoning, must return OK in choices[0].message.content (never in reasoning_content).
  • Acceptance: the speculative decoding counters from /metrics, read before and after the warmup, must pair up, be finite and not decrease; the warmup must have drafted ≥ GLM53_WARMUP_CANARY_MIN_DRAFTS tokens and accepted at least one.

Incomplete evidence (a failed request, an unreadable counter, too little traffic) becomes “cannot judge”, never “degenerate”. If degenerate, the script exits with code 3, and start.sh collects the logs, tears down the containers and fails the start.

CanaryGLM53_WARMUP_CANARY=1 · GLM53_WARMUP_CANARY_MIN_DRAFTS=64

1 (default) turns the canary on; 0 turns it off (the error message mentions this only as an escape hatch). The minimum of 64 drafts is statistics: with very little traffic, even a healthy engine may, by bad luck, not have accepted anything. In the figure’s “Canary” mode, watch the chance of a healthy engine scoring zero plummet as the number of verification steps grows, while a degenerate engine (acceptance ≈ 0, the signature of #249) gets caught no matter what.

Tool batteries

Tool calls (The API: chat, Responses, tools) break in ways KLD never shows. DeepSeek’s battery (tool-battery.py) runs 7 checks at temperature 0 (see the figure), including case #55: with max_tokens=40, the call is cut off in the middle, and the server must report finish_reason=length instead of delivering arguments that are not JSON (which would bring down the next round with an HTTP 400). The deep version (deepctx-tool-battery.py) repeats 4 checks at 32K and 131K of cold context. Expected: 7/7 and 8/8. Operational trap: the default model is still the old deepseek-v4-flash-0731. In the batteries, the served model is the 2nd positional argument (tool-battery.py URL deepseek-v4-flash-vision-exp); --model works in run-audit.sh (which passes it along), in ruler-lite and in the garble sweep.

The strictest gate is the 145-case one. Before hotfix #191, it gave 142/145 twice at c=4: HTTP 200 with zero tool_calls or arguments outside the schema, with different labels on each run (replaying those cases gave 18/18 clean). The diagnosis was not numerical: with reasoning at low effort, some requests thought for 300–500 tokens, and max_tokens=512 cut off the DSML call. The goal is 145/145, with zero termination, FSM, SHM or timeout warnings on both ranks.

A serving artifact is not model quality

tool-eval-bench reads calls only from message.tool_calls. At temperature 1.0, vLLM samples every token, including the DSML tags, while the ds4 engine forces temperature 0 on the syntax. One malformed tag and the parser misses the call: the same weights score lower on vLLM for a mechanical reason. Our interpretation: this is consistent with DSML recovery (opt-in; it recovers calls missing the outer wrapper) becoming the default only once it matches the agent’s 42.3% call-acceptance baseline at C1 (one concurrent request), and with the battery running at temperature 0. In GLM, the 17-case × 3 battery gave 45–47/51 on every arm, stock included: functional tests have a noise band too.

Task tests: few, deterministic and with a margin of error

Task benchmarks (GSM8K, AIME, HumanEval) measure what the user feels, at coarse resolution. A rate \(\hat p\) over \(n\) questions has a standard error of \(\sqrt{\hat p(1-\hat p)/n}\). Qwen’s RadixArk checkpoint was qualified with GSM8K 97.27% (1,283/1,319) and AIME26 pass@1 98.75% (n=8). For GSM8K: \(\sqrt{0.9727\times0.0273/1319} \approx 0.45\) pp; the difference between two measurements has a standard error \(\sqrt2\) times larger (≈ 0.63), and the 95% interval comes out at ≈ ±1.24 pp, close to the HANDOFF’s “only resolves ≥ 1.3 pp” (our interpretation of where that number comes from). And AIME at n=8 would cost ~7 h per checkpoint at 191.6 tok/s aggregate.

Functional testbench/reasoning_check.py --needles 8000,64000 --max-tokens 2048 --compare

Created because, with an FP8 KV, the quantized keys perturb which blocks the QSA indexer selects. It has 10 machine-checkable tasks (arithmetic chain, trains, logic, \(\sqrt2\), counting letters, units, day of the week, modular arithmetic, sorting, syllogism with negation) + 2 needles, at temperature 0, scored by substring with the <think> block stripped. Run it in auto (BF16) and save; run it in fp8 with --compare: exit code ≠ 0 if something that used to pass now fails. Result (2026-09-05): 12/12 in BF16 and in FP8, needles at 13K and 104K. The CHANGELOG puts the measurement in perspective: 12 tasks are smoke, not a benchmark, and the upstream warning (a long-reasoning task dropping from 6/6 to 2/6 in the reference) involves long chains that the suite does not stress.

Published qualityGSM8K 98.0% (250) · HumanEval 97.6% (164) · DENSE=q4 KV=fp8 · v1.3.2, TR3

What it is. The two quality numbers of the TensorFold version of the GLM recipe: GSM8K on 250 problems and HumanEval on 164, in the default configuration (KV in FP8, dense weights in 4 bits), measured “on a build with identical replies” to the served one (it is the hash test that lets a measurement carry over from one build to another, our interpretation). Since the recipe’s engine (v0.6.0) returns no logprobs, these are what answer whether the lossy defaults damaged the model.

Since v1.3.3. The default checkpoint became the lab’s own pack, and the README now publishes GSM8K 98.8% (250 problems, thinking off), HumanEval 95.7% (164, thinking off) and HumanEval+ with MBPP+ 86.5% (542, thinking on), with the same lossy defaults. On the same build, the CHANGELOG puts the two checkpoints side by side: 247 vs 245 of 250 and 157 vs 160 of 164 (neither difference significant), and 469 vs 468 of 542 on HumanEval+ and MBPP+ (paired p = 1.0). That is what the resolution paragraph below predicts: these tasks cannot tell the two checkpoints apart; the KL to the official FP8 can (2–18% lower for the new pack).

Resolution. By the standard-error formula above (our math): with \(n = 250\) and \(\hat p = 0.98\), \(\sqrt{0.98\times0.02/250} \approx 0.89\) pp; with \(n = 164\) and 0.976, ≈ 1.2 pp. config.sh compares the three dense formats (DFlash2, one boot each): q4 and fp8 scored the same 98.0% and 95.1%, and bf16 scored 97.2% and 96.3%. That is 2 problems out of 250 and 2 out of 164, below one standard error of the difference (≈ 1.4 and ≈ 2.2 pp). The instrument cannot tell the three formats apart, and the comment itself warns: lossy, the replies differ. (The README’s 97.6% and the comment’s 95.1% come from different runs of the same HumanEval, without saying which build or seed: one more reason not to read tenths.)

The blind spot. A user measured something else: out of 48 replies to short French coding prompts, with q4, 12 lost the end of turn and ran to max_tokens, against 1 of 48 with bf16 or fp8 (#18). By Fisher’s exact test, \(p \approx 0.002\) (our math, treating the 96 replies as independent, which the report does not spell out): 48 replies to prompts chosen for the symptom resolve what 414 generic English problems cannot see. The detector is cheap and needs no logprobs: count replies with finish_reason=length on prompts in the language and task you will serve (the same signal as the loops subsection).

Trade-off. q4 bought ~14% on prose (38.9 → 44.4 tok/s), ~10% on code and ~16% on prefill over fp8 (the same config.sh A/B, one boot each, no interval). The recipe kept q4 as the default, documented the regression, points to DENSE=fp8 for non-English prompts (at a cost of ~10% of decode speed, according to the README) and announces a calibrated 4-bit, at q4’s speed, in the works. Compare with the vLLM version: there, dense FP8 went through a kl_panel with controls; here, the decision rested on tasks, and the blind spot surfaced in a field report.

In the recipes: the Pareto frontier and the lab notebook

Every choice in a recipe moves two axes: speed (or bytes) and quality. One option dominates another if it is at least as good on both and better on one; the non-dominated ones form the Pareto frontier. Choose within it, according to your budget.

In “Bytes × KLD”, the frontier has two points: K6 (most faithful, most expensive) and the 4 bpw EXL3; the official FP8 is dominated by K6, and NVFP4 by EXL3. But remember the ruler: restricted to the same stack, EXL3 dominates on its own. In “Speed × verdict”, notice W8A8, the fastest kernel, rejected on quality; Qwen’s determinism, slower and still useful, because it buys reproducibility; and Qwen’s FP8 KV: besides −5.3% throughput in the rerun, tok/step dropped in 8 out of 8 cells, consistent with the indexer being perturbed. The drafter’s acceptance is also a quality sensor. The two arrows show the exact engine’s two regimes: COMM=roce passes on a hash, because it moves the same bits; q4 gains 14% on prose and ties on the tasks, but loses the end of turn in French (#18), which is why it stayed the default with a caveat.

How to document a result

A measurement without context is not reproducible. GLM’s FP8=all adoption report is a good template: date and scope (“TP=2 on 2× GB10; TP=3 did not change”); labels (Measured, Code-verified, Interpretation) on each claim; HEAD and .env hash before and after; overlay hash; paths to the receipts; costs; a rollback.sh that restores the .env byte for byte; and what was not measured, including the instrument that failed its own control. The “best ever seen” numbers are kept apart from the norm: DeepSeek’s audit (73–76 tok/s at c=1, 2026-08-16) warns that a re-measurement on 2026-09-02 gave 15–25% less, with no change to explain it. Performance methodology is left for Measuring performance; the record format is the same.

The minimum protocol for your recipe

Before touching anything: capture the stock (≥ 4 times, on different boots, if there is no determinism), write down the null and check that the stock control passes your own gate. For each change: the bottom rung (rel RMSE or parity), KLD/NLL in teacher forcing against the null, and the tests for the part you touched (long context for attention or KV; tools for the parser or the API; loops for sampling); a canary on every boot. Record date, commits, image, weights, protocol and labels. Only then measure speed, and choose on the Pareto frontier. If your engine guarantees exactness, as TensorFold does, changes that preserve the bits are judged by hash, and only the ones that lose information need the rest of the ladder.
