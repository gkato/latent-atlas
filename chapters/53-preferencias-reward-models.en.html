After SFT, the model knows how to imitate demonstrations. But for a lot of things, it is easier to recognize a better answer than to write it. Almost nobody drafts the perfect summary of a paper, yet any attentive reader can point out which of two summaries is more faithful. Preference-based post-training exploits this asymmetry in two stages. First, people (or other models) compare responses. Then a reward model (RM) learns to turn those comparisons into a number \(r(x,y)\) that an optimizer can push up. This chapter builds that number from scratch: why compare instead of score, how to collect preferences and measure annotator agreement, where the Bradley–Terry model comes from, what the RM loss is and how its gradient behaves, what the architecture looks like inside, how to calibrate, and what happens when the reward starts lying (length bias, reward hacking, and over-optimization). At the end come process rewards, AI judges, and RLAIF. This is the raw material for everything that follows: PPO, GRPO, and DPO.

Why compare instead of score

Training with preferences requires a function \(r(x,y)\): it takes a prompt \(x\) and a response \(y\) and returns a scalar that says how good the response is. Nobody writes this function by hand. It is learned from human judgments, and the first design decision is what kind of judgment to ask for. The answer from the literature, ever since the first RLHF, has been the same: ask for comparisons.

  • Christiano et al. 2017, the work that kicked off RL from human preferences, showed the annotator two 1–2 s clips of an agent and asked which one was better. The rationale is explicit: the authors found it much easier to get consistent comparisons than consistent absolute scores. In continuous control, predicting comparisons worked much better than predicting scores. Each query took 3–5 s, and humans gave feedback on less than 1% of the agent's interactions.
  • Ziegler et al. 2019, the first RLHF on a language model (GPT-2), reports that annotators struggled to make fine, consistent quantitative distinctions when asked for an absolute number. Stiennon et al. 2020 (summarization) sticks with comparisons.
  • The counterpoint: HelpSteer (Wang et al. 2023) rated each response independently, on a 0–4 Likert scale, because the number of comparisons grows quadratically with the number of responses per prompt. Absolute scores scale better.

The intuition (our interpretation, supported by these reports): every annotator carries a personal ruler. One is generous and gives a 6 to almost everything, another is strict and rarely goes above 4, and a third uses only the middle of the scale. An absolute score mixes the quality of the response with the ruler of whoever rated it. A comparison cancels the ruler: if the generous one gives 6 and 5 and the strict one gives 4 and 3, both agree that A is better. The figure simulates exactly that.

Look at the numbers, keeping in mind that the two bars do not start from the same baseline. With identical rulers, score agreement is already lower (~65%: hitting the same number out of 7 is harder than hitting the direction) and direction agreement sits near 85%. As the rulers drift apart, score agreement plummets (~16% at 1.0) and direction agreement barely moves (~82%). Comparison is not “more precise”. It throws away the absolute level, which is exactly the part annotators do not share, and keeps the difference, which is the part they do share. The price is losing magnitude: a pair where A is “a little better” becomes the same bit as a pair where A is “much better”. That is why modern datasets combine both. HelpSteer2 has per-attribute Likert scores and HelpSteer2-Preference adds preferences with strength; UltraFeedback gives 1–5 scores that are later converted into pairs.

Analogy: the blind tasting

Ask ten people to rate a wine from 0 to 10 and you will get ten scattered numbers, because each person has their own “7”. Serve two wines side by side and ask which is better: each person compares within their own ruler, and the answers tend to converge much more. It is the same bet RLHF makes.

Collecting preferences: prompts, policies, and annotators

A preference pair has three ingredients: a prompt, two (or more) responses, and a judgment. Each one hides decisions that change the final RM.

  • Prompts. InstructGPT used real API prompts (filtered for personal data, deduplicated, at most 200 per user, with the train and validation splits made by user) plus prompts written by the labelers themselves to bootstrap the process. In the API, 45.6% were generation, 12.4% open QA, and 11.2% brainstorming. Anthropic's HH-RLHF used open-ended dialogues with the model; in the harmlessness set, the annotator picked the more harmful response (red teaming), a choice the authors themselves consider probably not ideal for teaching good behavior.
  • Responses. To cover the space well, responses come from varied sources. Llama 2 sampled both from different model variants, at different temperatures. UltraFeedback drew 4 from a pool of 17 models and also injected different “principles” into the prompt. HelpSteer2 generated only 2 per prompt, to reduce the annotator's cognitive load.
  • Which policy the samples come from. In InstructGPT, most pairs came from the supervised policies, with a share coming from the PPO policies. Anthropic collected in three waves, the last of them online: RMs and RLHF policies were updated on a roughly weekly cadence. Llama 2 says that accumulating new RM data in parallel with model improvements is crucial to keep the RM on-distribution. The reason is simple: after RL, the policy produces text the RM has never seen, and the RM is only reliable where it was trained (section 7).
  • Which pairs to ask about. Christiano 2017 sampled many pairs and sent humans the ones on which the members of an RM ensemble disagreed most. On some tasks this made results worse. Lightman 2023 actively sought out “convincing but wrong” solutions and gained 2.6× data efficiency in its process RM.

Guidelines and annotator management

Agreement numbers depend as much on the people as on the wording of the guideline. InstructGPT hired ~40 labelers, selected through a screening test with a “soft” cutoff of 75% agreement with the researchers. The criteria were helpful, truthful, harmless, but with an important asymmetry: during training, helpfulness was prioritized; in the final evaluation, truthfulness and harmlessness. Llama 2 wrote separate guidelines for helpfulness and safety, because the two criteria contradict each other. In HH-RLHF, ~20 workers produced ~80% of the data. In HelpSteer2, each response had at least 3 annotators (average of 3.41; two more were brought in when helpfulness scores diverged by more than 2), and about 50% of the annotations were discarded in quality control.

Percent agreement is misleading, because part of it happens by chance. If both annotators choose “A better” 90% of the time, they will agree on ~82% of the items even when picking at random. Cohen's κ discounts that chance:

\[ \kappa = \frac{p_o - p_e}{1 - p_e}, \qquad p_e = \sum_k p^{(A)}_k\, p^{(B)}_k \]
  • \(p_o\): observed agreement (fraction of items with the same label)
  • \(p_e\): agreement expected by chance, given each annotator's marginals
  • \(p^{(A)}_k\): fraction of the items that annotator A labeled as class \(k\)
  • \(\kappa = 1\): perfect agreement; \(\kappa = 0\): nothing beyond chance

For ordinal scales (Likert, −3…+3), disagreeing between 0 and 4 is much worse than disagreeing between 0 and 1. The quadratic weighted κ takes that into account:

\[ \kappa_w = 1 - \frac{\sum_{i,j} w_{ij}\, O_{ij}}{\sum_{i,j} w_{ij}\, E_{ij}}, \qquad w_{ij} = \frac{(i-j)^2}{(k-1)^2} \]
  • \(O_{ij}\): proportion of items labeled \(i\) by annotator A and \(j\) by B
  • \(E_{ij}\): what would be expected by chance (product of the marginals)
  • \(w_{ij}\): disagreement weight; 0 on the diagonal, 1 in the opposite corner
  • \(k\): number of levels in the scale

That is why HelpSteer2 chose the quadratic κ. With ~1,000 annotators, the same two rarely share samples, which rules out Fleiss' κ and Krippendorff's α. Play with the math in the figure: the binary example has 75% raw agreement, but a κ of only 0.50.

Human noise can also be built into the model. Christiano 2017 assumed that 10% of the time the human answers at random. The predicted probability becomes \(\hat P = 0.9\,\sigma(\Delta) + 0.05\), which stays between 5% and 95%: a wrong label costs at most \(-\ln 0.05 \approx 3.0\), and its gradient vanishes when the RM is confident in the opposite direction (the model attributes the error to chance).

Why this matters

An RM with 65–70% accuracy on HH or TL;DR is not necessarily bad: humans themselves agree only ~63–77% of the time. Stiennon's 6.7B RM reaches 66.5% on CNN/DM, almost equal to the 66.9% between labelers. The quality test for an RM is not “did it get 95%?”, but “did it get close to human agreement, and does it generalize to where the policy is going?”.

Bradley–Terry: from comparisons to a scale

We have pairs “\(y_w\) beat \(y_l\)”. We want one number \(r(x,y)\) per response. The bridge is a probabilistic model that says what \(P(y_w \succ y_l)\) should be given \(r\). The classic construction assumes each response has a noisy latent utility, and the human picks the one with the highest utility at that moment:

\[ u(x,y) = r(x,y) + \epsilon_y, \qquad y_1 \succ y_2 \iff u_1 > u_2 \iff \epsilon_2 - \epsilon_1 < r_1 - r_2 \]
  • \(r(x,y)\): the “true quality” the RM wants to learn
  • \(\epsilon_y\): i.i.d. judgment noise (mood, attention, interpretation)
  • \(y_1 \succ y_2\): “\(y_1\) is preferred to \(y_2\)”

Everything hinges on the noise distribution. If \(\epsilon\) follows a Gumbel(0,1), the difference of two independent Gumbels is a logistic, whose cumulative distribution function is the sigmoid (the three-line derivation is in the box just below). Result:

\[ P(y_1 \succ y_2 \mid x) = \sigma\big(r(x,y_1) - r(x,y_2)\big) = \frac{e^{r_1}}{e^{r_1} + e^{r_2}} \]
  • \(\sigma(z) = 1/(1+e^{-z})\): logistic sigmoid
  • \(e^{r_i}\): the “strength” \(p_i\) of response \(i\), as in Bradley & Terry (1952): \(P(i\succ j) = p_i/(p_i+p_j)\)
  • \(\Delta = r_1 - r_2\): the log-odds of the preference
Why two Gumbels give a sigmoid

For Gumbel(0,1), the CDF is \(F(\epsilon)=e^{-e^{-\epsilon}}\) and the density is \(f(\epsilon)=e^{-\epsilon}F(\epsilon)\). With \(\Delta = r_1 - r_2\), response \(y_1\) wins when \(\epsilon_2 < \epsilon_1 + \Delta\). Fix \(\epsilon_1 = \epsilon\), use \(F\) for the chance that \(\epsilon_2\) lands below, and integrate over \(\epsilon\): \[ P(y_1\succ y_2)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(\epsilon)\,F(\epsilon+\Delta)\,d\epsilon \] \[ =\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-\epsilon}\,e^{-e^{-\epsilon}(1+e^{-\Delta})}\,d\epsilon \] The second step merges the two exponents, because \(F(\epsilon+\Delta)=e^{-e^{-\epsilon}e^{-\Delta}}\). Substitute \(u=e^{-\epsilon}\), with \(du=-e^{-\epsilon}d\epsilon\) (as \(\epsilon\) goes from \(-\infty\) to \(\infty\), \(u\) goes from \(\infty\) to 0): \[ =\int_0^\infty e^{-u(1+e^{-\Delta})}\,du=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\Delta}}=\sigma(\Delta) \] It is the sigmoid, exactly (calculation).

This is the Bradley–Terry model (BT). If the noise is normal, you get the Thurstone model (1927), with \(P = \Phi\Big(\dfrac{r_1-r_2}{\sqrt2\,s}\Big)\), where \(\Phi\) is the normal CDF and \(s\) is the standard deviation of the noise. The two curves are nearly identical when the noises have the same variance. The logistic wins in practice because its gradient is simple and it matches the softmax that LLMs already use. Christiano 2017 notes that it is the same Luce–Shepard choice rule and that it is “analogous to Elo in chess”.

Elo is the same BT written in another base: \(P(A\succ B) = 1/\big(1+10^{-(R_A-R_B)/400}\big)\). Setting it equal to \(\sigma(\Delta r)\):

\[ \Delta r = \frac{\ln 10}{400}\,\Delta R_{\text{Elo}} \approx 0.005756\,\Delta R_{\text{Elo}} \]
  • \(\Delta R_{\text{Elo}}\): Elo rating difference; \(\Delta r\): BT reward difference
  • +100 Elo ⇔ \(\Delta r = 0.5756\) ⇔ 64.01% win rate; +400 Elo ⇔ \(\Delta r = 2.3026\) ⇔ 90.91%

That is why Chatbot Arena and Anthropic report models in Elo. The LMArena leaderboard, by the way, does not use online Elo (which depends on the order of the battles): it fits a Bradley–Terry model by maximum likelihood over all battles and only then reports the result on an Elo-like scale.

Rankings of K responses: Plackett–Luce and the InstructGPT pairs

InstructGPT did not ask for pairs: it asked the labeler to rank \(K=4\) to \(9\) responses. The natural model for a ranking \(\tau\) is Plackett–Luce (PL): 1st place is chosen by a softmax over all of them, 2nd place by a softmax over those remaining, and so on. The softmax also falls out of noisy utilities: the same calculation as in the box above, with \(K\) Gumbels, shows that the chance of \(y_i\) having the highest utility is \(e^{r_i}/\sum_j e^{r_j}\), a softmax; sorting all of them by utility gives exactly Plackett–Luce.

\[ P(\tau \mid x) = \prod_{i=1}^{K} \frac{\exp\big(r(x, y_{\tau(i)})\big)}{\sum_{j=i}^{K} \exp\big(r(x, y_{\tau(j)})\big)} \]
  • \(\tau(i)\): index of the response placed at position \(i\)
  • the denominator sums only over the responses not yet chosen
  • with \(K=2\), PL becomes Bradley–Terry; the 1st factor alone is Ziegler 2019's “best of \(K\)” model

InstructGPT, however, did not use PL. It decomposed each ranking into all \(\binom{K}{2}\) pairs and applied BT to each one:

\[ \mathcal L(\theta) = -\frac{1}{\binom{K}{2}}\,\mathbb E_{(x,y_w,y_l)\sim D}\Big[\log\sigma\big(r_\theta(x,y_w) - r_\theta(x,y_l)\big)\Big] \]
  • \(\binom{K}{2}\): number of pairs in a ranking of \(K\); from 6 (\(K=4\)) to 36 (\(K=9\))
  • \(r_\theta\): the RM; \(y_w\) is the higher-ranked response of the pair

The decisive engineering detail is that all pairs from a prompt go into the same batch element. Pairs from the same prompt are highly correlated. Shuffled into an ordinary dataset, each response would take part in \(K-1\) separate updates, and the RM overfit in a single pass. Grouped, it takes only \(K\) RM forwards per prompt (each response goes through once) instead of \(2\binom{K}{2}\), and validation accuracy and log loss got “much better”. A batch of 64 prompts carries up to \(64\times36 = 2{,}304\) comparisons. Ties were discarded.

The two numbers differ because they measure different things. The pair average is a composite likelihood (interpretation): it treats the pairs as if they were independent, which they are not, but it is consistent under BT and much simpler. PL is the joint likelihood of the whole ordering. Both are minimized by pushing the scores into the right order, and both use only \(K\) forwards.

The RM loss and its gradient

With BT in hand, training the RM is maximum likelihood: choose the parameters \(\phi\) that make the observed judgments as likely as possible. The negative log-likelihood of a dataset of pairs is the canonical loss of every modern RM:

\[ \mathcal L_{\text{RM}}(\phi) = -\,\mathbb E_{(x, y_w, y_l)\sim\mathcal D}\Big[\log \sigma\big(r_\phi(x,y_w) - r_\phi(x,y_l)\big)\Big] \]
  • \(r_\phi\): the RM with parameters \(\phi\)
  • \(y_w\), \(y_l\): preferred (chosen) and dispreferred (rejected) response
  • \(\mathcal D\): the dataset of pairs; \(\sigma\): sigmoid

Look again: it is the binary cross-entropy from chapter 1, with label 1 (“\(y_w\) wins”) applied to the logit \(\Delta = r_w - r_l\). The RM is a binary classifier whose logit is a difference of two outputs of the same network. The gradient explains almost all of the training behavior:

\[ \frac{\partial \mathcal L}{\partial \Delta} = -\big(1 - \sigma(\Delta)\big) = -\sigma(-\Delta), \qquad \nabla_\phi\mathcal L = -\sigma(-\Delta)\,\big(\nabla_\phi r_w - \nabla_\phi r_l\big) \]
  • \(\sigma(-\Delta)\): the probability the RM itself assigns to being wrong on this pair
  • \(\nabla_\phi r_w\): direction that increases the reward of the preferred response (the gradient raises it)
  • \(-\nabla_\phi r_l\): direction that decreases the reward of the rejected one (the gradient lowers it)

Each pair pulls \(r_w\) up and \(r_l\) down with strength \(\sigma(-\Delta)\). An already well-separated pair (\(\Delta = 3\), \(P = 0.9526\)) contributes a weight of 0.0474. An inverted pair (\(\Delta = -0.8\)) contributes 0.69. A tied pair contributes 0.5, with loss \(\ln 2 = 0.6931\). Training concentrates its effort where the RM is still wrong, and that has a downside: a wrong label keeps pulling with weight close to 1 forever. That is what Christiano's noise model fixes: in the figure's “10% noise” variant, the weight of a badly inverted pair goes to 0. Try it in the figure.

Invariances: why every RM is normalized

The loss only sees differences. Adding a constant to all rewards changes nothing: the RM is translation invariant, and the zero of the scale is arbitrary. That is why every pipeline normalizes the RM before RL. Stiennon adjusts the outputs so that the reference summaries have mean 0. InstructGPT sets a bias so that the labelers' demonstrations have mean 0. Ziegler normalizes to mean 0 and variance 1 under samples from the initial model. The “N+ details” reproduction computes the mean over the SFT references and writes it into the head's bias.

The loss is not scale invariant. Multiplying all rewards by 2 makes the RM more “confident”: the scale works as the temperature of the preference. In RL, it interacts directly with the KL penalty coefficient. Llama 2, for example, applies \(\text{whiten}(\text{logit}(\cdot))\) to the reward to balance it against the KL term (logit is the inverse of the sigmoid: Llama 2's RM output lies in [0,1] and goes back to the log-odds scale; whiten subtracts the mean and divides by the standard deviation over the batch); this comes back in the PPO and RLHF chapter.

Soft labels, ties, and loss variants

When the label is a distribution \(\mu\) rather than a hard 1 (tie → \(\mu = (\tfrac12, \tfrac12)\); an AI labeler that says 60/40), you use the full cross-entropy:

\[ \mathcal L = -\mu(1)\log\hat P(y_1\succ y_2) - \mu(2)\log\hat P(y_2 \succ y_1), \qquad \Delta^* = \log\frac{\mu(1)}{\mu(2)} \]
  • \(\hat P\): the probability predicted by the RM, \(\sigma(\Delta)\)
  • \(\Delta^*\): the optimum of the loss with a soft label; a tie asks for \(\Delta^* = 0\), a 60/40 asks for \(\Delta^* \approx 0.405\)

The variants used in practice change how the strength of the preference gets used:

Llama 2's margins per preference level were, in the “small” version, 1, 2/3, 1/3, and 0 (from significantly better to negligibly better/unsure), and in the “large” one, 3, 2, 1, and 0. The average gain was small (62.5% accuracy without a margin, 63.0% with the small one, and 62.9% with the large one) and showed up mainly on the “significantly better” pairs (79.1 → 80.4 → 80.7). The authors note that the large margin pushes the scores toward a bimodal distribution. In HelpSteer2-Preference, scaled BT was the best variant on RewardBench: 92.7 versus 91.5 for plain BT and for margin, training from scratch; 93.7 starting from the regression model; and 94.1 adding ExPO weight extrapolation. In other words, regression and BT carry complementary information.

Architecture and training: an LLM with a scalar head

A modern RM is the same Transformer from Module 1 with the last layer swapped out. InstructGPT starts from the SFT model, removes the unembedding layer (the lm_head, which projects the hidden state onto the vocabulary), and puts in its place a projection to a single number. Llama 2 initializes its RMs from checkpoints of the chat model itself, so that the RM “knows what the chat model knows”. Stiennon adds a randomly initialized linear head. As a formula:

\[ \begin{gathered} r_\phi(x,y) = w^\top h_L(x,y)_{\text{EOS}} + b \\[4pt] w_i \sim \mathcal N(0,\sigma^2),\quad \sigma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{d_{\text{model}}+1}}\ \ \text{(at initialization)} \end{gathered} \]
  • \(h_L(x,y)_{\text{EOS}} \in \mathbb R^{d_{\text{model}}}\): last-layer hidden state at the position of the EOS token, after reading the whole prompt and response
  • \(w \in \mathbb R^{d_{\text{model}}}\), \(b\): the scalar head; \(b\) is where normalization stores the mean
  • the initialization of \(w\) is the one from the “N+ details” reproduction, which fixes a typo in the original paper; \(\sigma\) is the standard deviation of each entry \(w_i\)
  • why this \(\sigma\): with entries of \(h\) of order 1 (after the final LayerNorm), \(\mathrm{Var}(w^\top h)\approx d_{\text{model}}\,\sigma^2\approx 1\), and the initial reward starts out at unit scale (interpretation)

Why read at EOS? With causal attention, position \(t\) has only seen the tokens up to \(t\). The only position that has read the whole response is the last one, and that is where the score is read. The N+ reproduction insists on it: the reward is extracted only at EOS (the index of the first PAD minus 1); the “reward logits” at the other positions are worthless and come out almost always negative. Reading in the wrong place, at a PAD padding token for example, is a silent bug: training runs, the loss goes down, and the number does not mean what you think. Another detail from the same source: the RM trains with right padding, but in PPO the queries are left-padded for generation, and the masks need to be adjusted.

HelpSteer2's RM projects the end-of-response state onto 5 scalars, one per attribute (helpfulness, correctness, coherence, complexity, verbosity), and trains by regression. A multi-attribute RM lets you choose the combination at optimization time, for example discounting verbosity.

Size, data, and the training recipe

InstructGPT used a single 6B RM for 1.3B, 6B, and 175B policies. A 175B RM did reach lower validation loss, but (1) its training was more unstable, which made it worse as a starting point for PPO's value function, and (2) a 175B RM and critic would greatly increase the cost of PPO. Stiennon used an RM, policy, and critic of the same size. As for scale, Stiennon trained 7 RMs from 160M to 13B on 8k–64k comparisons: doubling the data yielded ~+1.1% validation accuracy, and doubling the model, ~+1.8%. In Gao 2022's setup, with fewer than ~2,000 comparisons the RMs stayed near chance at every size. Askell et al. 2021 also proposed preference model pre-training (PMP) on “cheap” preferences (StackExchange, Reddit, Wikipedia) before the human data, which improves sample efficiency in large models.

Bridge to Module 1: scoring is a prefill

Interpretation: to give a score, the RM does one forward pass over prompt + response and reads one position. There is no sampling and no decode, just a prefill the length of the sequence. In the RL loop, each response generated by the policy costs the RM one prefill, while generating it cost hundreds of decode steps. That is why, in an RLHF pipeline, generation dominates the time (the subject of the PPO chapter), not scoring. A generative judge, which writes a critique before the score (section 9), changes that math: it also decodes.

Evaluating an RM: accuracy, calibration, and RewardBench

The main metric of an RM is pairwise accuracy on a held-out set: the fraction of pairs where \(r(y_w) > r(y_l)\). We have already seen the human ceilings. But there is a second, subtler question: when the RM says “\(P = 0.9\)”, is it right 90% of the time? A BT RM is calibrated if, among the pairs where it predicts \(\sigma(\Delta) = p\), the fraction where the human agrees is \(p\). This matters because, in RL, score differences become reward. An overconfident RM exaggerates small differences; an underconfident one flattens them.

Bai et al. 2022 (Anthropic) plotted accuracy against \(\Delta\) and found the preference models trained only on helpfulness to be “very well calibrated”, and those trained on the helpfulness + harmlessness mix to be “slightly underconfident”. The N+ reproduction redid the plot on TL;DR and found RMs that were “still under-calibrated”. The standard measure of miscalibration is ECE:

\[ \text{ECE} = \sum_b \frac{n_b}{N}\,\big|\text{acc}_b - \text{conf}_b\big|, \qquad \text{conf} = \max\big(\hat P,\ 1-\hat P\big) \]
  • \(b\): confidence bins; \(n_b\): pairs in the bin; \(N\): total
  • \(\text{acc}_b\): fraction correct in the bin; \(\text{conf}_b\): mean predicted confidence in the bin

The simplest remedy is temperature scaling: replace \(\sigma(\Delta)\) with \(\sigma(\Delta/T)\) and fit a single scalar \(T\) that minimizes cross-entropy on a validation set. Gao 2022 did this with their proxy RMs. Recalibrating does not change the order of the scores, so it does not alter any best-of-\(n\); according to the same authors, it should not affect RL either in their setup, which optimizes without a KL penalty (Adam is invariant to the scale of the loss). With the KL penalty of standard RLHF, dividing \(r\) by \(T\) is equivalent to multiplying the KL \(\beta\) by \(T\), and then scale matters again (section 4 and the PPO chapter).

Accuracy and calibration on the training distribution still do not answer the question that matters most: does the RM generalize to where the policy is going? Stiennon tested this. The RM trained on TL;DR transferred to CNN/DM with 62.4% (1.3B) and 66.5% (6.7B) agreement, versus 66.9% between humans. It preferred minimal edits that improved a summary in 79.4% and 82.8% of cases (humans: 84.1%) and detected swapped roles in 92.9% and 97.2%. But it preferred the edits that shortened the summary only 62.6% of the time, versus 76.4% for humans: the first sign of the length bias in the next section.

RewardBench: a standardized suite

RewardBench (Lambert et al. 2024) gathers verified prompt–chosen–rejected triples in four categories. The core has 2,985 pairs: Chat 358, Chat Hard 456, Safety 740, and Reasoning 1,431, plus “Prior Sets” with 17.2k pairs from older datasets. The metric is pairwise accuracy. It is where HelpSteer2's Nemotron-4 340B RM reached 92.0% (1st place on June 12, 2024) and HelpSteer2-Preference's Llama-3.1-70B with scaled BT reached 94.1 (1st on October 1, 2024). RewardBench 2 (2025) changes the format to best-of-4 (1 correct response against 3 incorrect ones) across 6 domains (Factuality, Precise IF, Math, Safety, Focus, Ties). Models score about 20 points lower than on the first one, and the authors correlate the score with downstream use, in best-of-\(N\) and PPO.

Pitfall: the benchmark is not the use case

An RM is evaluated on fixed pairs, but used against a policy that hunts for its weak spots. High accuracy on RewardBench is necessary, not sufficient. The test that matters is behavior under optimization, which is the subject of the next section.

When the reward lies: length, hacking, and over-optimization

Goodhart's law says: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. The RM is a learned proxy for human judgment. Optimizing it a little improves the responses; optimizing it too much finds and exploits its errors. This phenomenon goes by two names: reward hacking, when the policy discovers a shortcut that the RM rewards and humans do not, and over-optimization, the curve in which the proxy reward keeps rising while the real quality falls.

Length bias

The best-documented shortcut is response length. Gao 2022 reports that optimized policies producing very long responses, even when a short one would be preferred, were a real problem in the InstructGPT setting. Singhal et al. 2023 (“A Long Way to Go”) measured it. PPO greatly increased length in every setting (WebGPT, Stack, RLCD), and when the reward gain was decomposed, 70–90% of the improvement on WebGPT and RLCD came from length. A PPO whose reward was only length reproduced most of the gain: 56% versus 58% win rate for standard PPO on WebGPT, and 64% versus 63% on RLCD. The dominant source of the bias is the RMs, “easily influenced by length biases in the preference data”. Mitigations listed in the literature include penalizing length in the reward, RMs with separate heads for length and quality (ODIN), length-controlled evaluation (AlpacaEval LC), and length-balanced data.

There are other classic examples. Ziegler 2019's RL summarizers became “smart copiers” that copied whole sentences from the text, because the labelers used “did it copy?” as a quality heuristic. Stiennon saw that, under light optimization, the models improved according to the labelers and, under strong optimization, got worse, with the RM still giving high scores to summaries that humans rejected. “Secrets of RLHF” describes a pattern collapse in PPO: the policy fools the RM with specific patterns to obtain abnormally high scores.

Gao's scaling laws

Gao, Schulman & Hilton 2022 measured over-optimization cleanly, without humans. InstructGPT's 6B RM plays the role of the human (the gold RM) and labels 100,000 synthetic pairs, which train proxy RMs from 3M to 3B. A policy is then optimized against the proxy (best-of-\(n\), or PPO without a KL penalty) and the gold score is measured. The distance traveled is \(d = \sqrt{D_{KL}(\pi\,\|\,\pi_{\text{init}})}\), with the KL divergence taken relative to the initial policy. The gold score curves follow simple forms:

\[ R_{\text{bon}}(d) = d\,(\alpha_{\text{bon}} - \beta_{\text{bon}}\,d), \qquad R_{\text{RL}}(d) = d\,(\alpha_{\text{RL}} - \beta_{\text{RL}}\log d), \qquad \text{KL}_{\text{bon}} = \log n - \frac{n-1}{n} \]
  • \(R(d)\): gain in gold score; \(R(0) := 0\)
  • \(\alpha\): “how much it improves” at first; \(\beta\): “how much is lost” to over-optimization. Both depend on the size of the proxy RM and on the data
  • \(\text{KL}_{\text{bon}}\): the Stiennon/Gao analytic formula for the KL of best-of-\(n\) (sample \(n\) responses and keep the one with the highest proxy score); it is an upper bound (Beirami et al., 2024), nearly exact when the \(n\) responses are distinct with no reward ties (chapter 59): \(n = 1{,}000\) → ≤ 5.91 nats, \(n = 60{,}000\) → ≤ 10.00 nats
  • peaks: \(d^* = \alpha/2\beta\) for BoN and \(d^* = e^{\alpha/\beta - 1}\) for RL (calculated from the forms)

With illustrative coefficients (\(\alpha_{\text{bon}}=1\), \(\beta_{\text{bon}}=0.25\); \(\alpha_{\text{RL}}=1\), \(\beta_{\text{RL}}=0.5\)), BoN peaks at \(d = 2\) (KL = 4 nats, \(R = 1.0\)) and RL at \(d = e\) (KL ≈ 7.39 nats, \(R ≈ 1.359\)). Gao's main findings:

  1. RL spends much more KL than BoN for the same amount of optimization, and of over-optimization. So KL is not a good way to compare the amount of optimization across methods. As a function of the proxy score, the two look alike.
  2. The coefficients vary smoothly with proxy RM size: \(\alpha_{\text{bon}}\), \(\beta_{\text{bon}}\), and \(\beta_{\text{RL}}\) change roughly logarithmically with the number of parameters, and \(\alpha_{\text{RL}}\) stays nearly constant.
  3. Policy size matters little: larger policies gain less from optimization, but over-optimize about the same.
  4. KL penalty ≈ early stopping: in their setup, it improved the proxy score reached per unit of KL, but not the gold × KL frontier. The authors caution that this result is sensitive to hyperparameters.

Goodhart's taxonomy helps read the curves. In regressional Goodhart, the proxy is the gold plus independent noise. With Gaussian noise, optimizing partly selects for noise and the real gain is smaller than it looks, but the gold keeps rising. The gold can fall in two cases. The first is extremal Goodhart: outside the RM's training distribution, the proxy–gold relationship breaks down, as with exaggerated length. The second shows up when the RM's own error has heavy tails, that is, when the RM occasionally errs by a lot and best-of-\(n\) ends up “finding” that error (interpretation; this is what the figure simulates). The figure builds a toy best-of-\(n\) with both mechanisms.

Bai 2022 saw something consistent with this in their training runs: early on, the preference model score gain grows roughly linearly in \(\sqrt{D_{KL}}\), across all runs. Splitting the data 50/50 between a “train” RM and a “test” RM, RL stayed robust up to ~150k training samples; after that, the two RMs began to disagree.

Mitigations: KL, ensembles, and weight averaging

The first line of defense is the KL penalty to the reference model, which keeps the policy in the region where the RM was trained. It will be the centerpiece of the PPO and RLHF chapter. The others go after the RM itself:

  • Ensembles. Christiano 2017 already trained several predictors by bootstrap and used the average of the members, each one normalized separately. Coste et al. 2023 compared three ways to combine \(k\) RMs:
\[ R_\mu = \tfrac1k\sum_i R_i, \qquad R_{\text{WCO}} = \min_i R_i, \qquad R_{\text{UWO}} = \tfrac1k\sum_i R_i - \lambda\,\tfrac1k\sum_i\Big(R_i - \tfrac1k\sum_j R_j\Big)^2 \]
  • \(R_\mu\): mean; not conservative, because it only takes one member overestimating
  • \(R_{\text{WCO}}\): worst case (minimum); no hyperparameter, but it can cost performance
  • \(R_{\text{UWO}}\): mean minus \(\lambda\) times the variance across members; penalizes disagreement
  • illustrative example: \(R_i = (1.8,\ 2.0,\ 2.2,\ 0.6)\) → mean 1.65; WCO 0.60; UWO (\(\lambda=0.5\)) 1.4562

With and without 25% label noise, conservative optimization practically eliminated over-optimization in BoN, with improvements of up to 70%, and always reduced it in PPO; combined with a small KL, it prevented over-optimization at no performance cost. There is a limit, though. Eisenstein et al. 2023 showed that ensembles that vary the pre-training seed generalize better than those that vary only the fine-tuning seed, but that not even they eliminate reward hacking, because all members err in similar ways. That is the shared bias in the figure. WARM (Ramé et al. 2024) replaces averaging the predictions with averaging the weights of several RMs fine-tuned from the same pre-training, exploiting the linear connectivity between them: at inference it costs a single RM, and it is more robust to distribution shifts and to inconsistent preferences. Rounding out the toolbox are early stopping with separate or human evaluation, RMs retrained on on-policy data (Bai, Llama 2), reward normalization and clipping, and length control.

Outcome or process: ORM, PRM, and Math-Shepherd

Up to now, the RM gave a score to the whole response. In multi-step reasoning, you can ask something else: is each step right? Uesato et al. 2022 defined the two kinds with an RM that predicts a “correct/incorrect” token after each step:

  • ORM (outcome reward model): the label for every step is whether the final answer matches the reference answer. Maximizing the ORM step by step ≈ maximizing the estimated chance of reaching the right answer; in other words, the ORM learns something like a value function.
  • PRM (process reward model): the label after each step says whether the steps so far are correct. It requires step-by-step annotation.

On GSM8K, supervising only the outcome gave a similar final-answer error, with fewer labels. But for the reasoning to be correct, process supervision was needed, or RMs that imitated it. The best system reduced final-answer error from 16.8% to 12.7% and trace error (solutions with the right answer and wrong reasoning) from 14.0% to 3.4%. Lightman et al. 2023 (“Let's Verify Step by Step”) scaled the idea up with PRM800K. Humans labeled only up to the first wrong step, and a solution's score is the product of the probabilities of each step being correct:

\[ \text{score}_{\text{PRM}}(y) = \prod_{k=1}^{S} p\big(\text{step}_k\ \text{correct} \mid x, \text{step}_{1..k}\big) \]
  • \(S\): number of steps in the solution; \(p\): probability given by the PRM (neutrals count as positives)
  • using the minimum instead of the product makes “small” differences, according to the authors
  • illustrative example: \((0.98,\ 0.95,\ 0.60,\ 0.97)\) → product 0.5418, minimum 0.60

Choosing the best of 1,860 solutions on a 500-problem subset of MATH, the PRM got 78.2% right, the ORM 72.4%, and majority voting 69.6%. Step-by-step annotation is expensive, and Math-Shepherd (Wang et al. 2023) generates the labels without humans: starting from step \(s_i\), a “completer” finishes \(N\) solutions and checks the final answers \(\{a_j\}\) against the reference answer \(a^*\):

\[ y^{\text{HE}}_{s_i} = \mathbb 1\big[\exists j: a_j = a^*\big], \qquad y^{\text{SE}}_{s_i} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbb 1[a_j = a^*] \]
  • HE (hard estimation): the step is good if any continuation gets it right
  • SE (soft estimation): the fraction of continuations that get it right
  • example: \(N = 4\) continuations with answers (42, 17, 42, 42) and reference answer 42 → HE = 1, SE = 0.75

With step-by-step PPO against Math-Shepherd, a Mistral-7B went from 77.9% to 84.1% on GSM8K and from 28.6% to 33.0% on MATH; with verification at the end, 89.1% and 43.5%. Notice what SE is (interpretation): a Monte Carlo estimate of the value \(V(s_i)\), the expected final reward from that prefix. A PRM trained on these labels is a critic learned outside the loop. This bridge comes back in the next chapter, when the value function and the critic show up.

Why DeepSeek-R1 did not use a PRM

The DeepSeek-R1 report says it did not use neural RMs, neither process nor outcome ones, for reasoning RL, because they suffer from reward hacking at scale. For the PRM, the authors give three reasons: it is hard to define what a “step” is, annotating steps is expensive and noisy, and any model-based PRM ends up leading to hacking. They acknowledge that PRMs work well for reranking the top N responses. Where an exact verifier exists (numeric answer, code tests), the “RM” becomes a deterministic function: that is RL with verifiable rewards, from the GRPO and RLVR chapter.

AI judges: LLM-as-judge, generative RMs, and RLAIF

A scalar RM only returns a number. A large LLM, instructed to compare two responses, also judges, and explains itself too. Zheng et al. 2023 used GPT-4 as a judge on MT-bench and Chatbot Arena: agreement with humans was 85% (no ties), above the 81% between humans, and humans found 75% of the GPT-4 judgments they disagreed with “reasonable”. There are three modes of use: pairwise comparison, single-answer grading, and reference-guided grading. Four biases are catalogued: position, verbosity, self-preference (the judge prefers its own answers), and limited reasoning in math (the judge gets wrong what it cannot solve). The mitigations: judge in both orders and accept only consistent wins, give the judge a reference, and ask for brief reasoning before the verdict.

The next step is to train the judge as a reward model. In GenRM (Zhang et al. 2024), the verifier is trained with next-token prediction, and the reward is the probability of the “Yes” token after the question “is the answer correct (Yes/No)?”. In the GenRM-CoT variant, it first writes a rationale and takes a majority vote over several rationales, spending more compute at test time. In best-of-\(N\), GenRM took an algorithmic task from 5% to 45.3%, GSM8K from 73% to 93.4%, transfer to MATH from 28% to 44.6%, and MMLU abstract algebra from 37.9% to 53.5%. DeepSeek-GRM (Liu et al. 2025) goes further: a pointwise generative RM, trained with online RL (Self-Principled Critique Tuning) to first write principles adapted to the question and then the critique, which scales at inference through parallel sampling and voting guided by a meta-RM.

Pairwise or pointwise (interpretation)? Pairwise judgment matches BT and is more robust to scale drift. But PPO and GRPO need one scalar per response, and that is why modern generative RMs convert the critique into a score.

RLAIF: when the labeler is also an AI

If an LLM judges well, it can produce the RM's training pairs itself. That is RLAIF (RL from AI Feedback). In Constitutional AI (Bai et al. 2022b), a model labels which of two responses is less harmful according to a list of principles, and the preference model is trained on a mix of AI preferences (harmlessness) and human ones (helpfulness). Lee et al. 2023 (“RLAIF vs RLHF”) unpacked the recipe:

  1. Soft label. The labeler (PaLM 2 L, without RL) reads a preamble, optional examples, the two responses, and “Preferred Response=”. The softmax of the log-probs of the tokens “1” and “2” becomes a soft label, and the RM is trained with cross-entropy against it, in a kind of distillation.
  2. Position bias. Mitigated by running two inferences with the order swapped and averaging them.
  3. Chain of thought. Asking the labeler to reason first consistently improved alignment with humans; a detailed preamble and few-shot examples helped only on some tasks.
  4. Result. Humans preferred RLAIF and RLHF over SFT in 71% and 73% of cases (summarization) and in 63% and 64% (helpful dialogue), a non-significant difference. On the rate of harmless responses: RLAIF 88%, RLHF 76%, SFT 64%. With a labeler the same size as the policy, still 68% against SFT.
  5. d-RLAIF. A variant skips the RM: the LLM gives a score from 1 to 10 directly during RL, avoiding an outdated (stale) RM, and matches or beats canonical RLAIF.

The figure shows why swapping the order helps, and also what that swap does not fix.

AI labels are cheap and consistent, but they carry the judge's biases (position, length, self-preference). Everything in section 7 applies, and an RM trained on AI labels inherits these biases and sometimes amplifies them (interpretation). UltraFeedback is RLAIF at dataset scale (GPT-4 as the labeler), and Skywork-Reward-V2 used human + AI curation over 40M pairs.

The technical reports of the families that Module 1 serves show how these pieces combine in 2025–2026. There is no “pure” RM: they all mix rules, RMs, and judges.

GLM-5: hybrid reward

In the General RL stage, the reward combines rules (precise, but limited in scope), ORMs (low variance, but hackable), and generative RMs (robust, but high variance). Expert human responses come in as style anchors, to avoid the verbose, formulaic “model text” pattern.

General RL covers more than 20 tasks, with three reward sources: rules (format, instruction following), a reference-based model (Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct scores the response against a reference answer), and a reference-free model (a trained RM).

🧑‍⚖️

DeepSeek: from the CoT RM to the actor-judge

V3 used rules (boxed answer, compiler and tests) and an RM trained from V3's SFT on preference data that includes the chain of thought leading to the reward, to mitigate hacking. V4 does away with scalar RMs: RL is guided by rubrics and the actor itself works as a generative RM.

There is also a direct bridge to Module 1: the three models served by the recipes on the two Sparks (GLM-5.3-Flash-EXL3, qwen3.8-flash-next, deepseek-v4-flash-vision-exp, all with an OpenAI API on port 8888) form a panel of judges from three distinct families. PoLL (Verga et al. 2024) showed that a panel of smaller models from different families has less intra-model bias and costs more than 7× less than one large judge. The rule of thumb, which the evaluation chapter details: exclude from the panel the family of the teacher that generated the data and the family of the student, to steer clear of self-preference.

Roadmap: your first RM (or judge)

  1. Decide the format before collecting. Pairs (or rankings of \(K\) responses, grouped in the batch) for direction; strength or per-attribute Likert if you want magnitude or to control verbosity later.
  2. Generate the responses from the policy you are going to optimize (or one very close to it) and plan fresh collections after each round of RL. An off-distribution RM is a hackable RM.
  3. Measure agreement (κ, or weighted κ for ordinal scales) on a sample with 2–3 annotators, or between judge and human. It is your RM's ceiling.
  4. Train 1 epoch, with the SFT backbone, a scalar head read at EOS, dropout off, and a few seeds; normalize the bias to mean 0 on the references.
  5. Evaluate accuracy, calibration, and generalization (RewardBench and a set from your own domain), and check how the score correlates with length.
  6. Under optimization, watch the gold: samples evaluated by humans or by a judge from another family, the mean length, and the KL. Proxy rising while gold stays flat is Goodhart.

Now we have \(r(x,y)\): a number that summarizes preferences, with its flaws mapped out. What remains is the central question of reinforcement-based post-training: how do you make an LLM push this number up, if the reward only arrives at the end of the response and you cannot differentiate through sampling? The next chapter answers with the policy gradient theorem, REINFORCE, and the family of ideas that leads to PPO.
