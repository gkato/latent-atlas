Imagine receiving a box with 180 billion numbers (123.5 GiB on disk) and two book-sized computers, each with 121.69 GiB of memory. The task: get this model — Qwen3.8-Flash-Next, the open preview of the Qwen4 architecture — to talk to tools, see images, remember 262K tokens and answer fast. Eleven minutes after a ./start.sh, Mia's recipe delivers exactly that: 52 tok/s in a single conversation and about 225 tok/s aggregate with eight prose conversations. In this chapter you will rebuild that recipe from scratch, decision by decision, understanding why each parameter has the value it has — and which chapter holds the concept behind it.

Step 0 — The byte budget rules everything

A recipe doesn't start in the terminal: it starts on a sheet of paper. Before choosing an image, flags or speculation, Mia answered the two questions from the journey map — does it fit? and is it fast? — with numbers. Everything else in the recipe follows from that arithmetic. Here is the full roadmap; the numbers match the section titles (Step 0 to Step 8), and each step points to the chapter where the concept is taught in depth.

  1. Byte budget Add up weights, KV cache, activations and system per node, remembering that the memory is unified (unified memory, prefill, decode and KV).
  2. Hardware and network Two DGX Sparks linked by ConnectX-7; find the interface, HCA and GID on each side (NCCL and RDMA).
  3. Image and lane Choose between the day-0 image, the stable vLLM 0.30 or the single-node variant (inside vLLM).
  4. Weights Pick the checkpoint, check the hashes and distribute it to the other node (checkpoints, FP8/FP4).
  5. Fix the loading Patch the metadata and the loader so vLLM understands the checkpoint (N-gram and MTP, kernels).
  6. Arguments Assemble the vllm serve line: parallelism, memory, scheduler, KV, speculation, compilation and API (parallelism, scheduler).
  7. Launch Bring up the worker, then the head, and follow the boot until /health (recipe engineering).
  8. Verify and operate Read the right log lines, measure quality and acceptance; then stop, switch lanes and keep an eye on memory (measuring quality, benchmarking).
  9. Tune Find out where the step's milliseconds go and pull one lever at a time (MTP and reduced vocabulary).

What has to fit

The default checkpoint, nvidia/Qwen3.8-Flash-Next-NVFP4, is a numerical patchwork quilt: each piece of the model was stored in the format its role can tolerate. Total: ≈123.5 GiB. A single Spark has 121.69 GiB visible to CUDA — and it still has to run Linux and hold the KV cache and activations. It doesn't fit. With two nodes and tensor parallelism (TP=2) plus expert parallelism (EP), each GPU holds half the experts (256 of 512), half the N-gram table (split by vocabulary range) and half the dense matrices. Only a few pieces stay whole on both sides: the HyperConnections, the routers, the indexer and the vision tower. The log measured 64.46 GiB of weights per node.

\[ M_{\text{KV}} = \text{GMU}\cdot M_{\text{total}} - \big(W + N_{\text{non-torch}} + A_{\text{peak}}\big) \qquad T_{\text{KV}} = M_{\text{KV}}\cdot\tau \qquad C = \frac{T_{\text{KV}}}{L_{\max}} \]
  • \(M_{\text{total}}\): CUDA-visible memory per node (121.69 GiB on the GB10)
  • \(\text{GMU}\): --gpu-memory-utilization, the fraction vLLM is allowed to budget
  • \(W + N_{\text{non-torch}}\): weights plus allocations outside PyTorch (68.52 GiB measured)
  • \(A_{\text{peak}}\): activation peak measured in the profile run (1.07 GiB)
  • \(\tau\): tokens per GiB of pool — 114,060 with FP8 KV, 67,229 with BF16, measured on day-0 at GMU 0.835 with the SSM state apparently in float32 (the README doesn't say; with a bf16 SSM the same GMU gave 4.24–4.38 M tokens in FP8)
  • \(L_{\max}\): --max-model-len; \(C\) is the “maximum concurrency” the log prints

Check the math against the real log at GMU 0.835: \(0.835 \times 121.69 = 101.61\) GiB budgeted; subtract 68.52 and 1.07 and you are left with 32.02 GiB of KV — exactly what vLLM printed, along with GPU KV cache size: 3,652,200 tokens and Maximum concurrency for 262,144 tokens per request: 13.93x. By the log's own arithmetic, the 0.54 GiB of CUDA graphs sit outside this sum. In the figure, play with the GMU and the KV type.

Notice three things. First: the KV cache is the remainder; every extra GiB for the system is one GiB less of cache. Second: the math is a model, and models can be wrong. At 0.80 it promises 3.17 M FP8 tokens, but lane 0.30 (a different image) measured 2.64 M, ~10× 262K (≈5× in BF16); a bf16 SSM, on the other hand, pushes the other way (4.24–4.38 M at 0.835). The truth is the GPU KV cache size line in the log. Third: with FP8, more full contexts fit than the 8 allowed by --max-num-seqs, so the scheduler is the limit. In BF16, eight 262K-token conversations would no longer fit together (vLLM would preempt), but real conversations rarely fill the context. As the README puts it: the weights, not the KV, are the binding constraint on this machine.

Why it matters

Why do only 13 layers hold KV? Of the 48, 36 are Gated DeltaNet, with a fixed-size state per request. That leaves the 12 QSA layers plus the MTP: \(13 \times 2\,(K,V) \times 2\ \text{KV heads} \times 256 \times 2\,\text{B} = 26{,}624\) B per token in BF16, 13,312 B per GPU with TP=2, half that in FP8. The measured figure is higher (9,418 B/token/GPU in FP8) because the indexer, the compressor and the GDN state stay in BF16 and the allocator rounds pages up: that's why FP8 yields 1.70×, not 2× (PagedAttention and the KV budget).

ParameterGPU_MEMORY_UTILIZATION=0.80

The value used to be 0.85 and then 0.835. At 0.835 the driver held 114.5 GiB per node, and a soak with six clients left MemAvailable between 0.3 and 0.9 GiB — the zone where the GB10 does a hard reset instead of raising an out-of-memory error. At 0.80, 4.5 GiB (head) and 7.8 GiB (worker) stayed free during an hour of load. Cost: the KV pool shrinks by about 4 GiB per node. Speed lost: none — decode does not depend on the GMU. It's the concept of unified memory turned into a parameter. A note on rigor: .env.sample gives the rule of thumb “+0.01 GMU ≈ +0.83 GiB ≈ +95K FP8 tokens”. It comes from the 0.835 → 0.85 delta quoted in the README (+1.24 GiB, measured in the BF16 era with an old container): 1.24 / 1.5 ≈ 0.83. By definition it would be 0.01 × 121.69 = 1.22 GiB, and the CHANGELOG measured “~4 GiB less” for −0.035, consistent with the definition. The figure above uses the definition.

Step 1 — Hardware and network: two Sparks, one cable

Each DGX Spark has a GB10 GPU (sm_121, 48 SMs), 20 ARM cores and 121.69 GiB of LPDDR5x shared between CPU and GPU, with ~273 GB/s in theory (235–245 GB/s in practice). The two nodes talk through a ConnectX-7 card running RoCE v2 at 200 GbE, cabled port to port. With TP=2 this cable literally becomes the model's internal bus: every layer ends with a sum across the two GPUs.

Before touching vLLM, write down three things on each node: the name of the cable's interface (ip -o -4 addr), the name of the RDMA HCA (ibstat) and the link IPs. In the figure, switch between the four traffic “planes” to see which variable governs each one.

Look at the data plane: there are about 106 collectives per decode step — 36 all-reduces of the GDN outputs, 12 of the attention outputs, 48 of the MoE, 1 all-gather of logits and 9 of the drafts —, each 5 to 20 KB. At 20–30 µs per collective, that adds up to 2 to 3 ms per step (6–8% of the time), and that floor does not shrink with the batch: it's latency, not bandwidth. Prefill is the opposite: each 8192-token chunk does 96 all-reduces of 42 MB, about 150–180 ms of communication. The α-β model that explains the difference is in NCCL and RDMA.

Pitfall

Network failures in vLLM look like hangs, not errors: init sits still for a minute and nothing explains why. Validate RDMA in isolation first (nccl-tests all_reduce_perf with NCCL_IB_HCA==<hca>, NCCL_IB_GID_INDEX=3 and NCCL_DEBUG=INFO). The repository also lists suggested fabric knobs that were not adopted — a second CX7 link with NCCL_CROSS_NIC=1, the LL/LL128 protocols, GPUDirect RDMA — with an estimated 3–8% decode gain. An estimate, not a measurement.

Step 2 — The image and the lanes: day-0, vLLM 0.30 and TP1

The Python code that knows what a QSA layer, an N-gram table or an MTP is lives inside the Docker image. Choosing the image means choosing which implementation of the model you will run — and, therefore, which fixes you will need. The recipe knows two:

day-0 image

vllm/vllm-openai:qwen38-flash-next: NVIDIA's development build from launch day (vLLM 0.1.dev20073+g8e685d198, V1 engine, FlashInfer 0.6.17, torch 2.13+cu130, NCCL 2.30.7; ~20.6 GB). Code in qwen3_8_flash_next/nvidia/. It works, but it trips over several checkpoints — hence the patches.

Stable vLLM 0.30.0

vllm/vllm-openai:v0.30.0: the release that absorbed the model as qwen4_exp. It loads the nvidia checkpoint natively and already ships the prefix cache fixes, but its QSA kernels only accept a BF16 KV.

A lane is a combination of image, checkpoint, overlays and defaults validated together. Instead of copying start.sh, the recipe creates few-line wrappers that export OVERRIDE_* variables and run exec start.sh. That way the cluster configuration (IPs, HCAs, ports) lives in a single .env and each lane only declares what changes. The figure is a decision map: pick your goal and see which train to take.

No lane is “the best”: day-0 holds more context (FP8 KV) and is the only one with determinism and a kernel profile; 0.30 has half the multi-turn TTFT and equal or higher decode, but a BF16 KV by default; the official FP8 uses Qwen's own weights and pays for it in cache; TP1 fits on a single Spark.

The control panel: who wins?

Wrappers only work because start.sh has a strict precedence order — and it's a surprising one. The script reads the environment, then runs source .env: the .env beats the command line for every key it defines. Two exceptions are captured before the source and reapplied afterward: ABLIT and HF_TOKEN. Finally, the OVERRIDE_* variables are read after the .env, and final guards pick the checkpoint and turn YaRN off when it makes no sense. Pick a variable in the figure and switch the layers on to watch the ball roll down.

Analogy

Think of a cake recipe covered in sticky notes. The notebook (.env) is the household's source of truth; anyone shouting in the kitchen (the command line) is ignored if the notebook has that line — except for two messages the head of the household always says to obey (ABLIT, HF_TOKEN). The lane wrappers are sticky notes stuck on top of the notebook: they outrank everything, but only for that day's party. The engineering lesson is in recipe engineering: document the precedence and print the effective value in the log.

Step 3 — Weights: choose, check, distribute

The same architecture can arrive under different contracts. Each quantizer decides which format to give each piece and — what hurts most — how to record that in the metadata. That's why switching checkpoints almost always wakes up a different patch. The figure shows the same model in six outfits: each component's bar is proportional to its estimated bytes (parameters × bytes per parameter of the format), and the panel lists what each choice demands from the recipe.

Notice how the estimate matches the published size when the formats are known (nvidia ≈123.5 GiB; RadixArk ≈125.9 GiB, because its MTP experts are BF16) and how the official FP8 jumps to ≈173 GiB: FP8 experts cost twice as much as NVFP4 (112.5 GiB versus 63.3), and the dense layers do not shrink. The performance report read this checkpoint's headers (correction of 2026-09-02): modules_to_not_convert lists linear_attn.*, hyper_connection* and lm_head, and the tensor scan confirms GDN, attention, HC and lm_head in BF16. Only the routed experts and the MTP's experts are FP8. The phrase in start-fp8.sh (“block-128 over the whole checkpoint”) describes the quantization_config (a global Fp8Config), not the dtype of each tensor. Split across two GPUs, that's about 25 GiB more weights per node — which is what leaves only ~500K tokens of KV in this lane. The math of the formats is in FP8, FP4 and microscaling.

Download, check and distribute

The download (download.sh) goes to the head only. Three checks prevent the most expensive failures:

  • Completeness: resolve_snapshot.py only accepts a snapshot if every shard in model.safetensors.index.json exists (exit 0 complete, 1 incomplete, 2 missing). An interrupted download used to look finished — and rsync would copy the hole.
  • refs/main without a trailing newline: huggingface_hub 1.28 reads the file without strip(); an echo $SHA > refs/main leaves a \n and vLLM dies with “repo not found” in offline mode. Use printf. start.sh normalizes it on both nodes.
  • Hash: a corrupted shard keeps a similar size and only blows up at ~33% of the load (safe_open: “incomplete metadata”). ./check-weights.sh --verify compares the SHA-256 of the LFS blobs and the SHA-1 of the git blobs against the Hugging Face API manifest — ~1 min for 135 GB at 2.4 GiB/s.

For the worker, there are two paths. rsync (the default) copies ~126 GiB to the worker's disk once: independent nodes and a faster boot. NFS exports the head's cache over the cable: zero disk on the worker, great for switching checkpoints, but the head must stay up and every cold boot streams ~126 GiB. The repo's performance report attributes part of the 458 s of measured loading precisely to that streaming. Before launching, start.sh also evicts the checkpoint's own pages from the page cache (posix_fadvise(DONTNEED), no root needed): in unified memory, clean pages left by an rsync compete with the GPU — measured 4.15 → 0.00 GiB.

Deep dive — the FP8-dense checkpoint

Per decode step, the dense BF16 matrices account for ~6.9 of the ~9.2 GiB read. The recipe's converter turns 591 linears (GDN, attention, HyperConnections, shared experts, lm_head) into FP8 E4M3 with one scale per output channel and dynamic per-token activation: no calibration, and it accepts the rank-320 projections, which are not multiples of 128. Whatever is sensitive stays in BF16 (embeddings, routers, indexer, gates, norms, MTP, vision). Measured error: relative RMSE 0.0240/0.0262/0.0268 (min/median/max), cosine ≥ 0.9996. Expected gain: 9.2 → 5.7 GiB per step, ~1.5× at batch 1 — analytical, never measured on a GPU. And the generator files/overlay/apply_patches.py is not in the repository (see Step 4).

In the recipe — the abliterated checkpoint

ABLIT=1 swaps the checkpoint for a gated derivative with the same layout as nvidia's, in which only the 12 self_attn.o_proj tensors of the QSA layers (3, 7, 11, …, 47) received a projection that removes the refusal direction (abliteration). It needs HF_TOKEN and accepted terms; it applies to this recipe only. Since the server listens on 0.0.0.0 with no authentication, safeguards become the operator's responsibility.

Step 4 — Fixing how the checkpoint is read and loaded

On the day-0 lane, the nvidia checkpoint doesn't load “out of the box”. And the way it fails is the worst possible: almost always ~7 minutes after starting, with the weights already in memory. Fixes 1 to 3 below are required for nvidia; fix 4 serves checkpoints with MXFP8 layers and is always mounted, just in case. Each one was born from a real crash, and the golden rule they teach is to turn late failures into a preflight that takes seconds.

  1. Fix 1 — N-gram table: tell vLLM the dtype The nvidia checkpoint declares the PLE as FP8 only inside quantization_config.config_groups. detect_ple_dtype.py looks for the group whose target is ple_embedding.ngram_embedding (num_bits 8 → float8_e4m3fn, 4 → nvfp4) and the recipe injects --hf-overrides '{"text_config":{"ple_embedding_dtype":"float8_e4m3fn"}}'. patch_ple_layer.py ports the dispatch from vLLM PR #53899: methods that keep the table quantized (NVFP4 rows packed into uint8 with FP8 scales every 16 and an FP32 global scale; or FP8 shards with a single global scale) and dequantize only the 16 rows fetched per token. Without it the table turns into BF16 (~102 GB) and the load dies. (The README's PLE_QUANT_OVERRIDE is obsolete; see N-gram and MTP.)
  2. Fix 2 — MTP: matching the names vLLM instantiates the MTP layer at the absolute index num_hidden_layers + i = mtp.layers.48 and looks up its quantization by exact string; the checkpoint only annotates mtp.layers.0. patch_checkpoint_config.py adds .0. → .48. aliases to config.json and hf_quant_config.json (mounted over the snapshot, without touching the cache) and normalizes the FP8_PB_WO label (group 128) to FP8_BLOCK_SCALES. The --mtp-moe-algo mode runs as a preflight and exits with code 3 if the algorithm can't be built.
  3. Fix 3 — MTP: building the block scales Even once it finds the name, the ModelOpt dispatch only knew how to build FP8, NVFP4, W4A16_NVFP4 and MXFP8 experts. patch_modelopt_fp8_block_moe.py adds the Fp8Config(weight_block_size=[g,g]) branch with g read from group_size — and refuses to guess: a wrong block size silently applies misaligned scales.
  4. Fix 4 — MXFP8: respecting the kernel envelope A fallback for MXFP8 layers whose shape the kernel doesn't accept, vision included. It matters for checkpoints with MXFP8 layers, such as the local-inference-lab one used by the TP1 lane; on nvidia, whose dense layers are BF16, it probably does nothing. See the second figure in this section.

The kernel envelope: when the matrix shape picks the path

High-performance quantized kernels only accept matrices whose dimensions fit their tiles. FlashInfer's mm_mxfp8, measured on sm_121, requires \(N \ge 128\), \(K \ge 128\) and both multiples of 32 — regardless of \(M\), the number of tokens. The 72 linear_attn.in_proj_a/b have shape [48, 2560] (\(N = 48\)) and killed the boot with AssertionError … N >= 128, got N=48 after ~7 min; the 27 visual…mlp.linear_fc1 [4304, 1152] gave Problem size is not supported, because \(4304 \bmod 32 = 16\). patch_modelopt_mxfp8.py checks the shape already split by TP, layer by layer, and swaps the kernel for an emulation kernel that dequantizes to BF16 once at load time (+≈17 MB); the whole visual.* tower goes to emulation by prefix.

Which checkpoint do these shapes come from? The sources disagree: the README says “this checkpoint”, the patch docstring talks about RadixArk, the TP1 HANDOFF puts the MXFP8 attention in local-inference-lab, and NVIDIA's model card says nvidia is BF16 outside the experts. The most coherent reading (interpretation): the crash comes from checkpoints with MXFP8 layers, and on nvidia the patch is mounted but inert. Always mounting it is cheap, because it only acts on MXFP8 layers. Explore the envelope:

The same reasoning explains --mm-encoder-tp-mode data. With weights, the vision MLP (intermediate size 4304) would be split across the two GPUs: \(4304 / 2 = 2152\), which is not a multiple of 16 — the divisibility that the recipe's .env says the NVFP4 kernels require — and the load crashes. With data, the 0.84 GiB encoder is replicated whole on each GPU. Divisibility is one of the “taxes” of parallelism; the extreme case shows up in TP=3 and TP=4.

Deep dive — the bug of the YaRN that never was

Until September 2026, turning on YARN_ENABLE emitted --hf-overrides '{"rope_parameters":{…}}' at the root level. ModelConfig._apply_dict_overrides only descends into nested configs, and in qwen4_exp the parent also exposes rope_parameters as a plain dict: the value stayed on the parent and never reached text_config. Every old “1M” run used unscaled RoPE. The fix is a single nested payload: {"text_config":{"ple_embedding_dtype":"float8_e4m3fn","rope_parameters":{"rope_type":"yarn","factor":4.0,"original_max_position_embeddings":262144}}}. That's why the default became MAX_MODEL_LEN=262144 and the 1M context is not validated. Moral: an override that doesn't raise an error doesn't prove it was applied (checkpoints and --hf-overrides).

Step 8 — Numbers, tuning levers and pitfalls

With the server up, the question becomes: where do the milliseconds go? When decoding a single conversation, the GPU spends most of its time reading weights (decode is bandwidth-bound). The repository's performance report added up the bytes read per GPU in one step with MTP=3 (verifying 4 tokens + 3 one-token drafts): ≈9.2 GiB. At an effective 240 GB/s, that read alone takes ~41 ms. But the step measured at S=1, with the full vocabulary, is 59–61 ms (rerun of 2026-09-16: prose 59.3 ms, code 60.7 ms). How many tokens come out of each step? At 72.8% acceptance over 3 drafts (823 of 1,131), that's 2.18 accepted drafts plus the token the target always emits: \(\bar a \approx 3.2\) tokens per step (≈2.9 on prose in the 09-11 run). And 3.2 tokens / 61 ms ≈ 52 tok/s, which matches the 52.1 measured. The math adds up, and it says something important: ~2/3 of the step is bandwidth; the other ~17–20 ms are overhead outside the weight read.

\[ \begin{gathered} t_{\text{step}} \approx \frac{B_{\text{step}}}{\beta_{\text{eff}}} + t_{\text{fixed}} \qquad\qquad \text{tok/s} \approx \frac{\bar a}{t_{\text{step}}} \\[6pt] \underbrace{\tfrac{9.2\ \text{GiB}}{240\ \text{GB/s}}}_{\approx 41\ \text{ms}} + \underbrace{20\ \text{ms}}_{t_{\text{fixed}}} \approx 61\ \text{ms} \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{3.2}{0.061\ \text{s}} \approx 52\ \text{tok/s} \end{gathered} \]
  • \(B_{\text{step}}\): bytes read per GPU in one decode step (≈9.2 GiB with MTP=3, batch 1, full vocabulary)
  • \(\beta_{\text{eff}}\): effective LPDDR5x bandwidth (235–245 GB/s in practice, 273 in theory)
  • \(t_{\text{fixed}}\): overhead outside the weight read, obtained by difference (measured step − read ≈ 17–20 ms): the ~106 collectives (2–3 ms) and, by interpretation, the launches of the three draft passes, sampling and scheduling
  • \(\bar a\): tokens emitted per step = 1 + accepted drafts (≈3.2 at 72.8% acceptance; ≈2.9 on prose)
A report calculation that doesn't add up

The performance report concluded that batch 1 was already “at the bandwidth ceiling”: 41 ms × ~2.2 tokens per step ≈ 53–56 tok/s, “exactly” the 55.8 measured. Interpretation: the 2.2 are the accepted drafts (72.8% × 3), without the token the target always emits; and the 55.8 tok/s come from an old README table (MTP off, different weights). With the measured steps, ~1/3 of the step lies outside the weight read: cutting bytes is still the biggest lever, but the fixed overhead is a target too.

The reduced vocabulary works as a test of the model. It removes ~1.1 GiB of reads per step (≈5 ms at 240 GB/s), and the measured step dropped by 6–7 ms (prose 59.9 → 53.3 ms; code 61.2 → 54.0 ms, run of 2026-09-11). So there are four levers: read fewer bytes, accept more tokens per step, serve more sequences per step (which amortizes the read and the overhead) or shrink the fixed overhead (communication, kernel launches).

Notice that lm_head is the largest slice (26%): it's read four times per step — once by the target and three times by the drafter. That's why cutting the drafter's vocabulary to 47K ids is “the biggest single lever” of the step. It conflicts with FP8-dense for a mechanical reason: both overlays rewrite nvidia/mtp.py, and start.sh refuses to take both together. Notice the HyperConnections too: 13% of the bytes for a small module. They stay replicated on both GPUs and in BF16, because the model code hardcodes quant_config=None for them; only the FP8-dense overlay quantizes them.

The order of the levers, according to the repo itself

(1) Fewer bytes per step: reduced vocabulary (done), FP8-dense (analytical), K=2 if the 3rd position accepts little. (2) More accepted tokens: build the vocabulary from the model's own output on your traffic (gen_draft_corpus.py → build_draft_vocab.py --balance-shards 2), choosing the size by coverage, not by the id count. If your traffic is in another language, start from the seven 65k files the recipe already ships (MTP_DRAFT_VOCAB=files/draft_vocab_<lang>_en_code_65k.txt; es, zh, ja, de, pt, fr, ru; the default is still the 47k one). For anyone serving Portuguese: +9% normalized and +16% raw in tok/s, with accepted tokens per draft rising from 0.96 to 1.39 (TP=2, one boot per arm; details and caveats in chapter 36). (3) More sequences per step, up to MAX_NUM_SEQS and the KV. (4) Fixed overhead: NCCL knobs and a second cable, measuring with nccl-tests. (5) Kernels: a QSA profile for 48 SMs (QSA_PROFILE=gb10, split-K from 64 to 16 partitions, not measured), a skinny GEMM on sm_121. Changed MAX_MODEL_LEN, K, KV dtype, vocabulary, FP8-dense or QSA profile? Measure again.

Pitfalls that cost hours

Don't pass --kv-cache-dtype fp8 by hand: use the .env so the patch gets mounted along with it (and remember that the .env beats the environment). PLE_OFFLOAD=true on the dual kit asks for ~51 GB of “free CPU memory” that doesn't exist — in unified memory it's the same pool (log: Available RAM: 44.92 GiB). A .env written from scratch without MAMBA_SSM_CACHE_DTYPE, MTP_DRAFT_VOCAB, MTP_DISABLE_BLOCK_DROP and MTP_INDEX_SHARE loses four optimizations, because the start.sh fallbacks differ from .env.sample. MTP with media: the drafter doesn't receive the image embeddings and accepts less. At the T=1.0 recommended by generation_config (top_p 0.95, top_k 20), rejection sampling accepts fewer drafts than under the benchmarks' greedy decoding.

Across the three recipes

Qwen is the recipe of lanes and metadata fixes: almost all the engineering goes into getting a mixed-precision checkpoint read correctly and into cutting bytes from the drafter. The GLM recipe solves the same “does it fit, and is it fast?” with 4-bit EXL3 and the DFlash2 block drafter; the DeepSeek recipe, up next, takes the idea of lanes to the extreme — three lanes with different images and hotfixes, a 1M context and a compressed KV. Take the method with you: budget first, preflight before the boot, one variable at a time.
