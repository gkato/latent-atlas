You have walked the whole trail: bits and mantissas, attention, MoE, quantization, kernels, vLLM from the inside, speculation, parallelism. This chapter is a lab bench. Here every formula you learned becomes a dial that moves. You pick a model (or type one in), a weight format, how many nodes, how much context, how many users and which drafter, and the bench answers, live, the three questions of every recipe: does it fit?, how many fit? and how fast?. At the end it writes the skeleton of the .env and of the vllm serve, with a comment on every line. The main payoff, though, goes beyond the command: you will know where every number comes from, what was measured, what is an estimate and how to calibrate the estimate with the boot log.

The bench: from config.json to the command

The three recipes from Mia's lab (GLM-5.3-Flash, Qwen3.8-Flash-Next and DeepSeek-V4-Flash) and the MiMo-V2.6-Flash tutorial all started the same way. Someone opened a config.json, did the math on paper and only then pressed the button. This chapter turns that paper into a living tool. It works like an assembly line with five calculation stations (weigh, budget the node, count the KV, time and slice), plus two stops at the ends: read the config and write the skeleton. That makes seven stops in the figure. Each station receives a question, applies a formula you have already seen in some chapter and passes a number on to the next one.

In the figure below, the config.json card rides the conveyor belt. Use the stepper buttons (or leave it on autoplay) and watch what each station reads and what it delivers. The numbers in the speech bubble are computed by the lab's own model, using the GLM-5.3-Flash preset as the example.

Notice two things. The stations are almost sequential. There is no point timing the decode before knowing how much is left for the KV cache, because the decode batch size depends on how many requests fit in the pool. The exception is the number of nodes \(P\): Station 1 already needs it, but only Station 5 confirms that the model can actually be sliced into \(P\) pieces. Treat it as an initial guess; if Station 5 rejects it, go back to Station 1. And the belt loops back to the start: the last stage is measuring the boot and recalibrating. A recipe starts as a hypothesis that then has to pass the test of the log.

Analogy: the bridge engineer's spreadsheet

Before building a bridge, the engineer computes loads with simplified formulas and safety margins. Then they instrument the bridge and compare what was predicted with what was measured. Nobody builds a bridge to see whether it falls, and it makes just as little sense to boot a pair of DGX Sparks only to find out whether the model fits. The lab is that spreadsheet: deliberately simplified, with the assumptions in plain sight and the recipes' measurements to check against.

Why predict before measuring

Every attempt is expensive. A GLM restart took 259 s and, after a lot of optimization, 122 s. The Qwen cold start measured 10m55s, and DeepSeek takes about 6 minutes to load. In unified memory the cost of getting it wrong is also higher: going over the limit does not always produce a clean OOM. The Qwen recipe recorded that, with MemAvailable close to zero, the GB10 enters the zone where it may hard reset. A 30-second calculation saves an afternoon of reboots.

Station 1 · The price of a parameter

The first question is the crudest one: how many bytes do the weights take up? The naive answer, parameters × bits ÷ 8, is almost right. The "almost" has two causes. The 4- and 8-bit formats carry scales, so each weight pays for a few extra bits. And no frontier checkpoint uses a single format: the experts go down to 4 bits, while the sensitive parts stay at 8 or 16.

Effective bits: the scale tax

In a microscaling format, a block of \(n\) elements shares one scale. The whole block pays for that scale, and the per-element cost becomes the number of effective bits:

\[ b_{\text{eff}} \;=\; b_{\text{elem}} \;+\; \frac{b_{\text{scale}}}{n_{\text{block}}} \;+\; \frac{b_{\text{global}}}{N_{\text{tensor}}} \]
  • \(b_{\text{elem}}\): bits of each element (4 in E2M1, 8 in E4M3, 16 in BF16)
  • \(b_{\text{scale}}\): bits of the block scale (8 in E8M0 and in E4M3, 32 in FP32)
  • \(n_{\text{block}}\): how many weights share the scale (16, 32, 128×128…)
  • \(b_{\text{global}}/N_{\text{tensor}}\): per-tensor scale diluted over the whole tensor (negligible)

MiMo follows the same path: about 620 GB in BF16, 310 GB in FP8 and 173 GB in the official MXFP4. In both cases, quantization is what makes it possible to run the model on two nodes.

Quantize where the parameters are

In MoE, almost all the weight lives in the routed experts: 97% in GLM (311.65 of 321.32 B), about 277 of 284 B in the DeepSeek trunk and 302.8 B of ~310 B in MiMo. That is why the recipes quantize only the experts aggressively and leave the rest at 8 or 16 bits. Each tensor group then has its own format, and the calculation becomes a sum:

\[ W \;=\; \sum_{g} N_g\,\frac{b_{\text{eff},g}}{8} \qquad\qquad W_{\text{rank}} \;=\; \frac{W - W_{\text{rep}}}{P} \;+\; W_{\text{rep}} \]
  • \(N_g\): parameters of group \(g\) (experts, dense, tables, drafter…)
  • \(b_{\text{eff},g}\): effective bits of that group's format
  • \(P\): number of tensor parallel ranks (here, 1 GPU per node)
  • \(W_{\text{rep}}\): bytes replicated on every rank (they do not shrink with TP)

The \(W_{\text{rep}}\) term changed real decisions. In Qwen, the hyper-connections (1.19 GiB), the routers and the vision tower stay replicated. Vision is replicated on purpose with --mm-encoder-tp-mode data, because slicing it would break a kernel (see Station 5). In DeepSeek, the latent KV projections and the indexer are replicated too.

Trap: GB vs. GiB

1 GiB = 1.0737 GB. The Qwen checkpoint shows up as 124 GiB, ~126 GiB, 133 GB and 135 GB in different places of the repository: those are mixed units and revisions. CUDA reports GiB (the GB10's 121.69), Hugging Face reports GB. In this chapter's calculations, weight bytes come out in GB and the node budget in GiB. Always convert before comparing.

Station 2 · The budget of a node

On a DGX Spark, CPU and GPU share the same LPDDR5X. Of the 128 GB, CUDA sees 121.69 GiB, and the operating system, the page cache, Docker and the API process compete with vLLM for that very same memory. The GMU parameter (--gpu-memory-utilization) is the fraction of that total that vLLM is allowed to budget. At boot it loads the weights and runs a profiling pass with a batch the size of MNBT (--max-num-batched-tokens: the per-step token budget, which is also the prefill chunk size) to measure peak activations. Optionally it subtracts an estimate of the CUDA graph memory, and it hands whatever is left to the KV pool:

\[ \underbrace{\text{GMU}\times M_{\text{vis}}}_{\text{budget}} \;=\; W_{\text{rank}} \;+\; \underbrace{O_{\text{non-torch}} + A_{\text{peak}}}_{O\ (\text{overhead})} \;+\; \underbrace{\epsilon\,G_{\text{est}}}_{\text{only if estimating graphs}} \;+\; \text{KV}_{\text{pool}} \] \[ F_{\text{host}} \;\approx\; M_{\text{vis}} \;-\; \big(W_{\text{rank}} + O + \text{KV}_{\text{pool}} + G_{\text{real}}\big) \;-\; H_{\text{ext}} \]
  • \(M_{\text{vis}}\): 121.69 GiB visible to CUDA per GB10
  • \(W_{\text{rank}}\): weights loaded on this rank (Station 1)
  • \(O\): CUDA/NCCL context outside torch + peak activations from profiling (includes the workspaces, which grow with MNBT)
  • \(\epsilon\): 1 if VLLM_MEMORY_PROFILER_ESTIMATE_CUDAGRAPHS=1 (the profiler subtracts the graph estimate \(G_{\text{est}}\)), 0 otherwise; \(G_{\text{real}}\) is what the graphs actually occupy
  • \(\text{KV}_{\text{pool}}\): the remainder, or a fixed value with --kv-cache-memory-bytes
  • \(H_{\text{ext}}\): memory that the process and the driver allocate beyond the GMU budget. On Qwen, with the node idle, 114.5 − 101.61 = 12.9 GiB, of which 0.54 are the graphs that were not subtracted; the LAB uses 12.4 GiB plus \(G_{\text{real}}\)
  • \(F_{\text{host}}\): estimated host headroom with the node idle. This is what the OS, the page cache and the load will eat into

GraphsCG_ESTIMATE → VLLM_MEMORY_PROFILER_ESTIMATE_CUDAGRAPHS

With the default (1), the profiler records a test capture, estimates how much the CUDA graphs will take up and subtracts that from the pool. On GLM the estimate was 2.43 GiB, but the graphs actually captured consume −0.19 GiB: "~2.6 GiB of KV is reserved and never used". With CG_ESTIMATE=0 the graphs stay on and only the subtraction goes away (and the patch_skip_cudagraph_profile.py overlay skips the test capture, 18 → 7 s of profiling). DeepSeek pins VLLM_MEMORY_PROFILER_ESTIMATE_CUDAGRAPHS=0: the pool gets bigger and the graphs' real memory comes out of the host headroom. The trade-off: with 1 you lose KV to a pessimistic estimate; with 0 you gain KV and pay for the graphs out of the OS margin. With the pool fixed in bytes, the estimate does not touch the KV.

Deep dive: why fix the pool

On UMA, profiling is unstable. The activation peak depends on the loader (InstantTensor cost GLM ~5 GiB of KV), on multimodal profiling (which "inflates the peak" on MiMo) and on the state of the page cache. vLLM also refuses to start if the free memory at startup is smaller than GMU × total: with 108.79 GiB free, the practical ceiling sits near GMU 0.89. Fixing the pool in bytes makes the boot deterministic, but the GMU stops governing the KV, and the host headroom becomes your responsibility. Details in Unified memory and PagedAttention. Watch out for the name: the GLM recipe uses --kv-cache-memory-bytes, and the Qwen log suggests --kv-cache-memory=<bytes>. Check with vllm serve --help=all on your image.

Station 3 · KV: bytes per token, capacity and concurrency

Every request occupies the pool in two ways. One part grows with the context: full-attention, MLA or compressed layers store something per token. Another part is fixed: the state of the linear attention layers (one matrix per head, whatever the context length) and the sliding windows (SWA) of the target and of the drafter. The accounting per layer type goes like this:

\[ b^{\text{GQA}}_{\ell} = \Big\lceil \tfrac{h_{kv}}{P} \Big\rceil (d_k + d_v)\,\beta \qquad b^{\text{MLA}}_{\ell} = (d_c + d_{\text{rope}})\,\beta \;+\; \text{scales} \qquad b^{\text{comp}}_{\ell} = \frac{b^{\text{row}}}{r} \] \[ s^{\text{linear}}_{\ell} = \frac{h_{\text{lin}}}{P}\,d_k\,d_v\,\beta_{\text{state}} \qquad s^{\text{SWA}}_{\ell} = w\cdot b_\ell \] \[ b_{\text{tok}} = \sum_{\ell\,\in\,\text{growing}} b_\ell \qquad S_{\text{seq}} = \sum_{\ell\,\in\,\text{fixed}} s_\ell \]
  • \(h_{kv}\): K/V heads (if \(h_{kv} < P\), each rank keeps a copy)
  • \(d_k, d_v\): K and V dimensions per head (192 and 128 in MiMo)
  • \(\beta\): bytes per KV element (2 in BF16, 1 in FP8)
  • \(d_c + d_{\text{rope}}\): MLA latent (512) + RoPE part (64); the latent is the same on every rank
  • \(r\): compression ratio (4 or 128 in DeepSeek)
  • \(w\): SWA window (128 in DeepSeek and in MiMo)
  • \(b_{\text{tok}}\): bytes per token per rank; \(S_{\text{seq}}\): fixed state per request

With that, each request costs a growing part plus a fixed part, and the concurrency for a context \(L\) comes out of a division:

\[ \text{KV}_{\text{req}}(L) \;=\; g\,b_{\text{tok}}\,L \;+\; S_{\text{fix}} \qquad C(L) \;=\; \frac{\text{KV}_{\text{pool}}}{\text{KV}_{\text{req}}(L)} \qquad N_{\text{log}} \;=\; C(L_{\max})\,L_{\max} \qquad B_{\text{eff}} = \min\big(\texttt{max\_num\_seqs},\ \lfloor C(L) \rfloor\big) \]
  • \(g\): per-token allocator factor (≥ 1): the page or group padding that grows with the context
  • \(S_{\text{fix}} \ge S_{\text{seq}}\): fixed per-request cost, the physical state plus the block IDs the allocator charges for each group
  • \(C(L)\): how many requests of \(L\) tokens fit at the same time (the log's "Maximum concurrency", with \(L = L_{\max}\))
  • \(N_{\text{log}}\): the log's "GPU KV cache size". It is not the pool size: it is concurrency × cap
  • \(B_{\text{eff}}\): the real decode batch, limited by the scheduler or by the pool

Now look at the mechanism. In the figure, the pool is a parking lot of pages (PagedAttention). Each request is a car with its own color: it takes solid squares for the growing part, hatched ones for the physical state and dotted ones for the block IDs that the allocator charges on top of the state. Increase the context or the number of requests until you run out of spaces. Requests that do not fit go to the queue, and vLLM does not OOM in that case: it preempts and redoes the prefill later. Switch to GLM and shorten the context: the dotted squares do not shrink.

DeepSeek is the neatest example: with a 1M cap and 6 slots, 6 × 50k fit comfortably, 6 × 200k (1.2M tokens) also fit, 6 × 500k is right at the limit and 6 × 1M is impossible. That is what the recipe's README says and what the figure shows. Compression explains why 1M fits: without it, 43 layers × 584 B would come to ~25 KB per token (26 GB per 1M request), and a typical GQA-8 BF16 would come to ~176 KB per token.

The allocator tax: per token and per request

If you divide 17.04 GiB by 3,850 B, you get ~4.75 million tokens. The DeepSeek boot says 2,331,430. The difference comes from vLLM's hybrid allocator, which charges in two ways. Per token: it requires the same physical page for all KV groups and raises the attention block size until the page holds the largest state (3584 tokens in GLM, 1600 to 3200 in Qwen, 37,376 B pages in DeepSeek). When that unification, or the grouping of layers (MiMo's 9 global ones), leaves padding that grows with the context, that is \(g\). Per request: each state group takes a few block IDs, whatever the context, and each ID costs one page across all the layers of the group. On GLM an ID costs 7,579 B × 3584 = 25.9 MiB, and each request takes 1 kpool ID, 4 KDA groups × (2 + k) IDs, plus the IDs of the drafter's windows.

\[ S_{\text{fix}} \;\approx\; n_{\text{IDs}}\;p_{\text{blk}}, \quad p_{\text{blk}} = n_{\text{layers/group}}\; b_\ell\; B_{\text{page}} \qquad\qquad \text{one boot: } \frac{\text{KV}_{\text{pool}}}{C_{\text{log}}} \;=\; g\,b_{\text{tok}}\,L_{\max} + S_{\text{fix}} \]
  • \(n_{\text{IDs}}\): fixed block IDs per request (state groups × (2 + k), kpool, windows)
  • \(B_{\text{page}}\): tokens per block after page-size unification (3584 in GLM TP2)
  • \(C_{\text{log}}\): the boot's "Maximum concurrency for \(L_{\max}\) tokens"

A single boot does not separate the two terms (with \(L_{\max}\) fixed, any mix of \(g\) and \(S_{\text{fix}}\) reproduces the same line); two boots do. For Qwen there are two same-day boots at 262k, with FP8 and BF16 KV (the state apparently in FP32; the README does not say so explicitly). Fixing \(g = 1\) and calibrating \(S_{\text{fix}}\) on the FP8 boot (≈ 0.47 GiB), the model predicts the BF16 boot with a 0.6% error (Figure 47.10); solving both together gives \(g = 0.985\). For GLM, the README hands you the decomposition ready-made: "~7.4 GiB fixed + 7.1 GiB per 1M" (MNBT 7168, without compact pages), and the 7.1 GiB per 1M are exactly the 7,579 physical B/token, that is, \(g = 1\). Calibrating \(S_{\text{fix}}\) on a boot at 850k (14 GiB → 1.03×, which gives 7.57 GiB fixed), the model predicts 14.6 GiB for a 1M request and 11.1 GiB for 500k. The README measures 14.52 and 10.98. With compact pages, the fixed term drops to ~1.58 GiB, and two boots (11 GiB → 1.45× and 14 GiB → 1.85×) give the same value. For DeepSeek and MiMo all published boots use the same \(L_{\max}\), so the LAB follows the recipes' own reading and treats the excess as a per-token cost (\(g \approx 2.04\) and \(\approx 1.15\)); the DeepSeek doc notes that the physical concurrency at 1M should be higher than the log's 2.22×. For your model, bring up the same pool with two max-model-len values and solve the two equations.

Caps, not reservations

--max-model-len and --max-num-seqs are admission limits and do not reserve memory. The real constraint is that the sum of the costs of the live requests fits in the pool. The fixed term explains the GLM recipe's warning not to lower the cap to 256k "to free up slots": shortening the context does not touch the block IDs of the state groups. In the LAB, with 11 GiB and compact pages, 850k → 256k takes concurrency from 1.45× to ~3.2×, not to the ~4.6× of a per-token-only calculation. The "GPU KV cache size" drops from 1.23 M to ~0.84 M, because it is \(C(L_{\max}) \times L_{\max}\) and shrinks along with the cap without anything physical changing. Without compact pages (~7.6 GiB fixed), a 256k request would cost ~70% of an 850k one.

State--mamba-ssm-cache-dtype bfloat16

The recurrent state of the 36 GDN layers comes in FP32 in the checkpoint. Storing it in BF16 halves the "Mamba" page, vLLM picks a smaller attention block, and the same pool goes from 3.65 M to 4.24–4.38 M tokens. In the model above, what changes is the fixed term (≈ 0.47 → ≈ 0.09 GiB per request), because each block ID now costs a smaller page. The decode gain measured on the single-node kit was +6.8% on one stream. If you write a .env from scratch without this key, start.sh silently falls back to FP32.

Station 4 · The speed ceiling

Decode is bound by memory bandwidth: at every step, the GPU has to read all the active weights, the KV and the request state all over again. FLOPs barely matter. The lab uses a step cost model that adds up the bytes read, divides by the effective bandwidth and adds the latency of the collectives:

\[ t_{\text{step}} \;\approx\; \frac{B_{\text{exp}} + B_{\text{dense}} + B_{\text{draft}} + B_{\text{KV}} + B_{\text{state}}}{\mathrm{BW}_{\text{eff}}} \;+\; n_{\text{col}}\;t_{\text{col}} \] \[ \bar E \;=\; E\,\Big[1 - \big(1 - \tfrac{1}{E}\big)^{k_e\,T}\Big], \quad T = B\,(k+1) \qquad\qquad B_{\text{exp}} \;=\; n_{\text{MoE}}\;\bar E\;\frac{s_{\text{exp}}}{P} \]
  • \(\mathrm{BW}_{\text{eff}}\): effective bandwidth (273 GB/s theoretical on the GB10; 230–245 GB/s realistic)
  • \(\bar E\): distinct experts touched per layer in the step
  • \(E, k_e\): experts per layer and the router's top-k
  • \(T\): tokens verified in the step (\(B\) sequences × \(k+1\) positions)
  • \(s_{\text{exp}}\): bytes of one expert in the chosen format
  • \(n_{\text{col}}, t_{\text{col}}\): collectives per step (~2 per layer) and the latency of each one

The \(\bar E\) formula is the "sampling with replacement" one: \(k_e T\) picks among \(E\) experts, assuming uniform, independent routing. With 1 token and top-8 you read 8 experts per layer. With 8 tokens (speculation with k=7) you do not read 64: on GLM, with 288 experts, the expected value is \(\bar E \approx 57\). That is why speculating on MoE pays off less than on a dense model: verifying more tokens forces you to read more experts.

\[ \tau \;=\; 1 + \sum_{i=1}^{k} a_i \;\overset{\text{geom.}}{=}\; \frac{1 - \alpha^{k+1}}{1 - \alpha} \qquad\quad \text{tok/s per stream} = \frac{\tau}{t_{\text{step}}} \qquad\quad \text{aggregate} = B\,\frac{\tau}{t_{\text{step}}} \]
  • \(\tau\): tokens per step (accepted + 1 corrected/bonus)
  • \(a_i\): fraction of steps in which position \(i\) was accepted (measured curve)
  • \(\alpha\): constant conditional acceptance per position (geometric model)

Let's do one calculation by hand with DeepSeek (TP2, DSpark k=6, 1 sequence). The step verifies 7 tokens × top-6 = 42 picks among 256 experts, which gives \(\bar E = 256\,[1-(255/256)^{42}] \approx 38.8\). An MXFP4 expert is 25.17 M × 4.25/8 ≈ 13.4 MB, or 6.68 MB per rank. Reading 43 layers × 38.8 × 6.68 MB gives ≈ 11.2 GB. Adding FP8 attention (2.4 GB), lm_head (0.53 GB) and the drafter (1.63 GB), we reach ≈ 15.7 GB per rank. At 230 GB/s that takes 68 ms; with 108 collectives of ~40 µs, the step comes to ≈ 73 ms. The closest per-position acceptance curve available comes from the TP=3 probe in the fable5-1 report (0.92 / 0.74 / 0.60 / 0.41 / 0.28 / 0.21), that is, from a different topology. It gives \(\tau \approx 4.16\), and the result is ≈ 57 tok/s. The report estimated 16.5–17 GB, 68–74 ms and ~4.15 tokens per step, which gives 56–61 tok/s. The median measured on 2026-09-02 was 56 tok/s, but in that same A/B the acceptance on natural prose was 24.7–27.2%, and the \(\tau\) of that run was not published. The agreement is indicative, not a validation. In the figure below you can repeat the calculation for each model and see where the time goes.

Look at GLM on prose (low α): the speculative step reads three times as many bytes as the plain step, because \(\bar E\) goes from 8 to ~57, and it accepts little. The gain almost vanishes. That is why the recipe created GLM53_ADAPTIVE_K, which verifies only 2, 4 or 7 drafts depending on a moving average of the acceptance: +13–21% on prose, with no loss at temperature 0.

Deep dive: when the sampling assumption fails

The \(\bar E\) formula assumes that each token picks its experts independently. In code and structured text, neighboring tokens tend to use the same experts (our interpretation), so the real \(\bar E\) ends up below the prediction and the ceiling goes up. In the validation at the end of the chapter, MiMo on code measures 70.5 tok/s, above our ceiling at 230 GB/s. When the measurement exceeds the ceiling, some assumption broke: here, probably independence (or the effective bandwidth is higher). To confirm, instrument how many distinct experts each step touches.

The measured curves show something else: acceptance drops with position, and it drops much faster on prose. Station 5 · Topology and divisibility

With one GPU per node, TP is the number of nodes. Each rank holds \(1/P\) of every sliced matrix, and so every sliced dimension has to divide cleanly among the ranks. When a dimension does not divide, there are three ways out: padding with fake units, replicating, or switching strategy (EP instead of TP inside the experts). The lab checks the divisibility rules below:

  1. Attention heads \(h_q \bmod P = 0\). If it does not work out, you need padding with zero-output heads, and the kernel may demand more: FlashInfer SM120 only instantiates {8, 16, 32, 64, 128} local heads, so GLM on TP3 (22 per rank) pads up to 32 inside the kernel and slices back.
  2. KV heads: in GQA, \(h_{kv} \bmod P = 0\), or replication when \(h_{kv} < P\). In MLA/MQA there is a single latent, replicated on every rank: the KV does not shrink with TP (DeepSeek).
  3. Experts: with expert parallel, \(E \bmod P = 0\). Without EP, the intermediate size \(m/P\) must be a multiple of the format's block (NVFP4 16, MXFP4 32, FP8 128) in all sliced experts, including those in extra layers such as MTP.
  4. Vocabulary: vLLM pads the vocabulary to a multiple of 64 and requires the result to divide by \(P\). On TP3, GLM (154,880, already a multiple of 64) does not divide, and the recipe's overlay changes the padding to \(\operatorname{lcm}(64, 3) = 192\): 154,880 → 154,944.
  5. Model extras: output projection groups (DeepSeek, 8), vision MLP (Qwen, 4304), drafter heads (GLM, 32/8), QKV pre-sliced in the checkpoint (MiMo, for TP=4).
\[ V_{\text{pad}} = \Big\lceil \tfrac{V}{64} \Big\rceil 64,\ \ P \mid V_{\text{pad}}\ \ \big(\text{overlay: } \operatorname{lcm}(64,P)\big) \qquad n_{\text{col}} \approx 2L + \text{extras} \qquad t_{\text{col}}(P) \approx t_{\text{col}}(2)\,(P-1) \qquad \text{bytes/all-reduce} = d \times 2\,\text{B} \times T \]
  • \(V\): vocabulary; \(P\): ranks
  • \(n_{\text{col}}\): one all-reduce after attention and another after the MoE, per layer (Qwen ~106, DeepSeek ~108, MiMo 96 per step)
  • \(t_{\text{col}}\): 15–50 µs per collective on the recipes' RoCE; the \(P-1\) term is the latency term of a ring all-reduce (α–β model)
  • \(d\): hidden; in MiMo, 4096 × 2 B = 8 KB per token

A wrong shard is silent

Wrong divisibility rarely hangs the boot. Most of the time it produces garbage that looks like a working model. GLM tried padding to 96 heads on TP3: one rank ended up with only fake KDA heads and the logits collapsed. The path that worked was 66 (22 per rank). On DeepSeek, the extra group needs zeroed wo_a/wo_b and attn_sink = −∞, and the boot must print DSv4 TP pad: heads 64 -> 72 for TP=3. On MiMo, if the global sliding_window leaks into the global layers, they start seeing only 128 tokens. Always validate with a needle test and a numerical comparison. See TP=3 and TP=4 and Parallelism.

Topology--enable-expert-parallel --all2all-backend allgather_reducescatter

With EP, each GPU holds 256 of the 512 experts whole, and the 640-wide experts do not need to be sliced into 320. The recipe calls EP mandatory ("Required for NVFP4"), but the reason is not the routed experts (in NVFP4, with a block of 16, 320 would work): it is the MTP layer, whose experts are FP8 in 128×128 blocks. The nvidia card asks for EP so that "the MTP expert width remains compatible with its 128x128 FP8 blocks". The recipe's report found that, with DP=1, the all-to-all backend is never invoked: the tokens are already replicated by TP, each rank computes its local experts and the result goes into the same all-reduce. Here EP only changes which weights each GPU holds, and the collectives stay the same.

The recipe builder

Now the five stations work together. Pick a preset, which loads the real architecture and launch parameters of each recipe, or "free fields" to type in a new model. Then move any control. The four panels and the alert list update instantly, and the next figure writes the matching .env and vllm serve skeleton.

  1. Model and weights Expert format (BF16, FP8, NVFP4, MXFP4 or EXL3 with adjustable bpw) and dense format (checkpoint native, BF16 or FP8).
  2. Topology and memory Nodes (= TP), EP, GMU or fixed pool, graphs inside or outside the budget, and the two allocator terms (per-token \(g\) and the per-request fixed term, which go back to the preset value when you change TP, dtype or speculation).
  3. Serving max-model-len (cap), typical context in use, max-num-seqs, MNBT (tokens per step and prefill chunk; it only generates alerts, see "does not compute"), KV dtype and linear state dtype.
  4. Speculation and hardware Method, k, α (with buttons for the measured profiles) and effective bandwidth.

Four experiments to do right now, each with a recipe lesson:

  1. Change GLM's format. With the GLM preset, switch the experts to FP8: "does not fit" appears (328 GB). Go back to EXL3 and raise the bpw to 6 (TR3 K6 is 254 GB with KLD 0.0137, much better than 0.0246). At ~118 GiB per rank it still does not fit on TP2. Move to 3 nodes: the weights fit, but now the divisibility alerts light up (64 heads, the EXL3 trellis intermediate, which calls for EP, and the vocabulary). Turn on expert parallel and the experts alert goes away: 288 ÷ 3 = 96 per rank. A KV alert remains: on TP3 the fixed term calibrated on the kit is ~5.3 GiB, and the 11 GiB pool cannot hold one 850k request. Raise the fixed pool; the recipe's TP3 variant uses 32–40 GiB. Those are exactly the trade-offs it makes.
  2. Fill DeepSeek with long requests. With a 1M cap, raise the context in use up to 1M: concurrency drops to ~2.3× (the real log measured 2.22×), and the alert warns that only 2 requests of 1M can coexist. The others will be preempted and redone. Lower the context to 200k and the 6 slots fit again.
  3. Take the FP8 off Qwen's KV. Switch the KV to BF16: the log tokens drop ~1.85× in the builder with the state in BF16, and 1.71× with the state in FP32 (switch the state too to check), exactly the ratio the recipe measured (3.65 M ÷ 2.13 M). In the builder there is still headroom for the 8 slots at 262k. Interpretation: that is why the vLLM 0.30 lane, whose stock QSA only accepts BF16, runs with BF16 KV by default (FP8 is optional, via a backport, with 2.64 M tokens). But there the measurement was 1,467,072 tokens (5.6×) at GMU 0.80, well below the LAB's ~2.2 M: a different engine, and a fixed term not calibrated on that image. It is the second instructive error in Figure 47.10.
  4. Compare prose and structured. In any preset with speculation, toggle the α buttons. On prose the speculative speedometer almost touches the non-speculative needle: the step reads many more experts and accepts little.

A skeleton is not a recipe

The generator does not know whether your image has each flag, whether the kernels exist for sm_121 or whether the model needs an overlay. The MiMo tutorial reminds you that --kv-cache-dtype fp8 is silently ignored without the right overlay, and that the QKV ends up zeroed with certain loaders. Run vllm serve --help=all on the pinned image and make start.sh fail if any flag does not exist. See Recipe engineering and Recipe from scratch.

Predicted vs. measured: calibrating the lab

A model only deserves trust after it has been compared with measurements. The final figure runs the lab on the closest available configurations to the ones in which the recipes measured something and puts estimate and measurement side by side. When the measurement and the model's input come from different runs, the table says so. The estimated/measured ratio appears for each pair. The MiMo tutorial gives the exit criterion: a deviation of up to ±10% is acceptable; beyond that, find the reason before moving on.

What the validation teaches:

  • Weights land within 1–4%. Qwen comes in ~3% low (replicated parts the model does not know in detail). GLM comes in ~1.5% low, because overlay buffers are included in the log's number.
  • Pool lands within ~5% for Qwen and DeepSeek, with the measured overhead. MiMo at GMU 0.90 with the multimodal tower is the instructive error: we estimated ~23 GiB and the kit measured 13.89 GiB, because multimodal profiling inflates the activation peak. The tutorial's 4 GiB overhead does not hold in that configuration.
  • Log tokens: calibrated on one boot, the per-request model hits the other boots of the same image within ~1% for GLM (14 GiB), Qwen (BF16 KV) and MiMo (BF16 KV), and within ~8% for DeepSeek on TP3. Qwen's vLLM 0.30 lane is the second instructive error: ~1.5× above the measurement, because it is a different engine with a different allocator, and the fixed term was not calibrated on it.
  • Decode lands between 76% and 98% of the ceiling for GLM, Qwen and DeepSeek, which is the gap between pure bandwidth and kernels, CPU and synchronization. MiMo on code goes past the ceiling and exposes the independent-routing assumption (Station 4).

For Qwen, the recipe itself reads the same number differently. It estimates ~9.2 GiB read per step, 40–42 ms at 235–245 GB/s, multiplies by ~2.2 tokens per step (the accepted ones, without the bonus), arrives at 53–56 tok/s and compares that with 55.8 measured without MTP: "batch-1 is already at the bandwidth roofline". The LAB uses \(\tau = 1 +\) accepted \(= 3.23\), from the per-position curve, and therefore puts the measurement at 76% of the ceiling. The recipe's reruns settle it: the sweep.py runs from Sep 11 and 16, with the full vocabulary and S=1, measured 2.9–3.8 tokens/step (with the bonus; from prose to code) and 59.3–61.2 ms/step. \(\tau\) really is ~3.2, and the real step is ~40–50% slower than the bandwidth allows; the report's "roofline" came from 2.2 accepted without the bonus and from an old measurement without MTP. The product matched by accident, which is why ms/step and tokens/step are measured separately (Benchmarking).

The calibration cycle

  1. Predict Run the builder with your configuration and write down weights/rank, pool, tokens, concurrency and decode ceiling.
  2. Boot and read the log Model loading took X GiB, Available KV cache memory, GPU KV cache size, Maximum concurrency for L tokens. Recalibrate the overhead \(O\) (and check whether the graphs came out of the pool) and the fixed term \(S_{\text{fix}}\). To separate \(g\) from \(S_{\text{fix}}\), do a second boot with a different max-model-len.
  3. Measure under load Minimum MemAvailable on both nodes during a long prefill + concurrent decodes. If the real headroom is smaller than predicted, adjust \(H_{\text{ext}}\) and lower the GMU or the pool.
  4. Measure the decode With real prompts per category (the random dataset tanks acceptance), at C=1 and on the concurrency ladder. Compare with the ceiling. The MiMo tutorial says to expect 50–65% of it on a first boot without optimization; the optimized recipes reach 76–98%. If you land well below 50%, look for a slow kernel, a missing graph (eager) or a collective on the wrong path (NET/Socket instead of NET/IB).
  5. Change one variable at a time In A/B/A, with the decision rule written down before the test, and record the negative results in the CHANGELOG too. See Benchmarking.
Lab checklist before the first boot

(1) Weights per rank < budget, with the right format in each group. (2) Pool ≥ one request of max-model-len, otherwise the boot refuses. (3) Estimated idle host headroom ≥ 11 GiB; between 8 and 11, only with MemAvailable measured under load; below 8, run a soak before adopting. (4) No divisibility ✕ at the chosen TP. (5) k compatible with the drafter (block, n_predict) and capture sizes in multiples of \(k+1\). (6) MNBT ≥ max-num-seqs × (k+1). (7) KV dtype supported by the model's backend. (8) Every skeleton flag checked against the image.
