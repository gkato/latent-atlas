With two nodes, splitting an LLM is elegant: 64 attention heads become 32 + 32 and a 2048-column expert becomes 1024 + 1024. Put a third DGX Spark on the table and the math turns into 64 ÷ 3 = 21.33…; there is no such thing as a third of a head. This chapter shows what happens when the model's arithmetic doesn't match the cluster's: padding of heads, groups and vocabulary, whole experts on each node, cables in a directed ring, and the declared TP=4 workaround for a hang nobody has explained. By the end you'll be able to audit any new model starting from a single question: does this divide by N?

Why three is not two plus one

Recall tensor parallel (TP), which the chapter Splitting the model takes apart in detail. Every weight matrix is cut into slices, one per GPU, and each GPU is a rank. In a column-parallel layer the columns (outputs) are split; in a row-parallel one, the rows (inputs), and the partial sums are combined in an all-reduce. In Mia's recipes each Spark is a rank, so “TP=3” means three machines linked by cables.

\[ Y = XW = X\,\big[\,W_0 \mid W_1 \mid \cdots \mid W_{p-1}\,\big], \qquad W_r \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times n/p} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad p \mid n \]
  • \(X\): activations [tokens × d]
  • \(W\): weight [d × n], cut into \(p\) column slices
  • \(p\): TP degree (number of ranks)
  • \(n/p\): local width of each rank
  • \(p \mid n\): “p divides n”, the width must be a multiple of \(p\)

The condition \(p \mid n\) looks innocent until you factor the models' dimensions. Designers pick powers of two because the hardware works in tiles of 16, 64 and 128: 64 = 2⁶, 2048 = 2¹¹, 4096 = 2¹². GLM's vocabulary, 154,880, is 2⁸·5·11², and DeepSeek's, 129,280, is 2⁸·5·101. The factor 3 is a foreign prime in these numbers and only shows up by accident, as in GLM's 288 experts (2⁵·3²) and its dense MLP of 12,288 (2¹²·3). That's why TP=2 and TP=4 “just work” and TP=3 turns into an engineering project.

Not everything is sharded. Some parts are replicated, with a full copy on every rank, and then divisibility doesn't matter: the residual stream (hidden 4096) is complete on every rank after each all-reduce; the latent KV of MLA is a single vector per token; DeepSeek's indexer is a ReplicatedLinear. Classifying each dimension as sharded, replicated or distributed across experts is the first step of any multi-node recipe.

In the figure, pick a model and a TP degree: each row is a real dimension from config.json split across the ranks.

Notice: at TP=4 GLM has no amber or red row (64/4 = 16; 2048/4 = 512 = 32 tiles of 16, so the experts get sharded and EP isn't even needed; drafter 8/2), and the TP=4 lane needs no shape patches. At TP=3 almost everything needs help, each item in its own way. And the gray rows never change: replicated data doesn't shrink when you add nodes (section 9). Watch out for DeepSeek's mint row: “256 experts on every rank” is not replication. The experts are all there, but each rank stores only 704 of each one's 2112 columns, and those bytes do shrink with \(p\).

Analogy

A 64-slice pizza for three friends. You can put two “air” slices in the box and serve 22 to each (padding), hand out whole pizzas of different flavors (expert parallel) or give everyone an identical pizza (replication). What you can't do is cut one slice into three and hope nobody notices.

The worst error is the silent one

When the division isn't exact, stock code sometimes doesn't even complain. With DeepSeek, unpatched vLLM either refuses to start (64 % 3 != 0) or silently computes 8 // 3 == 2 local groups and drops the remainder, producing fluent, wrong text. That's why DeepSeek's TP=3 lane ships with a functional validator (section 3).

The arithmetic of padding: ceiling, lcm and waste

If \(p\) doesn't divide \(n\), the most common way out is padding: grow the dimension to the next convenient multiple and fill the excess with something inert. The math is a round-up:

\[ n' = \left\lceil \frac{n}{L} \right\rceil L, \qquad L = \operatorname{lcm}(a,\, p), \qquad n_{\text{local}} = \frac{n'}{p}, \qquad \text{waste} = \frac{n' - n}{n} \]
  • \(n\): real dimension, the one in the checkpoint
  • \(p\): TP degree
  • \(a\): alignment already required (64 for the vocabulary, 64 on the K axis of 4-bit kernels, 1 for counting heads)
  • \(L\): rounding step, the least common multiple
  • \(n'\): padded dimension
  • \(n_{\text{local}}\): each rank's slice

The lcm meets two requirements at once: \(n'\) stays a multiple of \(a\) and becomes a multiple of \(p\). Since 3 is coprime to every power of two, \(\operatorname{lcm}(64,3) = 192 = 3 \times 64\), and the local slice also comes out a multiple of 64. DeepSeek uses this for the expert width: 2048 → 2112 = 11 × 192, with 704 = 11 × 64 columns per rank, preserving the 64-alignment the 4-bit kernel requires on each rank's K axis.

What goes inside the padding

Zero is the natural candidate. A zero weight produces a zero activation, which goes through the all-reduce without adding anything: zero padding is inert in sums and products (in SwiGLU, silu(0) · 0 = 0). But zero isn't neutral for every operation. In a mean, extra elements change the denominator; in a softmax, e⁰ = 1 adds a new term. Wherever the pad touches an operation that isn't a sum, you need that operation's neutral element. Section 3 shows the case of a softmax with a sink, where the neutral element is −∞.

Going deeper: the neutral element of each operation

Sum: 0. Product: 1 (or 0, if the goal is to cancel). Max and softmax: −∞, because e−∞ = 0. Mean and normalization: don't count the pad in the denominator. This is a didactic generalization; the recipes handle each case in code.

Loading weights the disk doesn't have

The checkpoint keeps its original shapes; what grew is the parameter on the GPU. The stock weight loader does narrow(dim, rank × shard, shard) and requires matching shapes, which blows up on the last rank. In DeepSeek, wq_b has 64 × 512 = 32,768 real rows. With 72 heads each rank expects 12,288, and 3 × 12,288 = 36,864 > 32,768: ranks 0 and 1 load 12,288 real rows and rank 2 finds only 8,192, filling the other 4,096 with zeros. The patches in both recipes (the edited parameter.py in DeepSeek and the _pad_then_narrow of GLM's TP3 overlay) copy the intersection with what exists and zero out the rest.

# simplified pseudocode of the "pad-aware" loader
start = tp_rank * shard                   # where this rank's slice would start
avail = max(0, min(real - start, shard))  # how much of it exists in the checkpoint
dst.zero_()                               # padding = zeros
if avail > 0:
    dst[:avail].copy_(src[start:start + avail])
A real trap (DeepSeek)

A replicated parameter also receives the tp_rank. If the loader offsets the read by the rank's position, ranks 1 and 2 start past the end of the tensor and zero everything, silently, on 2 of the 3 ranks. The patch detects “shard equal to the real size” and loads the whole tensor on all of them. The merged-column loader (gate/up) failed with start (12) + length (6) exceeds dimension size (16) and got the same bounded copy.

DeepSeek-V4: groups, the r = 4096 contract and the −∞ sink

DeepSeek's TP=3 patch only makes sense once you look at how its attention ends. The 64 heads with head_dim 512 would form a 32,768-dimensional output. Instead of a direct 32,768 → 4096 projection (134 M parameters per layer), DeepSeek-V4 uses a grouped low-rank output projection: o_groups = 8 groups of 8 heads, each group going through wo_a[g]: 4096 → 1024 (the o_lora_rank), and the concatenation (8192) through wo_b: 8192 → 4096. Half the parameters (67 M); details in Shrinking the KV.

\[ o_g = \big[\,h_{8g} \,\|\, h_{8g+1} \,\|\, \cdots \,\|\, h_{8g+7}\,\big] \in \mathbb{R}^{r},\quad r = h_g\, d_h = 8 \times 512 = 4096, \qquad y = W_b\,\big[\, W_a^{(0)} o_0 \,\|\, \cdots \,\|\, W_a^{(7)} o_7 \,\big] \]
  • \(h_i\): output of head \(i\) (512 dims)
  • \(h_g\): heads per group (8)
  • \(d_h\): head_dim (512)
  • \(r\): input of each wo_a, fixed in the checkpoint (wo_a = [8192, 4096])
  • \(W_a^{(g)}\): 4096 → 1024, one per group
  • \(W_b\): 8192 → 4096, row-parallel + all-reduce

The 8 wo_a projections run as a single BMM in FP8, einsum("bhr,hdr->bhd"), and the kernel checks the shapes: \(r\) must be 4096. Stock vLLM shards groups across ranks and keeps 8 heads per group (4 groups per rank at TP=2, 2 at TP=4, 1 at TP=8), so \(r\) never changes. At TP=3, 8 groups don't divide evenly. The figure walks through the attempts in the order they were made.

The lesson is that the kernel's shape contract rules. R1 replicated the 8 groups on every rank, padded each group from 8 to 9 heads (72 in total, the 9th made of zeros) and gave 3 heads of each group to every rank: \(r = 3 \times 512 = 1536\) on all ranks, which the kernel rejects (einsum.hpp:164: m == m_ and n == n_ and k == k_). Padding wo_a up to 4608 only pushes it further from the activation's shape (1536) and the checkpoint's (4096), and the only count that would restore 8 heads per group under R1 would be 192, three times the model's head count. R2 flips the logic: pad the group count (8 → 9) and keep the heads per group. That's 72 heads, 24 heads and 3 groups per rank; wo_a now has 9 × 1024 = 9216 outputs (3072 per rank) and \(r\) stays 4096.

The sink has to be −∞

The 9th group is pure padding, with null wo_a and wo_b. But every DeepSeek-V4 head also has an attention sink, a learned logit that enters only the softmax denominator and lets the head “attend to nothing”:

\[ p_j = \frac{e^{s_j}}{e^{\sigma} + \sum_{k} e^{s_k}} \qquad\qquad \sigma_{\text{pad}} = -\infty \;\Rightarrow\; e^{\sigma_{\text{pad}}} = 0 \]
  • \(s_j\): attention score of key \(j\)
  • \(\sigma\): sink logit (one per head)
  • \(p_j\): attention weight; \(\sum_j p_j \le 1\), the leftover “goes to the sink”
  • \(\sigma_{\text{pad}}\): value used in the padding slots

According to the patch, with a sink of 0 in the padding, e⁰ = 1 would add a phantom term to the denominator and attenuate the real heads; with −∞ the term is zero, the neutral element from the previous section. That's why the sink doesn't go through the generic loader (which does zero_()) and has its own loaders that keep −∞ in the pad. Play with the phantom term below to see the damage.

The other edits in apply_tp3_patch.py

The applied patch (the “R2” version, vendored from a 3× DGX Spark repository under the MIT license) touches other spots, and each one teaches something:

  1. The boot blocker lives in the config config/model.py validates “heads divisible by TP” before the model class even exists (the SpeculativeConfig runs into it first). The patch accepts the padded count and logs DSv4 TP pad: heads 64 -> 72 for TP=3 (o_groups=8), which must show up on every rank.
  2. The error that looks like a network problem With padding_size 64, the vocabulary stays at 129,280 and the embedding dies with “129280 is not divisible by 3” after all three ranks have joined the NCCL group. It looks distributed, but it's padding: padding_size = lcm(64, tp) gives 129,408 (43,136 per rank).
  3. Experts: width and a misleading assert The width goes from 2048 to 2112 (704 per rank), with the real value in intermediate_size_per_partition_unpadded, since here the experts are sharded (EP off). An assert requires the 256 experts to be divisible by TP and suggests num_redundant_experts; following the hint (258) triggers another assert, “Redundant experts are only supported with EPLB”. The guarded values only matter for MegaMoE/EP, and the patch restricts the assert to those paths. The error message is not the diagnosis. And there's an open question: the expert loaders narrow the tensor to the parameter's shape, so columns 2048–2111 keep whatever was in the allocation. The patch itself warns that correctness depends on that parameter starting out zeroed (garbage in the tail would leak into the SwiGLU product) and marks the spot as “THIS IS THE UNVERIFIED PART”. It's one of the reasons validate_tp3.sh exists (below).
  4. The sink nobody was loading The stock attn_sink loader used 64 // 3 = 21 and windows [0:21] [21:42] [42:63]: head 63 wasn't loaded on any rank. The three loaders (model, MTP and DSpark) now use the padded count, bounded by the real one.
  5. Hashed markers Each edit writes a marker with the hash of its own body. An old patch in the container's writable layer becomes STALE and aborts, because docker compose restarts without recreating. The fix is to recreate (stop + ./start-tp3.sh), the topic of Recipe engineering.

There's also padding in the kernel: DeepSeek's SM120 backend only accepts local query-head counts in {16, 32, 64, 128}. At TP=2 there are 32 and everything fits; at TP=3, the 24 local heads become 32 inside the call, with a −∞ sink in the extras.

Two documents, two truths

The repository still carries the root file patches/dsv4_tp_pad.py (the R1 version, not even imported). It declares R1 “CORRECT (rel 2.3e-06)” and rejects as “arithmetically impossible” a different “R2” (8 → 24 groups of 3 heads), not the applied R2 (8 → 9 groups of 8 heads). The real contradiction is in R1: the applier and docs/TP3.md call it structurally wrong, because the kernel rejects \(r = 1536\). When the documentation contradicts itself, and even reuses the same name for different schemes, trust the code that runs and an end-to-end validation.

Prove the shard, not the HTTP

Since a wrong shard produces fluent text with no error, “it's up” proves nothing. scripts/validate_tp3.sh runs 7 checks in 5 groups that are the first to fail under degraded attention: model identity; three single-answer recalls (the capital of Japan, 17 × 23 = 391, the fox sentence ending in “dog”); multi-step reasoning (red=7 blue=3); a needle (CRIMSON-MERIDIAN-42) at about 1.5k tokens; and degeneration (unique-word ratio ≥ 0.5). Expected: 7/7 and the pad log line on every rank.

GLM-5.3-Flash has 34 KDA linear attention layers and 11 sparse MLA layers, both with 64 heads (Linear attention, Sparse attention). The README sums it up: TP=3 is not TP=2 with one more node, because almost nothing in the model divides by three. Why 66, and why never 96

66 is the smallest multiple of 3 above 64, with 22 heads per rank. The launcher injects the value without touching the disk, via --hf-overrides, which rewrites the config in memory at load time:

{"num_attention_heads":66,"num_key_value_heads":66,"linear_num_heads":66,
 "text_config":{"num_attention_heads":66,"num_key_value_heads":66,"linear_num_heads":66}}

The text_config block repeats the values because, in multimodal models, the text config lives nested inside the overall config; the launcher writes both. The number comes from TP3_HEAD_OVERRIDE=66, and leaving it empty keeps the checkpoint untouched, which only works for models that already divide by 3.

There's a tempting shortcut. The SM120 sparse MLA decode kernel (Kernels) is only instantiated for {8, 16, 32, 64, 128} local heads, and 22 isn't in the table. With 96 heads there would be 32 per rank, a number the kernel accepts. The READMEs veto it: with 96, one rank ends up with nothing but fake KDA heads and the logits collapse (the source records the symptom, not the mechanism). The solution separates the two concerns: the model gets the minimum padding (66) and the kernel gets a local, throwaway padding. The vendored backend flashinfer_mla_sparse_sm120.py pads Q from 22 to 32 heads with zeros and slices the output back. It's safe because the softmax is per head: the extras have zero Q, never mix with the real heads and vanish before the output leaves the attention layer.

The local pad also covers prefill. The upstream overlay only padded decode-sized calls (≤ 64 tokens), but the prefill dispatch rejects 22 just the same (Unsupported sparse-MLA prefill configuration: model=GLM_NSA num_heads=22 topk=2048 page_block_size=64) and took down the engine on the first real prompt. This recipe pads at every call size.

Another way out: split unevenly instead of padding

Padding is vLLM's answer because its layers, loaders and kernels assume every rank holds the same shapes. The TensorFold version of the GLM recipe, which ran on exactly two ranks in v1.3.2, gained a three-Spark mode in v1.4 (./start-tp3.sh, experimental, patches 0066–0068) that takes the other road: every split dimension is cut in whole units, as even as they go, with the remainder on the lowest ranks. The 64 heads become 22 / 21 / 21; each expert's 2,048 columns, in 128-wide Hadamard blocks, become 768 / 640 / 640 (6 / 5 / 5 blocks), with no expert parallel; the vocabulary goes in units of 64 rows, and the DFlash2 drafter's KV groups follow the same rule. Nothing is padded, because no exchanged tensor is per head or per column: the ranks gather full-width fp32 partials and top-k candidates, so they may hold different shapes. The only pad left is a row: at TP=3 a 2,048-row prompt chunk becomes 2,049 for the split prefill (0067), and the memory estimate counts it (0068). The price is imbalance: rank 0 holds 22/64 = 34.4% of the heads instead of a third, and each step waits for the slowest rank (our arithmetic; the recipe doesn't measure that cost in isolation). With sparkDash on three Sparks against two: prose decode 60.4 → 77.6 tok/s for one request and 108.8 → 146.2 for four at once, prefill about the same (1–4% faster). The rule transfers: an uneven split is legal wherever the communication moves whole tensors (an all-gather of full-width partials, as here, or an all-to-all with per-rank counts); wherever a contract assumes equal shards (a reduce-scatter, a fused kernel's fixed tile, vLLM's weight loaders), you pad.

Sharded vocabulary and overwritten files

In a vocab-parallel embedding, each rank owns a contiguous range of ids. On the input side, it looks up only the ids in its range, zeroes the others, and an all-reduce sums the contributions; on the output side (lm_head), it computes the logits for its range and they are gathered. With 154,944 rows, each rank stores 51,648. Ids 154,880 to 154,943 never come out of the tokenizer and their logits are discarded, because the module keeps the original size. That's why pad-tp3-config.py warns you not to change vocab_size in the config.

Since the image's files are read-only, GLM's TP3 bind-mounts full replacement versions on top of them: parameter.py (with _pad_then_narrow: pad before narrowing when the end of the shard goes past the stored size), vocab_parallel_embedding.py and model_loader/weight_utils.py (loader v2). The launcher uses validate_loadclone_artifacts to check that the vendored weight_utils keeps loadclone: whole-file overlays are powerful and fragile.

A_log, the shared expert and a picky Marlin

  • A_log is the parameter of KDA's decay gate, one per head (gate = lower_bound · sigmoid(exp(A_log) · (g + dt_bias))). The patch intercepts the 1-D tensor of 64 and pads it to 66 with zeros (F.pad(…, (0, 2))). The fake heads have null weights, so their gate doesn't affect the output.
  • The shared expert is native BF16 and goes from 2048 to 2112, staying sharded. Replicating it with disable_tp “triples the shared MLP”, the patch warns: each rank would compute the whole MLP and the following all-reduce would sum three contributions.
  • Dense FP8 (GLM53_DENSE_FP8=dense,kda) runs via Marlin, which requires a row pitch that's a multiple of 8 and offsets aligned to 16 B. In TP3 the fused KDA projection in_proj_qkvbfg_a has 8,726 rows per rank (12,576 in TP2). The comment in exl3.py spells out the sum: q, k and v with 22 × 128 each, b with 22, and f_a and g_a replicated with 128 each (22 × 128 × 3 + 22 + 128 + 128 = 8,726; in TP2, with 32 local heads, 12,576). f_a and g_a are 128-column slices of it, with pitch 8,726 (remainder 6 when divided by 8), so f_b_proj and g_b_proj stay in BF16 and the boot logs [glm53-dense-fp8] TP=3 keeps KDA f_b_proj/g_b_proj in BF16.
  • FlashKDA: with HAREM_KDA_FLASHKDA=1 (patch_flashkda_tp3.py), KDA's chunked prefill swaps the Triton kernels for a fused CUDA kernel (14-argument ABI, FP32 recurrent state, preallocated workspace that is safe for CUDA graphs). The expected advantage of a fused kernel is doing in one pass what the Triton path does in several, with fewer launches and fewer round trips to memory; the source doesn't measure this gain in isolation (interpretation). The _tp3 suffix comes from its origin: the patch was ported from tracks/tp3/patches/flashkda in the NNNtrance repository, based on the original vLLM integration (PR 55737). It only changes chunked prefill, not the recurrent decode, and when set to 1 it fails on unsupported inputs instead of silently falling back to Triton. The header warns that native GPU validation of this branch is still pending. .env.tp3 leaves it at 0; the throughput profile (section 9) turns it on, and its gain is part of a bundle that can't be isolated.

Whole experts and a lonely drafter

In GLM, each routed expert has width 2048 and is quantized as a 4-bit EXL3 trellis, packed in 16 × 16 tiles (trellis [K/16, N/16, 16·bits]). 2048/3 = 682.67 isn't even an integer, and the READMEs forbid padding moe_intermediate_size (the reading that this would require repacking already-encoded tiles is interpretation). The way out is to change the cutting axis: instead of a third of every expert, each rank gets a third of the experts, whole. That's expert parallel (EP), turned on by --enable-expert-parallel (ENABLE_EXPERT_PARALLEL=1): 288 / 3 = 96 experts per rank.

Here EP changes which weights each GPU stores, not the communication: with DP=1, every rank sees all the tokens of the step, computes its experts' contribution and the usual all-reduce sums the rest (the Qwen recipe audit showed the all-to-all isn't even invoked; the same vLLM mechanism applies to GLM with DP=1, by inference, not measurement). What changes is load balance: the step waits for the rank holding the most popular experts (a general consequence, not a measurement from the recipe).

Loader traps under EP

  • patch_exl3_ep_shard.py: under EP the EXL3 loader kept sharding each expert by the world size of 3 and died with dim 0 size 2048 is not divisible by tp=3. Since the rank already owns whole experts, the patch uses moe_parallel_config.tp_size, which is 1 under pure EP. It runs after patch_dense_fp8.py, which reinstalls exl3.py.
  • patch_exl3_expert_map.py: the expert map (288 entries that translate a global id into a local index, or −1 if the expert lives on another rank) is a read-only property under EP, and the EXL3 code tried to assign it (AttributeError). The patch stores the map in _exl3_pinned_expert_map.
  • Dense EXL3 rejected: start-tp3.sh rejects GLM53_DENSE_EXL3=1 before starting up. The reason is geometry, not policy: the dense pack shards the shared expert's 2048 as an EXL3 trellis, which doesn't divide by 3, and the 64 → 66 head pad in patch_tp3_glm.py only covers BF16 tensors. TP3 serves the shared expert in BF16, padded to 2112; dense EXL3 stays with TP=2.

A MoE kernel for the new geometry

TP2's cooperative decode MoE (Kernels) was specialized for a local width of 1024. In TP3/EP the geometry is different: local width 2048 with no intra-expert cut, up to 96 local experts, top-8, SwiGLU with limit 10. The extensions/cooperative_moe/tp3 variant (ABI 2) compiles 32- and 64-row units (256/512 slots), uses a 9-bit sort key and verifies the binary via manifest hashes. The choice between geometry 1, geometry 2 and the stock kernel is made per row count, according to dispatch_policy.json:

For 25 row counts (1–10, 12, 15, 16, 18, 20, 21, 24, 25, 30, 32, 35, 40, 48, 56 and 64), the candidate/stock ratio was measured across 3 EP partitions (ranks 0, 1 and 2) × 2 routing patterns, and the geometry with the lowest worst-case ratio was kept, as long as it was ≤ 0.97 (a gain of ≥ 3%). The 25 aren't random: they are exactly the 25 --cudagraph-capture-sizes in examples/tp3-throughput.env, the batch sizes the engine captures as CUDA graphs. Unmeasured counts stay on stock for lack of measurement, not because they lost (“Unmeasured shapes use stock”). Two measured ones lost: 21 rows (best worst case 0.9734, above the cutoff) and 64 rows, where the kernel works but came in at 1.0568; the README asks you not to describe every 64-row call as faster. The historical gains of the combined profile (section 9) came from a bundle of changes and can't be attributed to this kernel alone.

The drafter's divisibility tax

The DFlash2 drafter (DFlash2 and DSpark) uses GQA with 32 query heads and 8 KV heads, and neither divides by 3. The recipe runs the drafter only on rank 0 (DFLASH_DRAFT_TP=1), which the README calls the “divisibility tax”: jspark3's 3× stack is still ahead on structured prompts (~95 tok/s vs. 87.8), and the README attributes the gap to that tax. Even with draft TP 1, qwen3_dflash.py checks total_num_heads % tp_size against the world TP (3). So pad-tp3-config.py generates a TP3-only copy of the drafter's config, with model.safetensors as a hardlink to the resolved blob, and pads the GQA while keeping the 4:1 ratio.

pad-tp3-config.py can also pad the target's config.json, a legacy of the 3× NVFP4 recipe it was written for. There, the native MTP's SpeculativeConfig reads the file from disk, where --hf-overrides doesn't reach (it only rewrites the config in memory), and without the padded file MTP would die with “64 is not divisible by 3”. In this recipe (SPEC_METHOD=dflash, the default) the launcher applies the script only to the drafter's TP3 copy: the target snapshot is shared with TP=2 and must not be edited (“The shared snapshot must NOT be edited — start.sh at TP=2 loads the same files”). The moral for your own recipe: when a shape adjustment has to go to disk, make one copy per topology and never touch a checkpoint that another path also loads. The vision tower has its own workaround, MM_ENCODER_TP_MODE=data: the encoder is replicated and the batch of images is split across the ranks. Qwen already does this at TP=2, because 4304/2 = 2152 isn't a multiple of 16, as the NVFP4 kernels require.

The three-node mesh: directed ring, subnets and control plane

With two nodes there's one cable and one subnet. With three Sparks and no switch, each ConnectX-7 uses both QSFP ports (f0 and f1), one per neighbor, and the three cables form a triangle. Each cable is a point-to-point link with its own /24, so no data subnet reaches every rank. The consequences split into two planes (details in Communication: NCCL and RDMA):

Data plane (RoCE)

Collective tensors, via RDMA verbs on the HCAs listed in NCCL_IB_HCA. Fast, but each port only sees one neighbor.

Control plane (TCP)

Rendezvous, TCPStore, Gloo, NCCL bootstrap and the TP group's TCP. Little traffic, but it needs one interface that reaches everyone.

The GLM launcher's comment states the rule: Gloo accepts exactly one interface name (a comma-separated list makes it fall back to the default route), and in a triangle mesh no CX7 interface will do. Both recipes move the control plane to the RJ45 management LAN, enP7s7 on 192.168.1.0/24, the only layer 3 network shared by all three nodes. Never use lo: Gloo would bind to 127.0.0.1 and the mesh dies.

On the data plane, NCCL opens queue pairs between ports, and moving a QP to RTR (ready to receive) requires the peer's GID and route. Trying a peer on a subnet with no path makes the transition time out (ibv_modify_qp … 110 Connection timed out). Four settings prevent this:

  • Both HCAs: NCCL_IB_HCA=rocep1s0f0,rocep1s0f1 (DeepSeek: TP3_NCCL_IB_HCA). With just one, spark1 tried 10.0.22.1 → 10.0.23.3 and timed out on QP RTR.
  • Don't merge ports: NCCL_IB_MERGE_NICS=0. By default NCCL may fuse the two ports into one logical NIC, but here each port goes to a different neighbor and the merge builds QPs with the wrong peer.
  • Pair by subnet: NCCL_IB_SUBNET_AWARE_ROUTING=1 matches GIDs by subnet, not by NIC index. DeepSeek also pins NCCL_IB_SUBNET_PREFIX_LEN=24 because, according to the launcher's comment, with the default /16 the 10.0.22.x and 10.0.23.x networks would look like one. GLM, on the other hand, works without pinning the prefix (it only turns routing on and runs the image's own NCCL 2.30.7, with USE_HOST_NCCL=0; the DS image ships the same version in PyNCCL), and the source doesn't explain why. PREFIX_LEN=24 is the explicit, safe choice: it costs nothing and removes the doubt. The public variables page for NCCL 2.32.3 doesn't list either of these two; the recipes use them because they worked on the kit, which makes the behavior version-dependent (both images ship NCCL 2.30.7; GLM's NCCL_HOST_DIR only applies with USE_HOST_NCCL=1, which is discouraged because the preload duplicates DeepEP's NCCL).
  • Cross NICs: NCCL_CROSS_NIC=1 (GLM TP3/TP4). In the ring every cable connects dev0 on one end to dev1 on the other, and NCCL has to accept different indices at the two ends of a channel.

That leaves the cabling. The GLM recipe requires a directed ring: each node's Port0 (the bay next to the RJ45) goes to the next node's Port1 (head f0 → worker2 f1, worker2 f0 → worker1 f1, worker1 f0 → head f1). Only then does the rule “I send out one port and receive on the other” hold the same way on every node. A mirrored ring leaves one pair that never connects, and reordering NCCL_IB_HCA doesn't help, because NCCL enumerates the devices in system order. Finish with one /24 per cable, MTU 9000 and ipv4.never-default yes, so that no data link becomes the default route.

Per-node configuration

Each rank faces its neighbors through different ports, so worker 1's values can't be copied to worker 2. The launchers gain a third block of variables:

DeepSeek (WORKER2_*)

  • WORKER2_HOST=10.0.0.3, required: needs passwordless SSH from the head and the image already pulled (~19 GB); the launcher checks this, but won't do it for you.
  • WORKER2_VLLM_HOST_IP, WORKER2_DIR, WORKER2_HF_CACHE, defaulting to WORKER2_HOST / WORKER_*.
  • WORKER2_NCCL_IB_HCA=rocep1s0f1 and WORKER2_{NCCL_SOCKET,TP_SOCKET,GLOO_SOCKET}_IFNAME=enp1s0f1np1: spark3's port facing the head, used to resolve GIDs before the TP3 adjustment moves the sockets to the LAN.
  • WORKER2_NFS_SERVER_IP=10.0.23.1: the head's IP on the spark1↔spark3 link, not 10.0.22.1. The start script adds that /24 to the export (nfs_grant_subnet) and spark3 reads the weights from the head without a local copy.

GLM (WORKER2_*, WORKER3_*)

  • WORKER2_IP=10.0.0.3; USER, HOME, SSH, CX7_IF/IB and GID inherit from rank 1 when empty.
  • SOCKET_IFNAME (and HEAD_/WORKER_/WORKER2_SOCKET_IFNAME) = enP7s7; *_HOST_IP is the address each rank advertises (its VLLM_HOST_IP, also used by the workers' TCP message queues). It defaults to the rank's LAN address, but it doesn't have to live on SOCKET_IFNAME: it only has to be reachable from the other ranks (see the callout at the end of this section).
  • NFS: a single 164 GiB copy on the head instead of ~330 GiB of duplicates. Each rank mounts through the CX7 IP of its own cable (NFS_SERVER_IP_1=10.0.22.1, _2=10.0.23.1, nconnect=8); the launcher refuses 10.0.0.x, because mounting over the management network “turns a 164 GiB load into an overnight job”.
  • TP4: WORKER3_IP=10.0.0.4 and so on. The kit this profile was measured on used a CRS812 switch (two Sparks at 200G, two at 100G), not a direct ring. That was the kit's choice, not a limit: four Sparks also run as a switchless ring, as long as the ranks are placed with care and NCCL knows about the cables (section 8).

GLM inherits from the DS4.1 recipe (three Sparks on the same cabling) NCCL_P2P_DISABLE=1 and NCCL_SHM_DISABLE=1, to stay on IB, plus 1 MiB buffers, 256 KiB LL128, NCCL_PROTO=^LL128 and 8 fixed channels, which keep pinned memory in check (otherwise 512 connections × 9 MiB).

The control plane can carry hundreds of megabytes

“Little traffic” holds for the rendezvous and the bootstrap, not for everything that travels over TCP. vLLM's multiprocess executor broadcasts each new request's data to the remote workers over TCP message queues, image tensors (mm_features) included; a processor-cache hit restores those tensors in the head's EngineCore, but doesn't suppress the broadcast. On a three-Spark kit whose ranks advertised their management Wi-Fi addresses, a conversation with 12 images (1536 × 1024 PNGs, ~24.6k prompt tokens) sent ~230 MB to each worker on every request, and the two tool-result follow-ups took 56.7 and 61.7 s to the first token. The recipe's docs/tp3-cx7-rpc.md (2026-09-21) changes only the advertised addresses (HEAD_HOST_IP, WORKER_HOST_IP, WORKER2_HOST_IP) to CX7 ones, plus three /32 host routes so that each node reaches a peer's advertised address through that peer's own cable; Gloo and the NCCL bootstrap stay on the management network, and the collectives stay on RoCE. The same bytes, now on the cables: 2.3 and 7.9 s for the follow-ups and 37.9 s instead of 95.1 s for the first prompt (one observation per turn, not a benchmark). The lesson carries to any cluster: list every TCP channel between ranks and what it carries; a “control” socket that carries payloads belongs on the fast network.

Four nodes and the collective hang

Arithmetically, GLM's TP=4 is easier than TP=3: 16 local heads (in the kernel table), a vocabulary of 38,720 per rank, 512-column experts and drafter 8/2 (DFLASH_DRAFT_TP=4). On the 4-Spark kit (2026-09-02), the first TP=4 bring-up, launched per rank with the same docker run format as start.sh, decoded 1.45× faster than 2 nodes and made mixed phases 20–35% shorter. Only later did an autoresearch loop pin the values in .env.tp4.example (experimental, not tested by the maintainer; section 9). That bring-up also brought a failure mode that only shows up with more participants: the distributed stall.

A collective is a scheduled meeting: the all-reduce only finishes when every rank arrives. If one rank gets stuck in a kernel, the others spin while they wait (GPUs at 96% and 21 W). On the 4-Spark kit, a 150-min soak hung the engine 3 out of 3 times (between 31 and 78 min in). The fatal step was a 96k chunked prefill sharing steps with 6–7 speculative decode streams: all four ranks stopped in the same forward and vLLM died 5 min later with “RPC call to sample_tokens timed out”. The investigation is a good example of the scientific method applied to systems:

  1. “It's the drafter”: refuted The soak without the drafter survived 150 min (472 requests, 0 errors) and hung an hour later, on an isolated 282k cold prefill.
  2. “It's packet loss”: withdrawn The hangs kept happening with the RoCE loss counters flat.
  3. Localization A progress failure in H16 sparse attention (16 local heads) was pinned to the same step. The internal race was not identified.
  4. Bounded mitigation Cap the final sparse attention call at 64 query rows, where progress was stopping.

That's what VLLM_SM120_SPARSE_MLA_SLICE_TOKENS=64 does, via overlay/patch_sparse_mla_slice.py (from punkjazz-labs, MIT). Only the flashinfer_trtllm_batch_decode_with_kv_cache_mla call now runs in slices of ≤ 64 rows, with the same H16 kernels; the outer 2048-token batch, the EXL3 kernels, DFlash and NCCL don't change, and only 0 and 64 are accepted. The patch is source-exact (pre-image and result pinned by SHA-256, so it won't silently apply to another vLLM revision) and fail-closed outside the qualified geometry: BF16 Q [T, 16, 576] (16 heads × 512 of latent + 64 of padded RoPE), pages [64, 656] (64 tokens × 656 B of the fp8_ds_mla record), top-k 2048 and workspace ≥ 33,685,504 B.

Why 64, and why only a “mitigation”

The TP3 overlay defines _SM120_DECODE_MAX_TOKENS = 64 as the ceiling of that backend's decode path; slicing at 64 keeps each piece in a well-exercised call regime (a parallel that is interpretation). Qualification was done on another 4×GB10 kit: a 153-min mixed soak and a comparison against an FP32 reference at 1/64/65/129/193/2048 tokens, with a maximum absolute difference of 0.00185609, using DFLASH_TOKENS=3, mixed prefill off and --enforce-eager. The patch states that it neither identifies nor fixes the race; other combinations need their own soak.

The TP=4 profile is also a lesson in method. The values in .env.tp4.example came out of an “autoresearch” loop of 24 experiments: one knob per relaunch, ten minutes of benchmarking with the production mix after each one, hard reliability gates and a 50-min soak on the winner (188 requests, 0 errors). Changing one knob at a time is what lets you attribute each effect to a cause; the gates make sure no speed gain is bought with instability. The knobs and the reason for each one are in the table in section 9.

DiagnosticsPYTHONFAULTHANDLER=1

An extra variable that start-tp3.sh passes to the containers. Python installs the faulthandler at startup and, if the process dies from a fatal signal (SIGSEGV, SIGABRT, SIGFPE, SIGBUS, SIGILL), dumps the traceback of every thread, showing on which Python line a native crash happened. The recipe doesn't document why; reading it as an aid for diagnosing hangs on a new topology is interpretation.

Four Sparks, two ports: switch or ring

Three Sparks fit in a triangle because each one has two QSFP ports, one per peer. Four don't: a complete graph of four nodes needs three links per node, and each Spark still has two ports. There are two ways out. A RoCE switch gives every Spark one hop to every other (the GLM TP4 kit of sections 6 and 7 used a CRS812). A switchless ring, spark1 – spark2 – spark3 – spark4 – spark1, uses one cable between neighbours, one /24 per cable, and no diagonals: the two Sparks across from each other (1 and 3, 2 and 4) share no cable, so anything between them takes a relayed hop through a neighbour. Two of Mia's October recipes run that ring with the same checkpoint, DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash-1017: one on SGLang (./start-tp4-switchless.sh, a flat TP4) and one on TensorFold's native engine (./start-switchless.sh, the fabric its own README numbers were measured on). Between them they teach three lessons.

1. The communication library has to know the cables

NCCL builds its connections from rank numbers, not from cables. Its ring algorithm only talks to the previous and the next rank, which on a ring cabled in rank order is always a cable. Its tree and PAT algorithms, however, also connect ranks two positions apart. On the SGLang recipe, stock NCCL logged Connected all rings and then failed in ncclTransportTreeConnect, and NCCL_ALGO=Ring alone wasn't enough in the tested build. The fix is a patched library: sparkring's NCCL 2.30.7 (an external project, FujitsuPolycom/sparkring) recognizes NCCL_SWITCHLESS_RING_ONLY=1 and skips the tree and PAT transport setup, while keeping subnet-aware port selection between neighbours. The launcher bind-mounts it read-only over the image's pip NCCL (a second library through LD_PRELOAD made DeepEP abort with Duplicate NCCL runtime found), checks for the marker on every node, and adds NCCL_ALGO=Ring, NCCL_IB_SUBNET_PREFIX_LEN=24 and 4 channels (the switched TP4 default stays at 8). The TensorFold recipe takes a different route with the image's NCCL: NCCL_ALGO=Ring (its all-gather only sends to the next rank; PAT, which NCCL may pick with one GPU per node, also sends two ranks away), an NCCL graph file per rank that names, channel by channel, the device facing each neighbour, and the partner exchanges on two-rank communicators of their own, one per cable. A send/receive on the four-rank communicator had taken a port without that peer and failed with unhandled system error on every prompt past a few hundred tokens.

2. Place the ranks so that partners share a cable

TensorFold's DeepSeek engine (BertholomusAI's 2D split) isn't a flat TP4. Each Spark is a node \(g = 2p + r\) of a 2 × 2 grid: two TP2 pairs \(p\), each with two ranks \(r\). The heavy exchanges are always between partners: the row partner \(g \oplus 1\) (the other rank of the same pair) and the column partner \(g \oplus 2\) (the same rank of the other pair). On a switch, any placement works. On a ring, it matters. With the nodes placed 0-1-2-3 around it, the column partners (0 and 2, 1 and 3) sit across from each other, and every column exchange takes a relayed hop. With 0-1-3-2 (ORDER=auto, which becomes TF_DS_2D_ORDER=0,1,3,2), every partner pair has a cable of its own, and only \(g \oplus 3\), which the split never exchanges with directly, sits across the ring. That's the whole trick: lay the algorithm's communication graph onto the physical one.

On top of that, patch 0008 (by Hector, TF_DS_PAIR_SUM, off by default and on in the cluster the README measured) changes the prompt gathers. Instead of a four-rank all-gather of four quarters, each node sums its column's two TP2 partials with its row partner over their cable and then swaps the sum with its column partner over the other cable: in the patch's words, “two link transfers of one part each instead of the four-rank all-gather's three”. It only runs on chunks of 64 rows or more, and only if every rank has both partners as ring neighbours, which the ranks agree on at startup; otherwise every rank keeps the four-quarter gather. Try the placements and the collectives in the figure.

3. What the ring costs, measured

On TensorFold, the ring algorithm's all-gathers and the partner exchanges all ride on cables. What crosses the ring is the decode-size exchanges, which go over the engine's own RDMA (RDMA=1, writing to every peer): the recipe's patch 0001 made those RDMA rings ring-aware, so the Spark across is reached through its two neighbours, each relaying half (the engine's own code assumed a switch). That is “the ring's one relayed hop between the two nodes with no direct cable” which, in the README's words, a switch removes (the rendezvous and NFS of the worker across from the head also go through a neighbour). Its numbers, on the ring: 2026-10-25, four Sparks, 1,048,576-token window, 16 streams, DSpark drafts, CUDA graphs, side stream and L2 prefetch on, median of three runs.

How this transfers: ranks onto a topology

The ring of four Sparks is a miniature of a problem every cluster has: the algorithm's communication graph has to land on the physical one. On an H200 system the eight GPUs of a node share an NVLink/NVSwitch domain (hundreds of GB/s per GPU), while nodes talk over InfiniBand or Spectrum-X Ethernet, usually in a rail-optimized layout: GPU \(k\) of every node goes through NIC \(k\) to the same leaf switch, so traffic between same-numbered GPUs takes one hop and anything else climbs to the spine and back. That's why frameworks keep TP inside the NVLink domain (TP = 8 per node) and spread PP, DP or EP across nodes; why NCCL detects the topology (NCCL_TOPO_DUMP_FILE shows what it saw, and a graph file like the TensorFold recipe's imposes one); why it picks per message between ring (bandwidth-optimal, latency growing with \(p\)), tree (logarithmic latency) and, on NVSwitch, NVLS; and why PXN moves a message over NVLink to the GPU whose NIC sits on the destination's rail. Each leaf → spine → leaf crossing is an extra hop that charges α and shares uplinks, like the ring's relayed hop. The question this section asked, “which pairs talk the most, and do they share a link?”, is the one to ask when you order TP, EP and PP ranks on a cluster.

In the recipes: is growing worth it?

One more node splits the weights across more ranks, but the speedup depends on how much of the step is weight reading and how much is fixed cost, which grows with the number of participants. A toy model helps:

\[ t_{\text{step}}(p) \;\approx\; \frac{B_{\text{sharded}}/p \;+\; B_{\text{replicated}}}{\beta} \;+\; c(p) \]
  • \(B_{\text{sharded}}\): weight bytes split across the ranks
  • \(B_{\text{replicated}}\): bytes every rank reads in full (latent KV, indexer, norms)
  • \(\beta\): effective memory bandwidth per node
  • \(c(p)\): fixed cost (collectives, synchronization, padding), increasing in \(p\)

DeepSeek provides real anchors. At TP=2 each rank reads ≈ 16.5–17 GB per step (c=1), and decode is ~80% weight streaming. At TP=3 the bytes per rank drop to ≈ 11.5 GB (≈ 48 ms of streaming), but the measured step sits at ≈ 61 ms, with at least 13 ms of fixed cost (3-rank ring, heads padded from 64 to 72). TP=3 barely speeds up a single conversation: 1-stream decode gains +9% (41.9 vs. 38.6 tok/s), far less than the ~1.4× the drop from 16.5 to 11.5 GB per step would suggest, because the fixed cost rises by ≥ 13 ms. And TTFT gets worse. What it buys is capacity. With fewer weights per rank (79.17 → 60.69 GiB), the KV pool per rank grows from ≈ 17 to ≈ 35 GiB, and since in MLA every rank stores the full KV, the token count grows in the same proportion.

The TP=3 delivers 16 slots, ≈ 200 tok/s aggregate at 16 streams and 5.02 M tokens of KV (4.79× at 1M). The README talks about “~3× KV”; the measured pools give ≈ 2.2× in tokens (2.19–2.33 M → 5.02 M). Per-stream decode improves 4–13% and TTFT worsens 4–13% up to 64K and 22–23% at 128K–256K: replicated attention and indexer don't shrink with the third rank, and the 3-node all-reduce costs more. For a single long-context user, the README recommends the 2-node lane. The TP=3 won on decode (structured 87.8 vs. 73.4; code 54.9 vs. 45.0; prose 39.6 vs. 32.9) and freed memory (weights per node from 79.65 to ~55.75 GiB). TP3's 3,230,656 KV tokens come from a 40 GiB cap, versus an 11 GiB cap on TP2: the difference in tokens mostly measures the choice of cap, not the gain from the third node. In the first TP=4 bring-up decode reached 1.45×, but cold prefill rose only 29% (901 → 1162 tok/s at 282k), limited by the network, and the configuration didn't hold up in the soaks. After this snapshot the TP4 launcher started passing the E3 prefill kernel to its ranks (until then they silently ran E2), and the README reports cold prefill of 1,772 / 2,422 / 2,733 tok/s at 8k / 32k / 100k, up from 1,316 / 1,761 / 1,940 (a contributor's measurement on four Sparks, 2026-09-27, 1M context; other lengths than the 282k above, so not comparable with it).

Why this matters

Adding a node is a trade-off, not a speed button: more slots and more context in memory in exchange for more fixed cost per step, more padding code and a more fragile mesh. Many concurrent users and huge cached contexts favor TP=3; one long, solitary conversation favors TP=2.

The launchers

Both recipes isolate TP=3 in its own launcher, so that a forgotten TP_SIZE=3 in .env doesn't silently change the 2-node path, which always forces TP=2/NNODES=2. The start-tp3.sh exports DSPARK_TP3=1, TP_SIZE=3 and NNODES=3 and passes through --max-num-seqs, --host and --port. The start-tp3.sh reads .env and then .env.tp3, which wins, but first neutralizes TP2 knobs:

  • ABLIT=0, so that a 2-node kit with ABLIT=1 doesn't transplant o_proj into TP3 by accident.
  • EXL3_OVERLAY_HOST cleared: the .env overlay is TP2's cooperative adapter, with the wrong ABI.
  • GLM53_KDA_BF16_LARGE_M=0 and unset GLM53_EXL3_MOE_FAST GLM53_KDA_FP8_FAT: according to the launcher's comment, the thin-decode FAST path and the W8A8 “FAT” path from #182 stay TP2-only (the inventory doesn't describe what FAT does).
  • Removes --kv-cache-memory-bytes from .env: “14 GiB is a TP2 number” and can't hold a 1 M request.

Policy knobs (GLM53_*) must reach every rank identically: a rank that captures different CUDA graphs hangs NCCL after the PIECEWISE capture (the graph mode that splits the forward at the attention layers; see CUDA graphs and warmup), a self-inflicted distributed stall. The general rule: in TP, every rank must execute the same sequence of collectives, with the same shapes and in the same order.

The TP3's first boot takes ≈ 8 min to become healthy (engine init: 193 s): spark3 starts with an empty JIT cache and the TP=3 shard shapes are new to all three nodes. Don't restart a boot that is only compiling. Changes to the patch or to the slots require recreating the containers, not restarting them.

Your own recipe beyond 2 nodes

  1. Factor the config For each dimension (heads, groups, widths, experts, vocabulary, drafter, vision): sharded, replicated or split by experts? Does \(p\) divide it?
  2. Pick the fix Padding via the lcm; EP when the quantized weights can't be cut; data mode or replication for small pieces; a TP of its own (or 1) for the drafter.
  3. Respect the contracts Kernel shapes (r = 4096, head tables, Marlin pitch) and neutral elements (sink −∞).
  4. Pad-aware loaders Intersection copied, the rest zeroed, replicated parameters and special loaders checked.
  5. Cable and configure per node Directed ring or switch (with four nodes: a switch, or a ring with partners on cables), one /24 per cable, control on a shared LAN, per-node pins.
  6. Prove and measure A functional battery (like validate_tp3.sh), long soaks and an A/B against the 2-node lane on your workload.

Worked example: auditing a new config for TP=3 (illustrative)

Three things stand out. First, the 5 fake query heads (40–44) belong to the fake KV head (8), and all of them land on rank 2: they form a whole GQA group of zeros, like DeepSeek's 9th group, and no real head shares K/V with a fake one. Second, the factor 3 shows up by accident in the expert width (1,536 = 2⁹·3), just as it did in GLM's expert count (288 = 2⁵·3²), only here it's on the side that allows sharding, which makes EP unnecessary; the expert count, which doesn't divide, stops mattering. Third, the kernel contracts still need checking: 15 local heads must exist in the attention backend's table, and if they don't, the way out is to pad inside the call (15 → 16, like GLM's 22 → 32). If the model had an attention sink, the fake heads would get −∞. With that, the recipe would be: --hf-overrides with 45/9 heads, pad-aware loaders for q/k/v/o, padding_size = 192 for the vocabulary and the functional battery before any benchmark.
