Remember the base model from chapter 50 that answered a question with another question? Supervised fine-tuning (SFT) is the first remedy: you show the model thousands of demonstrations of how an assistant answers and train it to imitate them, with the same cross-entropy as pretraining. It is the least glamorous method in post-training, and it is everywhere: DeepSeek's six R1-Distill models are just SFT on ~800,000 examples, LIMA aligned a LLaMA-65B with 1,000, and fixing MiMo-V2.6's repeated tool calls through distillation cost ~$90K versus ~$2.31 million for the RL alternative. Here you will see the loss and the mask, the template as data, packing, the sources of demonstrations, on-policy distillation and MOPD, the curves, forgetting, and real configurations.

From completer to assistant

A base model came out of pretraining: it predicted the next token over trillions of tokens (27 trillion for MiMo-V2-Flash, more than 32 trillion for DeepSeek-V4). It knows a lot, but it only completes text: it has no conversation format, doesn't know when to stop, doesn't follow instructions reliably, and has no safety policy. Post-training almost always starts with SFT: (prompt, response) pairs written by humans or by stronger models, rendered in the model's chat template, with the loss computed only on the tokens the assistant “speaks”.

The mechanics are those of chapter 1: cross-entropy, gradient, optimizer. What changes is the data, and the effect is out of all proportion to its size. InstructGPT (2022) used ~13K SFT prompts (plus ~33K for the reward model and ~31K for PPO), and its 1.3B model was preferred over the 175B GPT-3. LIMA took the idea to the extreme and proposed the superficial alignment hypothesis: almost all knowledge comes from pretraining, and post-training mostly teaches format and style. With 1,000 curated examples, LIMA's answers tied or beat GPT-4's in 43% of cases.

Notice three things. The mean loss plummets, and that is the number the optimizer sees. The sample changes regime early: the favorite switches from “What” to “The” long before the loss reaches its floor. And, in this toy figure, the model learns to answer before it learns to stop: for a good stretch, it answers correctly and then tacks on a user turn. Stopping is only learned if the end-of-turn token is in the loss (section 3).

Why every method starts here

Almost all the rest of Module 2 presupposes an SFT checkpoint (the map is in chapter 50; here we revisit only what depends on it). In RLHF, it is the starting point of the policy and the KL anchor (\(\pi_{\text{ref}}\)); in DPO, it is usually the reference. In DeepSeek-R1, a cold-start SFT with a few thousand chains of thought comes before RL; R1-Zero, which skipped that stage, had poor readability and mixed languages. In MOPD, teachers and student are born from the same SFT. And there is an information argument (LoRA Without Regret): a policy-gradient episode yields \(O(1)\) bits, a scalar advantage; an SFT example yields \(O(\text{number of tokens})\) bits.

Analogy: onboarding week

The base model is an intern who has read the entire library. They don't need physics lessons; they need to know how the company answers an email, when to sign off, and when to stop writing. SFT is onboarding week with a folder of exemplary emails. It doesn't teach physics: it teaches the form in which to deliver what the intern already knows, and that is why it works with little material, as long as the material is good.

What post-training leaves for the server

Base, instruct, and reasoning models have the same tensors, and everything Module 1 taught about serving applies to all of them. But post-training leaves marks the server has to respect: the chat template, the reasoning parser, the recommended sampling in generation_config (for Qwen3.8 in thinking mode, temperature 1.0, top_p 0.95, top_k 20), and the end tokens, eos_token_id [248046, 248044] (<|im_end|> and <|endoftext|>). Each of these details was born in a piece of training data.

The loss: cross-entropy only where the assistant speaks

Write each example \(i\) as a prompt \(x_i\) (system, user, tools, previous turns) followed by the response \(y_i = (y_{i,1}, \dots, y_{i,T_i})\). SFT is maximum likelihood of the response given the prompt, with the average taken over the tokens that count:

\[ \mathcal L_{\text{SFT}}(\theta) \;=\; -\frac{1}{\sum_{i,t} m_{i,t}}\;\sum_{i}\sum_{t} m_{i,t}\,\log \pi_\theta\big(y_{i,t}\mid x_i,\, y_{i,<t}\big) \]
  • \(\theta\): the trainable parameters (all of them, or only those of a LoRA adapter)
  • \(i\): example in the accumulated batch (all micro-batches and devices)
  • \(t\): position within the rendered sequence
  • \(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid\ldots)\): the next-token distribution, the softmax of the logits
  • \(y_{i,<t}\): the previous tokens from the dataset, not from the model (teacher forcing)
  • \(m_{i,t}\in\{0,1\}\): the loss mask; 1 on the assistant's tokens
  • \(\sum m\): the total number of loss tokens in the batch

In the implementation, the collator copies the input_ids into labels at the same positions and writes -100 where \(m=0\). The one-position shift (the output at position \(t\) predicts token \(t+1\)) happens inside the model's loss function, which compares logits[:, :-1] with labels[:, 1:]. In HF/TRL, don't shift by hand: the model would end up predicting two tokens ahead. The −100 is the ignore_index of PyTorch's cross-entropy: the position enters neither the sum nor the denominator. The gradient is the one from chapter 1, \(\partial\ell/\partial z_v = p_v - \mathbb 1[v=y]\): the label goes up with strength \(1-p_y\), each competitor goes down by the probability it stole, and rows with −100 produce no gradient. The figure shows the logit matrix of a tiny conversation.

Take a few steps with the “assistant only” mask and see that only the last three rows change. With “all tokens”, the model spends capacity predicting the user and the template markers: waste and, as the next section shows, sometimes poison.

Averaged over what?

The denominator \(\sum m\) must count all the loss tokens in the accumulated batch, across all devices. If each micro-batch takes its own mean and you then average the means, each token weighs differently: with a 20-token response in one micro-batch and a 2,000-token one in the other, each token of the short one weighs 100 times more than one of the long one, and the result changes with the micro-batch size and the number of GPUs. That was the 2024 gradient accumulation bug. Tülu 3 replaced the mean with the sum (which requires recalibrating the LR); TRL normalizes by the total number of tokens in the accumulated batch with average_tokens_across_devices=True.

Imitating is forward KL, and it is off-policy

Minimizing the SFT loss is minimizing the forward KL from the data to the model, as chapter 50 anticipated: \(H(p_{\text{data}}, \pi_\theta) = H(p_{\text{data}}) + \mathrm{KL}(p_{\text{data}}\,\|\,\pi_\theta)\), and the first term doesn't depend on \(\theta\). This KL punishes the model whenever it gives low probability to something the data does, and it spreads mass over every mode, even the ones the model can't reproduce well (section 7 compares it with the reverse KL).

SFT is also off-policy: in training, the prefix at every position comes from the dataset; in generation, it comes from the model itself, and a small deviation takes it into states it has never seen, where errors compound. This is exposure bias, the motivation for the whole on-policy family ahead.

Deep dive: SFT seen as a policy gradient

The gradient of \(-\log p_\theta(y)\) is \(-\nabla p_\theta(y)/p_\theta(y)\). Read as a policy gradient, it is REINFORCE with an “implicit reward” proportional to \(1/p\): tokens that are rare under the model get huge pushes. Dynamic Fine-Tuning (arXiv 2508.05629) argues that this hurts generalization and multiplies each token's loss by its own probability under a stop-gradient, \(-\mathrm{sg}(p_\theta(y_t))\log p_\theta(y_t)\). In TRL it is loss_type="dft"; there are also NEFTune (noise in the embeddings, neftune_noise_alpha) and label_smoothing_factor. Treat them as hypotheses to measure, not as defaults.

The chat template is part of the data

Chapter 5 showed the chat template from the server side: the function that turns the list of messages into text with special tokens. In training, the template stops being an API detail and becomes part of the data. The most common input format is JSONL, one object per line. The frameworks accept conversations (messages), prompt–completion pairs, conversations with tools, and conversations with reasoning:

{"messages": [{"role": "system", "content": "You are an inference engineer. Answer in English, with numbers and units."}, {"role": "user", "content": "How much KV does one token take in GLM's fp8_ds_mla?"}, {"role": "assistant", "content": "656 bytes per token per DSA layer: 512 B of NoPE latent in FP8, 16 B of scales and 128 B of RoPE (64 BF16 values)."}]}
{"prompt": [{"role": "user", "content": "What is the default --max-lora-rank in vLLM?"}], "completion": [{"role": "assistant", "content": "16. It accepts 1, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256, 320 or 512."}]}
{"messages": [{"role": "user", "content": "How many KV tokens fit in 14 GiB at 656 B/token/layer with 11 layers?"}, {"role": "assistant", "tool_calls": [{"id": "c1", "type": "function", "function": {"name": "calc", "arguments": "{\"expr\": \"14*2**30/(656*11)\"}"}}]}, {"role": "tool", "tool_call_id": "c1", "content": "2083202.0"}, {"role": "assistant", "content": "≈ 2.08 million tokens."}], "tools": [{"type": "function", "function": {"name": "calc", "description": "Evaluates an arithmetic expression", "parameters": {"type": "object", "properties": {"expr": {"type": "string"}}, "required": ["expr"]}}}]}
{"messages": [{"role": "user", "content": "How many GB does a 27B take in NVFP4?"}, {"role": "assistant", "reasoning_content": "27.8e9 parameters × 4.5 effective bits…", "content": "About 16 GB for the NVFP4 weights alone…"}], "chat_template_kwargs": {"enable_thinking": true}}

The first format is the conversational one. The second computes the loss only on the completion, by default (completion_only_loss in TRL, --mask-prompt in mlx-lm). The third is the tool format: tool_calls in the assistant turn, with function.arguments as a JSON string; role: "tool" with the tool_call_id and the result; and the function schema in the tools column. Figure 52.3, just below, renders this conversation (with an extra system message and reasoning). The fourth is the reasoning format in the Qwen3.5/3.6/3.8 family: the thinking goes in the reasoning_content field, not as <think> inside content. The Qwen3.8-27B template renders <think>\n{reasoning_content}\n</think>\n\n{content}; putting the reasoning inline in content produces two thinking blocks. Each row's chat_template_kwargs is applied by the SFTTrainer when it renders that example.

The figure's four modes:

  • Full text. The model also learns to write the user's turns and the tool result. A model that learned to predict <tool_response> learned to invent tool results.
  • Assistant only. The correct one: reasoning, tool calls, answer, and end of turn go in. In TRL it is assistant_only_loss=True, which requires {% generation %}…{% endgeneration %} blocks in the template (TRL patches the template of known families, such as Qwen3).
  • Assistant without EOS. The most expensive mistake: without <|im_end|> in the loss, the model doesn't learn to stop and “speaks for the user” or generates up to max_tokens. TRL asks for the eos_token to be aligned with the template; for the base Qwen/Qwen2.5-1.5B, eos_token="<|im_end|>".
  • mlx --mask-prompt. Only the last message counts as the completion; earlier assistant turns are never trained. If every turn matters, split the conversation into several examples.

Five rules that save weeks of debugging

  1. Match the serving template byte for byte. A different space or line break before <|im_start|>assistant changes the tokenization, and at inference the model sees a prefix it never saw in training. Render with tokenizer.apply_chat_template using the same tokenizer_config.json that vLLM will use.
  2. The same reasoning history on both sides. Several Qwen3-family templates strip the <think> from previous turns; training with the full history and serving without it (or the reverse) creates a mismatch, and Qwen3.8's preserve_thinking kwarg (default true on the card) exists for exactly this. For agents, TRL requires “prefix-preserving” templates (appending a message doesn't change how the previous ones render) and automatically swaps in a fixed training template for Qwen3 and DeepSeek-V3.
  3. Tool-call format that matches the parser. The tool_calls in the data must come out in the format that --tool-call-parser will read (mimo, qwen3_coder, DeepSeek's DSML). An SFT that teaches a different format breaks the parser in vLLM (chapter 34).
  4. Checkpoint without Jinja, pre-tokenized dataset. When the template is a program, generate the tokens with that same program and hand over input_ids + labels (or assistant_masks), which SFTTrainer accepts.
  5. New tokens call for trainable embeddings. On a base without a template, TRL can clone another model's (chat_template_path="HuggingFaceTB/SmolLM3-3B"). If that introduces tokens the base never trained, a LoRA on the linear layers doesn't learn their vectors: you need to train embed_tokens and lm_head (modules_to_save in PEFT).
Parameter--tokenizer-mode deepseek_v4

DeepSeek-V4 ships no Jinja template: the chat is rendered by a Python encoder in the checkpoint (encoding_dsv4.py), and the Module 1 recipe enables it with --tokenizer-mode deepseek_v4. For an SFT served by this recipe, tokenize the examples with the same encoder (rule 4) and write the tool calls in DSML, the format that --tool-call-parser deepseek_v4 reads: a single malformed structural token keeps the parser from recognizing the call.

Packing: filling the suitcase without mixing the clothes

SFT examples vary wildly in size: a 40-token answer next to a 6,000-token reasoning trace. In a batch with padding, every example is filled up to the longest one, and the filler tokens cost memory and compute even though they are outside the loss. Packing fits several examples into a single row of max_length. In TRL (packing=True) there are three strategies:

"bfd" (default)
best-fit decreasing: sorts by size and puts each example in the fullest row where it still fits. Examples longer than the row have their excess discarded.
"bfd_split"
the same, but cuts long examples into pieces instead of discarding the excess.
"wrapped"
concatenates everything and cuts it into blocks, splitting examples in the middle. The Qwen3-Coder-Next report says this hurts.

Packing creates a new problem: in the packed row, example B comes right after A, and the usual causal mask would let B attend to A. With "bfd", TRL automatically turns on padding_free: the batch becomes a single sequence, the position_ids restart at 0 for each example, and variable-length FlashAttention keeps one example from attending to another. The position_ids restart because RoPE rotates each vector by its absolute index (chapter 8), and because the restarts are where the varlen kernel gets the example boundaries (cu_seqlens). TRL also puts −100 on the first token of each packed example (labels[position_ids == 0] = -100), so that the last token of A isn't trained to predict the first token of B.

Without FlashAttention 2/3, there is a risk of batch contamination. For serving, vLLM runs FA2 on SM12x (chapter 28), but for training, the sources don't confirm the FlashAttention 2/3 that padding-free needs on the GB10's sm_121. Without it, use SDPA with packing but without padding-free, as long as your transformers version builds the block-diagonal mask from the position_ids (check with a two-example batch whether B's logits change when A changes), or just use padding.

Try “wrapped” with the mask off: besides split examples, half the row attends to text from another example. DeepSeek-V3's SFT (1.5 million instances, 2 epochs, cosine LR from 5e-6 down to 1e-6) did it right: packing with a mask between samples.

Truncation and effective batch

TRL's default SFT max_length is 1024, and only truncation_mode="keep_start" is supported: the cut happens at the end, where the answer is, and on a reasoning trace it chops off the conclusion and the </think>. Filter or split long examples. As for the batch, the number that matters is tokens per optimizer step:

\[ B_{\text{tokens}} \;=\; b_{\text{micro}} \times n_{\text{accum}} \times N_{\text{dp}} \times \bar L \]
  • \(b_{\text{micro}}\): examples (or packed rows) per micro-batch, per device
  • \(n_{\text{accum}}\): gradient accumulation steps before each update
  • \(N_{\text{dp}}\): data-parallel replicas
  • \(\bar L\): useful tokens per row (with packing, close to max_length)

The reference effective batch in the reports is 128 examples (Tülu 3, MiMo-V2-Flash). A warning from LoRA Without Regret: LoRA tolerates very large batches worse than full fine-tuning does, and increasing the rank doesn't fix it, because the penalty is a property of the \(BA\) parametrization.

How Qwen3 filtered its cold start

Qwen3's first stage is an SFT on long chains of thought in math, code, logic, and STEM, all verifiable. The filtering was twofold. On the questions side, out went the unverifiable ones and those that Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct solves without reasoning (too easy to teach thinking). On the answers side, generated by QwQ-32B, out went the wrong, repetitive, and inconsistent ones. The post-training of the recipe's Qwen3.8-Flash-Next is not published.

Synthetic data: distilling from someone who knows more

The most common way to get demonstrations at scale is to ask a stronger model for them and run SFT on its outputs: the sequence-level off-policy distillation of Alpaca, R1-Distill, OpenThoughts, and MiMo (with internal domain-specialized checkpoints). One variant minimizes \(\mathrm{KL}(\pi_T\,\|\,\pi_\theta)\) over the teacher's full distribution at each position, which requires the same vocabulary (TRL's DistillationTrainer warns about this). The Module 1 models each have their own vocabulary (GLM 154,880, Qwen 248,320, DeepSeek 129,280): the student must belong to the same tokenizer family.

“Stronger” isn't always better. In the MOPD paper, swapping the math teacher born from the same SFT for Qwen3-235B-A22B, a much stronger model, made the student worse: the initial per-token KL rose from ~0.04 to ~0.19 and the entropy fell from 0.30 to 0.21. Our interpretation: a nearby teacher gives a learnable signal; a distant one teaches what the student cannot represent. And generating data with closed APIs may violate the provider's terms: check the licenses.

Generate, filter, train: RFT and STaR

The third source is the model itself. The rejection-sampling fine-tuning loop (or expert iteration) has four steps: sample \(k\) answers per prompt, keep the correct ones (verifier, tests, judge), run SFT on them, and repeat with the new model. With accuracy \(p\) on a prompt, the chance of keeping at least one example from it is

\[ \Pr[\text{prompt yields data}] \;=\; 1 - (1-p)^k, \qquad p = 0.2,\; k = 16 \;\Rightarrow\; 1 - 0.8^{16} \approx 0.97 \]
  • \(p\): the current model's accuracy on that prompt
  • \(k\): samples per prompt
  • prompts with \(p = 0\) never yield an example, no matter how large \(k\) is

STaR (Zelikman et al., 2022) goes after exactly the prompts the model always gets wrong. The RFT of Yuan et al. (2023) took LLaMA-7B from 35.9% (SFT) to 49.3% on GSM8K by combining samples from several models; the gain grows with the number of distinct reasoning paths and is larger for weaker models. ReST-EM views the loop as EM and scales better than human data in math and code. DeepSeek-R1's stage 3 is RFT at scale: ~600K correct reasoning samples without mixed languages, plus ~200K general ones, in a 2-epoch SFT on top of DeepSeek-V3-Base. Notice the trash bin in the figure. Our interpretation: RFT is a policy gradient with a binary reward whose negatives were thrown away, semi-on-policy (the data comes from an earlier version of the model) and as simple as an SFT: the natural bridge to the RL of the following chapters.

Reasoning and tools: SFT on long traces

A reasoning model writes a long chain of thought between <think> and </think> before answering. SFT comes in at two points: in the cold start, with a few thousand readable chains before RL (DeepSeek-R1, Qwen3), and in mode fusion (Qwen3's third stage is an SFT that merges thinking and non-thinking with the /think and /no_think flags). And the traces are long: the MOPD examples use 32,768 tokens in math and instructions and 65,536 in software engineering, with up to 50 turns.

The memory cost grows with the length \(s\). With FlashAttention, the activations saved for the backward pass grow linearly in \(s\), the attention FLOPs grow with \(s^2\), and the logits grow with \(s\cdot V\). The approximation from Korthikanti et al. for a Transformer block in 16 bits gives:

\[ A_{\text{no ckpt}} \approx 34\,s\,b\,h\,L, \qquad A_{\text{ckpt}} \approx 2\,s\,b\,h\,L + 34\,s\,b\,h, \qquad \text{logits}_{\text{FP32}} = 4\,s\,b\,V \ \ \text{(bytes)} \]
  • \(s\): tokens per sequence; \(b\): sequences per micro-batch
  • \(h\): hidden dimension; \(L\): layers
  • 34: bytes per hidden element per layer saved for the backward pass (without the attention matrix, which FlashAttention doesn't materialize)
  • ckpt: full gradient checkpointing; saves only each layer's input and recomputes the rest
  • \(V\): vocabulary; the logits \([s \times V]\) are usually the largest tensor in training

For Llama-3.1-8B (\(h = 4096\), \(L = 32\), \(V = 128{,}256\)), with \(b = 1\): at 2,048 tokens that is 9.13 GB of activations without checkpointing, 0.82 GB with it, and 1.05 GB of logits; at 32,768 tokens, 146.0 GB, 13.2 GB, and 16.8 GB. That is why TRL uses loss_type="chunked_nll" by default: it discards the positions with label −100 before the lm_head and computes the cross-entropy in chunks. On Qwen3-1.7B, this gave ~30% less peak VRAM (up to ~50% with FSDP2 on 4 GPUs). chunked_nll is not compatible with Liger, PEFT, or VLMs. The full accounting of training memory is in the previous chapter.

Tools follow the same logic: assistant turns with tool_calls enter the loss; the results (role: tool) stay out. At scale, this changes the math. MiMo-V2.6-Distill-Qwen-9B is an SFT of Qwen3.5-9B on 77.4 billion tokens generated by MiMo, of which only 27.2 billion (~35%) enter the loss; the rest is context, tools, and environment outputs. SWE-bench Pro went from 32.0 to 44.6 and AutomationBench from 5.0% to 30.3%. This is agentic distillation at continued-pretraining scale, and the budget is planned in loss tokens, not total tokens.

Reasoning effort is a post-training artifact

In DeepSeek-V4, each effort level (Non-think, High, Max) is an RL specialist trained with its own length penalty and context window, evaluated with windows of 8K, 128K, and 384K. Serving with a smaller budget than the one used in training cuts off reasoning that post-training taught to be long; in your own SFT, if the traces are 20K tokens long, the serving max_tokens has to accommodate them.

On-policy distillation: the teacher corrects what the student does

SFT has a dense signal (every token has a target), but it is off-policy. RL is on-policy, but the signal is sparse: one scalar per episode. On-policy distillation combines the two: the student generates its own responses, and a teacher weighs in, at each position, on the token the student wrote.

MethodWhere the states come fromSignal
SFT / off-policy distillationfrom the dataset or the teacherdense (every token)
RLfrom the model itselfsparse (one scalar per episode)
On-policy distillationfrom the model itselfdense (every token)

GKD (Agarwal et al., ICLR 2024) trains the student on its own sequences, with the teacher providing the full distribution at each position, and lets you choose the divergence. In TRL's DistillationTrainer, it is the generalized JSD:

\[ \mathcal L_\beta = \beta\,\mathrm{KL}\big(p_T\,\|\,p_M\big) + (1-\beta)\,\mathrm{KL}\big(p_S\,\|\,p_M\big), \qquad p_M = (1-\beta)\,p_S + \beta\,p_T \]
  • \(p_T\), \(p_S\): teacher and student distributions at the position
  • \(p_M\): the mixture of the two
  • \(\beta = 0\): TRL uses the forward KL \(\mathrm{KL}(p_T\|p_S)\); \(\beta = 1\): the reverse KL \(\mathrm{KL}(p_S\|p_T)\) (the extremes are handled separately, because the formula degenerates there)
  • DistillationConfig defaults: beta 1.0, temperature 1.0, max_completion_length 512, learning_rate 1e-6

The difference between the two ends, already seen in chapter 50, is the most useful conceptual distinction in the module. SFT's forward KL is mass-covering: it punishes giving no mass to anything the teacher does. The reverse KL is mode-seeking: it punishes putting mass where the teacher puts none, and the student concentrates on the modes it can represent. When the student is smaller, covering everything means putting mass in the valley between the modes, generating what the teacher would never say. The figure shows a two-mode teacher and a single-mode student.

Take \(\beta\) to 0: the student gets wide, centered on the valley, and a good share of its samples land where the teacher puts almost no mass. Take it to 1: the student picks the larger mode and gets narrow; it loses the other mode, but what it generates is plausible. That is why on-policy distillation works well when the student is smaller than the teacher.

The per-token advantage

With one sample per position, the gradient of the reverse KL becomes a policy gradient with a per-token advantage:

\[ \hat A_t \;=\; \mathrm{sg}\big[\log \pi_T(y_t) - \log \pi_\theta(y_t)\big], \qquad y \sim \pi_\theta \]
  • \(y_t\): the token the student sampled at position \(t\)
  • \(\pi_T(y_t)\), \(\pi_\theta(y_t)\): teacher and student probabilities for that token, given the same prefix
  • \(\mathrm{sg}\): stop-gradient; the advantage is a weight, not a gradient path
  • \(\hat A_t > 0\): the teacher likes the token more than the student does, so it gets reinforced

Exact examples: teacher 0.6 and student 0.3 give \(\hat A = \ln 2 \approx +0.693\), and the token goes up. Teacher 0.05 and student 0.4 give \(\ln 0.125 \approx -2.079\), and the token goes down hard. Unlike outcome-based RL, every token gets a signal. In the Qwen3 report (Qwen3-8B, table 21), on-policy distillation reached 74.4 on AIME'24 versus 67.6 for RL, and 65.5 versus 55.5 on AIME'25, with 1,800 versus 17,920 GPU-hours, about 1/10. In the Thinking Machines study, starting from a 400K-example SFT (60% on AIME'24), RL reaches ~68% and on-policy distillation ~70%, at a cost 9 to 30 times lower than extending the off-policy SFT.

In TRL

The DistillationTrainer (stable since v1.10) generates with vLLM (colocate or server), computes the JSD in chunks without materializing the teacher's \([s \times V]\), and supports tools; on the CLI, trl distillation --model_name_or_path … --teacher_model_name_or_path …. The AsyncDistillationTrainer (experimental, v1.11) keeps the teacher on a vLLM server over HTTP, ships only a top-k of log-probs (teacher_top_k), and bounds staleness with max_staleness; it only supports FSDP2, and the teachers need the student's tokenizer. It is Module 1's inference engine inside the training loop.

MOPD: many teachers, one student

MOPD (Multi-Teacher On-Policy Distillation) appeared in the MiMo-V2-Flash report and was formalized in a paper of its own (Ma et al., 2026, Peking University and Xiaomi). In three stages, it merges the skills of several specialists into a single model, without multi-domain RL, which is expensive and riddled with cross-domain interference:

  1. General SFT on a broad corpus: the common initialization.
  2. Domain-specialized RL starting from that SFT: one teacher per domain (math, instructions, SWE, tools…), trained in parallel, each with its own algorithm and hyperparameters. In the paper: on-policy GRPO with dynamic sampling, LR 3e-6, a batch of 144 prompts × 8 rollouts in math and instructions.
  3. MOPD: the student, reinitialized from the stage 1 SFT, generates rollouts. Each prompt is routed to the teacher for its domain, which returns per-token log-probs, and the student minimizes the per-token reverse KL. The fusion happens in policy space, not by averaging weights.
\[ \hat A^{\text{MOPD}}_t = \mathrm{sg}\big[\log \pi_{\phi_d}(y_t) - \log \pi_\theta(y_t)\big] \ \text{clipped to } [-A_{\max}, A_{\max}], \qquad \mathcal L_{PG} = -\,\mathbb E\Big[\tfrac{1}{|y|}\textstyle\sum_t \hat A^{\text{clip}}_t\,\log\pi_\theta(y_t)\Big] \]
  • \(\pi_{\phi_d}\): the teacher for the prompt's domain \(d\)
  • \(A_{\max}\): two-sided clipping limit
  • \(|y|\): response length; the average is per token
  • “the only change is the advantage computation”: the form fits into existing PPO/GRPO frameworks

There is a top-k form, with lower variance and a small payload for the teacher service: \(\sum_{v\in \mathrm{TopK}_k(\pi_{\phi_d})}\big[\pi_\theta(v)\log\frac{\pi_\theta(v)}{\pi_{\phi_d}(v)} - \pi_\theta(v) + \pi_{\phi_d}(v)\big]\). The extra term \(\pi_{\phi_d} - \pi_\theta\) corrects the truncation bias and puts the minimum at \(\pi_\theta = \pi_{\phi_d}\) on the top-k. In MiMo-V2-Flash, the samples come from the inference engine \(\mu_\theta\), and each token gets the weight \(w_t = \mathrm{sg}[\pi_\theta(y_t)/\mu_\theta(y_t)]\), or is discarded if that ratio falls outside \([\varepsilon_{\text{low}}, \varepsilon_{\text{high}}]\): two engines with the same weights don't produce the same log-probs (chapter 25). By default, the advantage from an outcome reward model is added on top: \(\hat A_t = \mathrm{sg}[\log \pi_{\text{dom}}/\pi_\theta] + \alpha\,\hat A_{\text{ORM}}\).

On the infrastructure side, the teacher's prefill is treated as reward computation: an asynchronous service, separate from the trainer, triggered as each sequence finishes while others are still being sampled. The authors report no measurable overhead in wall-clock time. It is the physics of chapter 35: a forward pass over positions that are already written is much cheaper than generating them.

MOPD2 and the checkpoint you served

MiMo-V2.6-Flash-MOPD, the model from chapter 46, is “the MOPD upgrade of the MiMo-V2.6-Flash-RL checkpoint”: same architecture (309B total, 15B active), different weights. The stage is MOPD2, Multi-Prefix Multi-Teacher On-Policy Distillation, applied after mixed RL. The teachers are mixRL (verifiable tasks) and SFT (synthetic demonstrations from open-ended domains, where a reward is hard to design), and three streams go into the same update: full rollouts; Teacher-Prefix OPD, in which \(k\) turns of a teacher trajectory become \(k\) prefixes and the student writes one turn from each; and SFT-Prefix OPD, in which the history comes from a demonstration: the SFT data becomes context, not target. Our interpretation: it is a direct answer to exposure bias, because the student learns on-policy on the last turn, conditioned on realistic histories it might never reach on its own.

The case of the repeated tool calls

MiMo-V2.6-RL kept repeating identical tool calls, “looking busy without making progress” (1.02% on OpenCode, for Flash). A blind spot in the reward: the flooding penalty only fired above 32 calls in a turn, and RL amplified the behavior below the threshold. Lowering it to 8 and redoing ~20 MixRL steps would have cost ~$2.31 million, and in replay the repetition only fell from 13.45% to 3.83%. The fix was a single-turn RL teacher, with a reward of 1 only when there was no repetition and no tool misuse: 12 steps (~7,000 examples) brought the repetition in replay to zero. The final MOPD was rolled back five steps and continued with teacher-prefix OPD that included this teacher. Cost: ~$90K, 4% of the alternative, with the rest of the benchmarks stable. Fixing a defect turned into “train a small teacher and distill it”.

Post-trainingmulti-teacher OPD, full vocabulary

In DeepSeek-V4, V3.2's mixed RL was entirely replaced by on-policy distillation: \(\mathcal L_{\text{OPD}} = \sum_i w_i\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta\,\|\,\pi_{E_i})\), reverse KL over student samples, with more than 10 teachers and full-vocabulary logits (129,280). The per-token advantage version was rejected because of high variance and instability. To make it fit, the teachers' weights live in central storage and are loaded on demand (ZeRO-style sharding); only the teacher's last-layer hidden states are kept, and the logits are reconstructed on the fly by the prediction head. Samples are sorted by teacher (one teacher head on the GPU at a time), and a TileLang kernel computes the exact KL.

Post-trainingstrong-to-weak distillation

The small Qwen3 models are distilled from the large model in two phases: off-policy (teacher outputs in both modes) and then on-policy, aligning the logits through KL. That is where the 74.4 versus 67.6 on AIME'24 with ~1/10 of the GPU-hours comes from. For Qwen3.8, third-party analyses attribute part of the gain to on-policy distillation from Qwen3.8-Max, without confirmation from a primary source.

To reproduce stage 3 at small scale, the AsyncDistillationTrainer accepts one URL per teacher and a teacher_id column per sample. The documentation itself warns that MOPD uses reverse KL (beta=1.0), not this trainer's default beta=0.0:

Three rules run through the table. LoRA wants a ~10× higher LR than full fine-tuning (≈15× in runs with fewer than ~100 steps), and the optimal LR barely changes with rank: less than 2× variation between \(r = 4\) and \(r = 512\), thanks to the \(\alpha/r\) prefactor (LoRA Without Regret). That is why TRL suggests ~1e-4 for PEFT versus its 2e-5 default. mlx-lm's scale is not PEFT's \(\alpha\): scale = 20 multiplies \(BA\) directly, the equivalent of \(\alpha/r = 20\) (\(\alpha = 160\) with \(r = 8\)), which explains the 1e-5 LR. Few epochs: 1 to 3 almost everywhere, because more epochs on a small dataset means memorization.

Why such low LRs? MiMo-V2-Flash's pretraining peaked at 3.2e-4; the full SFTs in the table sit 6× (MiMo, 5e-5) to 160× (Tülu 3 70B, 2e-6) below that. Our interpretation: the model starts near a good minimum and the dataset is small, so large steps mostly pull it away from the base, the KL axis of figure 52.9 and the root of the forgetting in section 10. Warmup protects Adam's first steps, while the second-moment estimates are still noisy; decay lets the model settle (details in chapter 51).

What to watch during training: SFTTrainer logs loss, entropy (mean entropy of the predicted distribution over the loss tokens), mean_token_accuracy (top-1 matching the label), grad_norm (before clipping), num_tokens, learning_rate and, for MoE, aux_loss. Our reading: entropy falling fast and per-token accuracy near 1 on training data, while validation loss rises, is memorization.

Four scenarios: with an LR 10× above the optimum, the loss jumps around and the KL to the base shoots up. With 5 or 6 epochs on 2K examples, training loss keeps falling while validation loss rises, and the best checkpoint is left behind. In LoRA with the full fine-tuning LR, training barely moves; with 10× more, it comes back, with less KL. With 30% general data (Thinking Machines used 70% domain and 30% chat), the general suite drops less.

Format overfitting

Validation loss, drawn from the same domain, misses one kind of overfitting: the model memorizes the training format. It answers everything as a list, starts every answer the same way, wraps in JSON what didn't ask for JSON, opens a <think> where it shouldn't. Chu et al. (2025) sum it up in their title: “SFT memorizes, RL generalizes”. The remedy is to measure out of domain (section 10) and keep the mix diverse.

MoE SFT and the optimizer: two cautions

In MiMo-V2-Flash's SFT, the critical stability metric was the number of parameters with zero gradient (num-zeros): if it rises, expert balancing gets worse; if it falls, the model overfits. It depends on the expert bias update rate (1e-4 in MiMo) and on AdamW's \(\varepsilon\) (1e-8); in TRL, the accessible equivalent is router_aux_loss_coef. Then the optimizer: in Moonlight, Muon in SFT only had an edge when pretraining also used Muon, as with Qwen3.8 and DeepSeek-V4; for a full FT of those models, it is the coherent choice (interpretation, not tested on these models; the mismatch is covered in chapter 51). With LoRA, stick with AdamW.

Forget less, measure properly, and bring it to the recipes

Tuning a model for a new task usually costs capabilities it already had (instruction following, safety, general reasoning, format): this is catastrophic forgetting. Three findings set the yardstick. RL's Razor (Shenfeld et al., 2025): how much is forgotten is determined by the KL between the tuned model and the base on the new task's distribution, and on-policy RL forgets less than SFT at the same performance because it tends toward minimum-KL solutions. Biderman et al. (2024): LoRA “learns less and forgets less” than full FT. And Thinking Machines: on-policy distillation recovers the lost behavior. Measure the KL in both places: on the new task's prompts, it predicts how much you will forget; on general prompts, it shows where the model has already drifted from the base.

With 100% domain documents, internal QA goes from 18% to 43%, but IF-eval plummets from 85% to 45%; with 70% domain and 30% chat, they land at 36% and 79%; and a round of on-policy distillation afterward brings them to 41% and 83%. The mitigations the sources support:

  1. Mix in general or replay data with the domain SFT, like the 30% chat above.
  2. Lower LR and fewer epochs; choose the checkpoint by general validation, not by training loss.
  3. LoRA instead of full FT when the task is narrow.
  4. On-policy distillation from the base (or from a teacher) after SFT, or a KL-to-base term in RL.
  5. Weight averaging between base and tuned model (WiSE-FT style) or merging adapters with a weight below 1 (add_weighted_adapter in PEFT).

From adapter to recipe

There are two ways to serve the result. The first is LoRA in vLLM: vllm serve <base> --enable-lora --lora-modules meu=./adapter --max-lora-rank 64 (the default --max-lora-rank is 16 and it sizes the memory; the request picks the adapter through the model field). The second is to merge into BF16, requantize (NVFP4, FP8, EXL3), and measure both the KLD against the merged BF16 model and the task metric, because a small LoRA delta can be comparable to per-weight quantization noise. Since it is not verified that vLLM's LoRA works on top of the recipes' custom quantizations (GLM's EXL3, Qwen3.8's NVFP4 MoE on SM121), merging and requantizing is the safe path.

That leaves the drafter: the MTP, DFlash2, or DSpark heads in the recipes were trained against the original target, and after an SFT the acceptance rate tends to drop. MiMo-V2.6 treated this as part of post-training: the RL's default draft became a block-6 DFlash, trained first on the SFT policy and then tuned on rollout logs, with a mean accepted length 31.3% higher than that of the MTP-3 inherited from SFT. After fine-tuning, measure acceptance again (chapters 35–37) and, if it drops, tune the draft too.
