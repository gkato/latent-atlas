Open the config.json of the three recipe models and you will find more geometry than “intelligence”: hidden_size: 4096 , q_lora_rank: 1536 , kv_lora_rank: 512 , hc_lowrank: 320 . Open the recipes' patches and the story repeats itself: a kernel (the program that runs on the GPU) that dies because N=48 < 128, a dispatch that switches paths when M > 512, a silent memory corruption caused by stride(0)=59136. All of it is linear algebra. In this chapter you will learn to see an LLM the way the machine sees it: a collection of tensors with exact shapes, multiplied by one another billions of times, and you will understand why every shape, every rank and every memory stride costs what it costs.

Vectors: arrows that carry meaning

A vector is an ordered list of \(d\) numbers, \(\mathbf x = (x_1, x_2, \dots, x_d)\). Inside an LLM, every token becomes one of these vectors (the embedding, in chapter 5), and that vector is rewritten layer after layer in the residual stream (chapter 7). The length of the list is the hidden_size: 4096 in GLM-5.3-Flash and in DeepSeek-V4-Flash , 2560 in Qwen3.8-Flash-Next . This number shows up in almost every weight shape of the three models, because it is the width of the “road” every token travels on.

There are two ways of looking at the same vector, and you will need both. To the machine, it is a list of numbers stored in memory, cell next to cell. To geometry, it is an arrow in a \(d\)-dimensional space: the direction says “where” the vector points (roughly, what it represents) and the length says “how much”. In two dimensions you can draw it. In 4096 you can't, but the math is exactly the same.

Dot product: the universal ruler of similarity

The most important operation in this entire chapter is the dot product. You multiply coordinate by coordinate and add everything up. The result has a beautiful geometric reading: it is the product of the lengths times the cosine of the angle between the arrows.

\[ \mathbf a \cdot \mathbf b \;=\; \sum_{i=1}^{d} a_i\, b_i \;=\; \lVert \mathbf a\rVert\,\lVert \mathbf b\rVert\,\cos\theta \]
  • \(a_i, b_i\): the \(i\)-th coordinate of each vector
  • \(d\): number of dimensions (4096 or 2560 in the recipes)
  • \(\lVert \cdot \rVert\): length (L2 norm) of the arrow
  • \(\theta\): angle between the two arrows

If the arrows point the same way, the product is large and positive. If they are perpendicular (orthogonal), it is zero. If they point in opposite directions, it is negative. Keep this in mind, because the dot product is everywhere inside a Transformer:

🎯

Logits

The score of each vocabulary token is \(\mathbf h \cdot \mathbf w_v\): the final state against row \(v\) of the lm_head (ch. 9).

🔎

Attention

How much one token attends to another is \(\mathbf q \cdot \mathbf k\), the query against the key (ch. 6).

🧭

MoE router

The token's affinity for each expert is the product of the token with a learned vector per expert (ch. 11).

🧱

Linear layer

Every number that comes out of a linear layer is the dot product of a row of weights with the input (next section).

Norms: the size of the arrow

The L2 norm is the length of the arrow (Pythagoras in \(d\) dimensions). Its problem is that it grows with \(d\): a vector of 4096 “ordinary” coordinates is much longer than one of 16. That is why networks use the RMS norm, the root of the mean of the squares, which measures the typical size of a coordinate and does not depend on \(d\). The cosine is the dot product without the influence of the lengths:

\[ \lVert \mathbf x\rVert_2=\sqrt{\textstyle\sum_i x_i^2} \qquad \operatorname{RMS}(\mathbf x)=\sqrt{\tfrac1d\textstyle\sum_i x_i^2}=\frac{\lVert \mathbf x\rVert_2}{\sqrt d} \qquad \cos(\mathbf a,\mathbf b)=\frac{\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b}{\lVert\mathbf a\rVert\,\lVert\mathbf b\rVert}\in[-1,1] \]
  • \(\lVert \mathbf x\rVert_2\): length of the arrow
  • \(\operatorname{RMS}\): typical size of a coordinate
  • \(\cos\): pure alignment, between −1 and 1

That is exactly what RMSNorm does (seen as a stabilizer in chapter 2): it divides the vector by its RMS, which places it (almost exactly, as you will see in a moment) on a sphere of radius \(\sqrt d\), and then multiplies each coordinate by a learned gain. To avoid dividing by zero, a tiny \(\varepsilon\) is added inside the square root: rms_norm_eps is 1e-5 in GLM , 1e-6 in Qwen and in DeepSeek-V4-Flash-0731, and 1e-20 in DeepSeek Vision-Exp .

Pitfall: the ε is not decoration

The GLM-5.3 checkpoint declares \(\varepsilon = 10^{-5}\) for the vision tower, and yet vLLM forces \(10^{-6}\). The comment in the code explains why: serving with the declared value makes the RMSNorm “drift”, and image descriptions come out repetitive and degraded. The output is \(\mathbf y = \mathbf x/\sqrt{\operatorname{RMS}^2+\varepsilon}\odot\mathbf g\), and without the gain its norm is \(\sqrt d\cdot\sqrt{\operatorname{RMS}^2/(\operatorname{RMS}^2+\varepsilon)}\): the \(\varepsilon\) does not change the vector's direction, but it decides how close to the sphere of radius \(\sqrt d\) the vector gets. Vectors with \(\operatorname{RMS}^2\) comparable to \(\varepsilon\) come out shrunk by \(\sqrt{\operatorname{RMS}^2/(\operatorname{RMS}^2+\varepsilon)}\). Swapping \(10^{-6}\) for \(10^{-5}\) changes that shrinkage, and the rest of the network was trained for the original value.

Drag the tip of \(\mathbf k\) (or use the controls) and watch the score \(\mathbf q\cdot\mathbf k\) and the “shadow” of \(\mathbf k\) on \(\mathbf q\) change. Next to it (or below it, on a phone), a question that only shows up in high dimension: what is the cosine between two directions drawn at random?

In 4096 dimensions, almost everything is perpendicular

Look at the histogram. In two dimensions the cosines spread across the whole interval, but as \(d\) grows the distribution squeezes around zero, with a standard deviation close to \(1/\sqrt d\). At \(d = 4096\), that is \(\approx 0.016\). This phenomenon is called concentration of measure, and it has three consequences that come back throughout the trail:

  1. There is room for many nearly independent directions. A 4096-dimensional space holds far more than 4096 “nearly orthogonal” directions. The hypothesis that the model stores concepts as directions (and sometimes more concepts than dimensions) comes from here. It is an interpretability hypothesis, not a theorem about your model.
  2. Removing one direction barely touches the others. That is what makes abliteration viable: project out a single direction and leave the rest intact.
  3. A random rotation spreads out any “special” coordinate. After a random rotation, one giant value in a single coordinate becomes many medium values across all of them. It is the incoherence trick behind the Hadamard rotations, which you will see in section 7.

Matrices: machines that deform space

In chapter 2 you saw a matrix warp the plane: the square grid stretches, rotates or tilts, and the columns tell you where the axes go. Here we will read the same matrix in two different ways, because each of them explains a part of the recipes. First, the definition. A matrix \(W\) with \(m\) rows and \(n\) columns is a function: it takes a vector of \(n\) numbers and returns one of \(m\), \(\mathbf y = W\mathbf x\). It is a linear transformation: \(W(\alpha\mathbf a + \beta\mathbf b) = \alpha W\mathbf a + \beta W\mathbf b\). Geometrically, the origin stays put, lines stay lines and an evenly spaced grid stays evenly spaced. The two readings are these:

\[ \underbrace{y_i = \sum_{j=1}^{n} W_{ij}\,x_j = \mathbf w_i \cdot \mathbf x}_{\text{row view}} \qquad\qquad \underbrace{\mathbf y = \sum_{j=1}^{n} x_j\, W_{:,j}}_{\text{column view}} \]
  • \(\mathbf w_i\): row \(i\) of \(W\), a direction “detector”
  • \(W_{:,j}\): column \(j\), where the \(j\)-th axis goes
  • \(x_j\): how much of each column goes into the mix

In the column view, the output is a mix of the columns, with the coordinates of \(\mathbf x\) as the mixing recipe. The columns are literally where the matrix sends the axes \(\mathbf e_1, \mathbf e_2, \dots\): this is the deformed grid from chapter 2. In the row view, the story moves somewhere else. Nothing deforms: each row \(\mathbf w_i\) is a ruler pointed along a direction of the input space, and the output \(y_i = \mathbf w_i\cdot\mathbf x\) is that ruler's reading, the length of the “shadow” of \(\mathbf x\) on \(\mathbf w_i\) times \(\lVert\mathbf w_i\rVert\). All the points with the same reading form a line perpendicular to the ruler, and the lines of whole-number readings (\(y_i = 0, \pm1, \pm2, \dots\)) draw a grid in the input space. This is the view you will use whenever a weight is called a “detector”, an “output channel” or a “direction”.

Notice two numbers in the panel. The determinant is the factor by which area changes (negative when the matrix “turns the plane inside out”, like a mirror). When it is zero, the plane is flattened onto a line and information is lost. The two readings describe this loss from two sides. By columns, the columns become parallel and every output lands on a single line, the image. By rows, the two rulers end up measuring the same direction, and the direction perpendicular to them becomes invisible: any \(\mathbf v\) on that line, the null space, gives \(W\mathbf v = \mathbf 0\). Several different \(\mathbf x\) (all the ones that differ by a null-space vector) land on the same \(W\mathbf x\), and no matrix can undo that. The dimension of the image is the rank of the matrix: the number of truly independent columns (or rows). Rank plus the dimension of the null space always equals the number of inputs. All of section 6 revolves around this idea.

Three “ranks” that have nothing to do with each other

(1) The rank of a matrix, as in q_lora_rank: how many independent directions it produces. (2) The number of axes of a tensor, which some libraries also call rank (“a rank-3 tensor”); here we will say ndim to avoid confusion. (3) The process rank in distributed inference: the ID number of each GPU (“rank 0 is the head”). When a log says rank 1, it is a machine; when the config says dspark_markov_rank: 256, it is a matrix rank.

Composition: stacking linear layers is not enough

Applying \(W_1\) and then \(W_2\) is the same as applying a single matrix: \(W_2(W_1\mathbf x) = (W_2W_1)\mathbf x\). Two linear layers in a row, with nothing in between, collapse into one. That is why neural networks interleave activation functions (chapter 2). But the same fact has a very useful side. It lets you factor a large matrix into two thin ones (section 6), and it lets you pre-multiply matrices so an intermediate is never materialized. That is the “weight absorption” that makes the KV cache (the memory where the model keeps the attention keys and values of every token already read, so it does not recompute them for each new token; chapter 10) of MLA, the compressed-latent attention of GLM and the DeepSeek lineage (chapter 12), so small.

Shape conventions: [out, in] and A[M,K]·W[K,N]

In math, \(\mathbf y = W\mathbf x\) with \(W\) of shape \([m \times n] = [\text{out} \times \text{in}]\). PyTorch stores the weight of an nn.Linear exactly like that, weight[out_features, in_features], but it processes tokens as rows: \(Y = X W^\top\). Kernel documents (the lab notes for EXL3, the 4-bit quantization format of GLM's experts, for example) write A[M,K] @ W[K,N]: \(M\) tokens, \(K\) inputs, \(N\) outputs. It is the same matrix stored transposed. The rule of thumb for reading the safetensors header (the JSON index at the start of each weights file, with the name, type and shape of every tensor) of a linear layer in BF16 or FP8: the first number is the output, the second is the input.

When the rule does not hold

The “[out, in]” rule is the one for an nn.Linear stored in BF16 or FP8. Three cases from the recipes themselves break it, and mixing them up leads to wrong TP and byte math.

  • Indexed tables. An embedding stores [indexed rows, width]. GLM's embed_tokens [154880, 4096] is not a layer that maps 4096 numbers to 154,880: it receives a token id (one of 154,880) and returns the corresponding row, 4096 numbers. The first dimension is the input index. The same goes for DeepSeek's markov_w1 [129280, 256] and for the codebooks [154880, 256] of the DFlash2 selector . Fun fact: the lm_head [154880, 4096] has the same shape as embed_tokens, but it is a real linear layer, from 4096 inputs to 154,880 outputs.
  • Quantized formats store packed shapes. Qwen's NVFP4 experts show up as U8 [640, 1280]: each byte carries 2 four-bit values, so the logical matrix is 640 × 2560. Anyone applying the rule would conclude “input 1280”.
  • EXL3 stores K first. In the GLM recipe's checkpoint, an expert's gate_proj (logically 2048 × 4096) is trellis I16 [256, 128, 64] = [4096/16, 2048/16, 64]: one tile of 16 × 16 weights per position, input before output (EXL3's A[M,K] @ W[K,N] convention), with 64 sixteen-bit integers per tile (256 weights × 4 bits). Next to it come suh [4096] (one value per input) and svh [2048] (one per output), the sign-and-scale vectors of the Hadamard rotations from section 7. The down_proj is [128, 256, 64].

Moral: before reading a shape, ask about the dtype and the format. U8, I16 or a strange third dimension almost always mean “packed”.

The same convention defines the vocabulary of quantization (Part IV). An output channel is a row of weight[out, in]: one neuron. A per-channel scale is one scale per row. That is why GLM's load-time FP8 quantization (in the overlay overlay/exl3.py, enabled by GLM53_DENSE_FP8) and Qwen's offline script make_fp8_dense_checkpoint.py compute amax(|W|, dim=1)/448 (448 is the largest FP8 E4M3 number; chapter 4): the maximum along the input, one value per row (output channel). A per-token scale is one scale per row of \(X\), the activation.

Multiplying matrices: row meets column

In practice, a model almost never multiplies a lone vector. It stacks the step's tokens as the rows of an activation matrix \(X\) of shape \([M, K]\) and does a single multiplication by \(W\) of shape \([K, N]\). Each row of \(Y\) is the output of one token.

\[ Y_{mn} \;=\; \sum_{k=1}^{K} X_{mk}\,W_{kn} \qquad\qquad [M\times K]\cdot[K\times N] \;\to\; [M\times N] \]
  • \(M\): rows = tokens processed in this step
  • \(K\): input dimension, the one that is contracted (summed over)
  • \(N\): output dimension (neurons, output channels)
  • \(Y_{mn}\): dot product of row \(m\) of \(X\) with column \(n\) of \(W\)

The only requirement is that the inner dimensions match: the \(K\) of \(X\) has to be the \(K\) of \(W\). It disappears from the result, because it gets summed over. The step-by-step below animates this dance cell by cell. Change \(M\) to see the single-token case.

Look at step 5: each weight \(W_{kn}\) is used \(M\) times, once per token. With \(M = 1\), each weight read from memory serves a single multiplication. This operation has its own name, GEMV (matrix × vector), while the general case is GEMM (matrix × matrix). The difference looks bureaucratic, but in section 8 it will decide whether your step is limited by memory or by compute.

Weights and activations

Weights are fixed, learned and huge: they live in memory and have to be read at every step. Activations change with every token and are born during the computation. The number of rows \(M\) depends on the phase the server is in, and the two phases of every text generation deserve names right now (chapter 10 covers them in detail):

prefill
processing the whole prompt at once. All the prompt tokens go through the model together, in chunks of thousands of tokens, so each GEMM has thousands of rows.
decode
generating the answer, one new token per sequence at each step. With several simultaneous conversations, \(M\) = sequences × (1 + drafts).
draft
a token proposed by a cheap auxiliary model and verified by the main model in the same step, as extra rows of the same GEMM. This is speculative decoding (chapter 35). Each recipe uses a different drafter: DFlash2 in GLM, MTP (the model's own multi-token heads) in Qwen and DSpark in DeepSeek.

With that, the recipes' values of \(M\) come straight out of the launch parameters:

GLM-5.3-Flash

  • Decode: up to 4 sequences × (1 + 7 DFlash2 drafts) = 32 rows
  • Prefill: chunks of up to --max-num-batched-tokens 7168

Qwen3.8-Flash-Next

  • Decode: 8 sequences × (1 + 3 MTP drafts) = 32 rows
  • Prefill: chunks of up to 8192

DeepSeek-V4-Flash

  • Decode: 6 sequences × (1 + 6 DSpark drafts) = 42 rows
  • Prefill: chunks of up to 8192

Between one regime and the other, \(M\) changes by a factor of about 200. The multiplications are the same, with the same weights, and yet the machine's behavior changes completely. Section 8 shows why, and chapter 32 explains how the scheduler assembles each step.

FLOPs: counting the work

Each cell of \(Y\) costs \(K\) multiplications and \(K\) additions (one multiply-accumulate per term). There are \(M \times N\) cells, so:

\[ \text{FLOPs}(Y = XW) \;=\; 2\,M\,N\,K \]
  • the factor 2: one multiplication + one addition per term
  • \(M, N, K\): the three dimensions of the GEMM
Sanity check (our own math)

The fused input projection of KDA in GLM (the linear attention of the KDA layers, chapter 14), per GPU at TP=2, has shape [12576 × 4096]. In a prefill chunk with \(M = 7168\): \(2 \times 7168 \times 12576 \times 4096 \approx 738\) GFLOP. The GB10, the DGX Spark chip the recipes run on, peaks at ~125 TFLOPS in dense BF16, so no kernel can do this in less than ~5.9 ms. The recipe measured 7.36 ms with cuBLAS (NVIDIA's GEMM library) in BF16, which works out to ~100 effective TFLOPS, about 80% of peak. A back-of-the-envelope calculation that matches the measurement is the best sign that you understood the shape.

A handy rule of thumb: in a forward pass, each active parameter takes part in one multiplication and one addition per token, so FLOPs per token ≈ 2 × active parameters (not counting the attention scores, which grow with the context). With 18 B active, GLM spends ~36 GFLOP per token; DeepSeek, with 13 B, ~26 GFLOP; Qwen, with ~6 B, ~12 GFLOP.

Block multiplication: a preview of tensor parallel

A matrix can be cut into blocks, and multiplication respects the cuts:

\[ X\,[\,W_a \;\; W_b\,] = [\,XW_a \;\; XW_b\,] \qquad\qquad [\,X_1 \;\; X_2\,]\begin{bmatrix} W_1 \\ W_2 \end{bmatrix} = X_1W_1 + X_2W_2 \]
  • left: cut the output columns; each piece of \(Y\) comes out whole
  • right: cut the contracted dimension \(K\); each piece gives a partial sum

Put each block on a GPU and you have tensor parallel. Cutting along the output (column-parallel) needs no conversation between the machines. Cutting along the input (row-parallel) leaves each GPU with a partial sum, which has to be added to the others' by an all-reduce (the collective operation in which the GPUs exchange their pieces and all of them end up with the sum). That is all the algebra of parallelism, and it explains why divisibility matters so much in the recipes: GLM's 64 heads split into 32 + 32 at TP=2, but they do not divide by 3, and the TP=3 recipe has to inflate them to 66 (chapter 40).

Tensors: shapes, heads, broadcasting and einsum

A tensor is the natural generalization: a grid of numbers with any number of axes. The number of axes is the ndim, and the tuple with the size of each axis is the shape. The three checkpoints have examples of every size:

Activations are tensors too. A batch of \(B\) sequences of \(T\) tokens has shape [B, T, d], and a linear layer only sees the last axis: […, K] → […, N], with all the leading axes treated as “batch”. vLLM goes further and flattens all the tokens of all the step's requests into a single tensor [num_tokens, hidden], with no padding between requests (chapter 30). That is why the \(M\) of the GEMMs is, to a first approximation, the number of tokens scheduled in the step. The only rounding comes from CUDA graphs (kernel sequences recorded for fixed shapes, chapter 29): a step with fewer tokens than a captured size is padded with dummy rows up to that size, and a step larger than the largest captured size runs without a graph. In DeepSeek , the 42 decode tokens (6 × 7) only run in a graph because the recipe rounds the capture ceiling up to 48; with the “raw” ceiling of 42 truncated to 40, the list stopped at 35 and the 42-token step ran eager, 12% slower in aggregate.

Heads: reshape and transpose

Multi-head attention (chapter 6) is, from the point of view of shapes, a choreography in three moves. The projection produces [T, H·dₕ], a wide matrix. A reshape reinterprets that width as [T, H, dₕ], without moving a single byte. A transpose swaps the axes to [H, T, dₕ], and now each head is an independent slice that computes its own [T, T] scores. At the end, the reverse path puts the heads back together and o_proj returns to [T, d].

Notice that steps 2 and 3 do no arithmetic at all: they only change the way the same memory is read. This distinction between changing the shape and moving data is the subject of the next section. The same reasoning explains fused projections: concatenating several matrices along the output gives a single GEMM, and the result is split afterwards. vLLM merges the six input projections of GLM's KDA into one, in_proj_qkvbfg_a, which per GPU at TP=2 is [12576 × 4096]: \(3 \times 4096\) (q, k, v with 32 heads × 128 each) + 32 (β per head) + 128 + 128 (the low-rank down-projections of the two gates). The math is that of six separate multiplications; the efficiency is that of one.

Broadcasting: when shapes don't match, but still fit

Adding a bias [N] to an activation [M, N] is so common that libraries do it without asking: the vector is “stretched” along \(M\). There are three rules of broadcasting:

  1. Align the shapes from the right. The last axis of one meets the last axis of the other. Missing axes on the left count as 1.
  2. On each axis, the sizes must be equal, or one of them must be 1. The one of size 1 is virtually repeated up to the size of the other.
  3. Any other case is an error. And the worst case is not even the error: it is when the shapes match by coincidence and broadcasting silently does the wrong math.

Two of the cases deserve special mention. The outer product [K, 1] ⊙ [1, N] produces a whole matrix from two vectors, and every matrix built this way has rank 1: each row is a multiple of the same row. It is the building block of section 6, and it is the “write” operation of linear attention (\(\mathbf v\mathbf k^\top\)). The mHC case is the same outer product with a batch axis in front: GLM and DeepSeek write the output \(\mathbf y\) of each sublayer into the 4 residual streams with weights \(\texttt{post\_mix}\), \([T,4,1]\odot[T,1,4096]\to[T,4,4096]\). Qwen's Gated Residual does the same write with one scalar per branch.

einsum: the notation that says it all

einsum notation gives a letter to each axis. Letters that appear in the inputs but not in the output are summed (contracted). The others survive, and there are two kinds of survivors: the free letter, which comes from a single operand, and the batch letter, which appears in both operands and in the output (the two inputs walk together along that axis, with no summing). A single line of einsum documents the whole operation, shapes included, and even gives you the cost for free: in a two-operand contraction, FLOPs = 2 × the product of the sizes of all the distinct letters.

Y = torch.einsum('mk,kn->mn', X, W)          # GEMM: K is contracted
S = torch.einsum('thd,shd->hts', q, k)       # per-head scores: [H, T, T]
P = torch.einsum('i,j->ij', a, b)            # outer product: rank 1
y = torch.einsum('bsgd,grd->bsgr', o, wo_a)  # DeepSeek-V4: grouped output

The three “einsum” cases in Fig. 3.5 animate the first, second and last lines. In the GEMM, k is contracted and \(2\,mkn\) is the \(2MNK\) from before. In the scores, h is batch (each head does its own math, as in the cube) and d is contracted. The last line comes from DeepSeek-V4's reference implementation . The attention output, with 64 heads × 512 = 32,768 numbers per token, is split into o_groups = 8 groups of 8 heads (4096 numbers each). The einsum says that each group \(g\) has its own \(r \times d\) = \(1024 \times 4096\) matrix (o_lora_rank = 1024): these are 8 independent multiplications in a single call, a batched matmul: g is a batch letter and d is contracted. The 8 outputs of 1024 are concatenated (8192) and wo_b brings them back to 4096. In vLLM at TP=2, each GPU gets 4 of the 8 groups.

Beneath the shape: memory, strides and views

A computer's memory is a one-dimensional tape: byte 0, byte 1, byte 2… A 4-axis tensor has to be “unrolled” onto that tape, and the stride is what says how. A tensor is not just data: it is a pointer to a block of memory plus metadata — type (how many bytes per element), shape, strides and starting offset. The address of any element comes from a simple calculation:

\[ \text{address}(i_0, i_1, \dots, i_{n-1}) \;=\; \text{base} + \Big(\text{offset} + \sum_{e} i_e \cdot \text{stride}_e\Big)\times \text{itemsize} \]
  • \(i_e\): the index along axis \(e\)
  • \(\text{stride}_e\): how many elements to skip to advance 1 along axis \(e\)
  • offset: where the tensor starts within the block
  • itemsize: 2 bytes for BF16, 1 for FP8

In a row-major contiguous tensor, the last axis has stride 1 and each earlier axis skips the product of the sizes that come after it: [3, 4] has strides (4, 1); [552, 2, 4, 128] would have (1024, 512, 128, 1). The magic is that several operations only touch the metadata and return a view, without copying anything:

transpose
swaps the strides around: (4, 1) becomes (1, 4). Reading in logical order now jumps around in memory, and the tensor is no longer contiguous.
slice
x[:, 1:3] changes the offset and the shape and keeps the strides.
expand / broadcast
stride 0 on the stretched axis: all the “copies” point to the same bytes.
view / reshape
only works without a copy when the strides are compatible; otherwise, reshape copies (and view fails).
.contiguous()
copies the data onto a new tape, in logical order.
as_strided
you pick the shape and strides by hand over an existing block. This is how vLLM fits several logical tensors into the same KV allocation (chapter 31).

The mail carrier who thought every house was 2 KiB

A GPU kernel (written in Triton, the Python kernel language, or in CUDA) receives raw pointers, not objects with a shape. If it computes addresses assuming a dense layout, instead of reading the tensor's strides, a non-dense view gets addressed wrong. The result is not an error: it is silent corruption. The GLM recipe ran into exactly this in the indexer of its sparse attention, DSA (chapter 13): a cheap module that scores groups of old keys and picks which of them each query will actually read.

GLM's indexer, the kpool, groups the keys 4 tokens at a time (“pools”) and stores each pool in 132 B. The KV cache is divided into fixed-size blocks (3584 tokens in the recipe, chapter 31), and each block has its own page in the indexer. The kpool's “tail” stores, for each KV block, the keys and scores of the last tokens that have not yet completed a group of 4. Logically it is a BF16 tensor [num_blocks, 2, KPOOL=4, HEAD_DIM=128], and if it were dense it would have stride(0) = 2 × 4 × 128 = 1024 elements (2048 B). But it is an as_strided view inside the indexer page: with the recipe's 3584-token block, each page has 896 pool rows × 132 B = 118,272 B, and stride(0) = 59136 elements. The prefill kernel that seeds the tail used the dense formula:

# before (dense): assumes each block occupies 2 consecutive KiB
base = (blk * 2 * KPOOL + t % KPOOL) * HEAD_DIM
# after (backport of vLLM #57477): reads the tensor's real stride
base = blk * TAIL_BLOCK_ELEMS + (t % KPOOL) * HEAD_DIM      # TAIL_BLOCK_ELEMS = tail.stride(0)
tl.store(tail_ptr + base + KPOOL_HEAD + offs, s, mask=m)    # KPOOL_HEAD = tail.stride(1) = 512

With the bug, the tail of block \(b\) was written at byte \(2048\,b\), that is, on page \(\lfloor 2048\,b / 118272 \rfloor\). The patch's test, with the geometry measured on a pair of GB10s (552 usable blocks), confirms that all 552 writes landed on pages 0 to 9. Block 58, for example, wrote inside block 1's tail. Each prefill left its own tail unseeded and scribbled over another request's indexer.

Modificationoverlay/patch_kpool_tail_seed_stride.py

Makes the seed kernel receive tail.stride(0) and tail.stride(1), instead of assuming a dense array. The fix does not depend on the geometry: it works for upstream's 1152-token block (a 38,016 B page) and for the recipe's 3584-token one. The patch is fail-closed: it checks the exact code anchor before writing and refuses a half-applied fix.

Why this matters to recipe writers

Whenever a recipe changes the block size, the page layout or the packing of a cache, every kernel that computes addresses by hand becomes a suspect. The right questions are: does the kernel read the tensor's strides or assume density? Does the multiplication \(\text{index} \times \text{stride}\) fit in 32 bits? (DeepSeek uses int64 pointer arithmetic because block_idx · block_stride exceeds \(2^{31}\) with ≳ 57 thousand blocks; see chapter 4.) And is there a test with a geometry different from the default one?

Low rank: squeezing a matrix through a bottleneck

Go back to the outer product: \(\mathbf u\mathbf v^\top\) is a rank-1 matrix. Add up \(r\) of them and you get a matrix of rank (at most) \(r\). Arranging the \(\mathbf u_i\) as the columns of \(A\) and the \(\mathbf v_i\) as the rows of \(B\), that sum is a product of two thin matrices. This is low-rank factorization:

\[ W \;\approx\; \sum_{i=1}^{r} \mathbf u_i \mathbf v_i^\top \;=\; \underbrace{A}_{m\times r}\;\underbrace{B}_{r\times n} \qquad\quad mn \;\to\; r(m+n) \text{ parameters} \qquad\quad 2Mmn \;\to\; 2Mr(m+n) \text{ FLOPs} \]
  • \(r\): the rank, the width of the bottleneck
  • \(B\): projects the input into an \(r\)-dimensional “latent” space
  • \(A\): expands the latent back to \(m\) dimensions
  • worth it when \(r < mn/(m+n)\)

The geometric reading is that of a funnel: everything the layer can express has to pass through \(r\) numbers. If the “true” transformation only uses a few directions, the funnel loses nothing. If it uses many, it does. To find out how many directions matter there is a perfect tool, the singular value decomposition (SVD): every matrix can be written as \(W = U\Sigma V^\top\), that is, it rotates (\(V^\top\)), stretches each axis by a singular value \(\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots \ge 0\) and rotates again (\(U\)). Keeping only the \(r\) largest singular values gives the best possible rank-\(r\) approximation (the Eckart–Young theorem), with error \(\sqrt{\sum_{i>r}\sigma_i^2}\).

Compare the two spectra. In the face, the first singular values carry almost all the energy, and with \(r\) around 6 (a quarter of the width) it already shows up. In the noise, all the singular values have similar sizes and every term you cut takes an equal piece of the matrix with it. Trained weights sit somewhere between the two extremes. That is why the recipe models do not compress full layers after training: they design the bottleneck into the architecture and train with it, and the network learns to use the latent. (A note on names: the lora suffix in q_lora_rank comes from LoRA, the fine-tuning method that adds a low-rank \(\Delta W = BA\) to frozen weights. The algebra is the same, but here the bottleneck is permanent and part of the model.)

Deep dive: the Markov head is a squeezed bigram

Chapter 5 shows that a Transformer with no layers is a bigram model, a \(V\times V\) table of “which token comes after this one”. DSpark's Markov head is exactly that table, but factored: \(\text{bias}(\text{previous}) = W_2\, W_1[\text{previous}]\). \(W_1[\cdot]\) is a row lookup (the previous token's row, 256 numbers) and \(W_2\) is a \(256 \to V\) GEMV. The rank 256 decides how many distinct “ways to continue” the drafter can tell apart.

Orthogonal matrices: rotate, reflect, project

Some matrices move space around without deforming it. An orthogonal matrix \(Q\) satisfies \(Q^\top Q = I\): its columns have length 1 and are perpendicular to each other. The consequence is that it preserves dot products, and therefore lengths and angles:

\[ (Q\mathbf a)\cdot(Q\mathbf b) = \mathbf a^\top Q^\top Q\,\mathbf b = \mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b \qquad\quad Q^{-1} = Q^\top \qquad\quad R(\theta)=\begin{bmatrix}\cos\theta & -\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta\end{bmatrix},\;\; R(\alpha)R(\beta)=R(\alpha+\beta) \]
  • \(Q^\top\): the transpose, which undoes \(Q\) for free
  • \(\det Q = +1\): rotation; \(\det Q = -1\): reflection
  • \(R(\theta)\): rotation by an angle \(\theta\) in the plane

Rotations and RoPE

RoPE (chapter 8) rotates each pair of dimensions of \(\mathbf q\) and \(\mathbf k\) by an angle proportional to the position. Since rotations compose, \((R_m\mathbf q)\cdot(R_n\mathbf k) = \mathbf q^\top R_m^\top R_n\,\mathbf k = \mathbf q^\top R_{n-m}\mathbf k\): the score ends up depending only on the distance between the tokens. DeepSeek-V4 uses the other property, \(Q^{-1} = Q^\top\): since the same vector serves as both key and value, the attention output carries the key's rotation, and the model undoes it by applying the inverse rotation (the “inverse RoPE”, in chapter 12). GLM , on the other hand, does not use RoPE in its MLA layers (qk_rope_head_dim: 0), but the kernel requires 64 position dimensions. The recipe fills those 64 with zeros, and that is exact: zeros add nothing to a dot product. The only price is memory, about 24% more KV in those layers.

Hadamard: the rotation that spreads outliers

The Hadamard matrix \(H_n\) has only entries \(\pm 1/\sqrt n\), is orthogonal and can be applied with \(O(n\log n)\) additions and subtractions. The practical effect is the one from section 1: a giant coordinate (an outlier, poison for quantization) becomes a medium value spread over all of them. And there is an invariance trick that makes this usable: since \((\mathbf xQ)(Q^\top W) = \mathbf xW\), rotating the activations by \(Q\) and the weights by \(Q^\top\) does not change the result, but it leaves both “well-behaved” for quantization. The EXL3 of GLM's experts uses random signs plus Hadamard (the suh/svh tensors), and GLM's indexer applies a size-128 Hadamard (index_head_dim: 128) before storing the keys in FP8. The details are in chapter 22.

Projections and reflections: removing (or flipping) a direction

Not every useful matrix is orthogonal. With a unit vector \(\hat{\mathbf r}\), \(\hat{\mathbf r}\hat{\mathbf r}^\top\) is a rank-1 outer product that projects any vector onto the direction \(\hat{\mathbf r}\). Subtracting it from the identity, with a strength \(\alpha\), creates a whole family:

\[ P_\alpha \;=\; I - \alpha\,\frac{\mathbf r\mathbf r^\top}{\lVert\mathbf r\rVert^2} \qquad\quad P_\alpha\,\mathbf x \;=\; \mathbf x - \alpha\,(\mathbf x\cdot\hat{\mathbf r})\,\hat{\mathbf r} \qquad\quad \text{component along } \hat{\mathbf r}\text{: } \times(1-\alpha) \]
  • \(\alpha = 1\): orthogonal projection, removes the direction (rank \(d-1\), no inverse)
  • \(\alpha = 2\): Householder reflection, orthogonal, \(\det = -1\)
  • \(\alpha > 1\): besides removing it, flips the component
  • \(\mathbf r\): the direction to remove (it does not need to be unit length)
ParameterABLIT_ALPHA=3.0

GLM's optional abliteration (ABLIT=1, which edits the o_proj of layers 15 to 45, the matrices that write into the residual stream) has two methods. The default, ABLIT_METHOD=auto, does a byte-for-byte transplant of those o_proj from a donor checkpoint whenever the ablit/transplant/ artifacts have been downloaded, and in that case \(\alpha\) is not used. It is in the proj method (ABLIT_METHOD=proj, or auto without the artifacts) that this section's algebra comes in: \(W' = (I - \alpha\,\mathbf r\mathbf r^\top/\lVert\mathbf r\rVert^2)\,W\). With the default \(\alpha = 3\), every output of those layers has its component along the “refusal direction” \(\mathbf r\) multiplied by \(1-3 = -2\): flipped and doubled. The repository itself describes \(\alpha > 1\) as “over-projection”, stronger and less coherent; \(\alpha = 1\) is the plain projection. Notice that the edit subtracts a rank-1 matrix from \(W\) (\(\alpha\,\hat{\mathbf r}(\hat{\mathbf r}^\top W)\)): low rank again. A caveat measured by the recipe itself: with the published direction vectors, the proj method behaves like a random direction against the original o_proj (\(\max|\mathbf r^\top W| = 0.084\), the same as for randomly drawn vectors), while the transplant reproduced the effect in 32 out of 32 test cases. How to find \(\mathbf r\), where to cut and how to measure the damage are all in chapter 19.

The same “identity minus an outer product” form reappears in the delta rule of GLM's and Qwen's linear attention: the recurrent memory is updated by \(S(I - \beta\,\mathbf k\mathbf k^\top) + \beta\,\mathbf v\mathbf k^\top\), which erases a bit of what was stored along the direction \(\mathbf k\) before writing the new value. If you understand \(P_\alpha\), you already understand half of that chapter.

The cost of a multiplication: FLOPs, bytes and the M regime

A GEMM has two costs: the arithmetic (FLOPs) and the memory traffic (bytes). The GPU has a ceiling for each. On the DGX Spark's GB10, that is ~125 TFLOPS in dense BF16 and ~273 GB/s of theoretical bandwidth (230–250 GB/s effective, according to the recipes' reports). The time of a multiplication is, at the very least, the larger of the two:

\[ t \;\gtrsim\; \max\!\Big(\frac{2MNK}{P},\; \frac{\text{bytes}}{B}\Big) \qquad \text{bytes} \approx \underbrace{K N\, b_w}_{\text{weights}} + \underbrace{M K\, b_a + M N\, b_a}_{\text{activations}} \qquad I = \frac{\text{FLOPs}}{\text{bytes}} \;\approx\; \frac{2M}{b_w}\;\;(M \ll K, N) \]
  • \(P\): peak FLOPs/s; \(B\): memory bandwidth (bytes/s)
  • \(b_w\): bytes per weight (2 in BF16, 1 in FP8, ~0.5 in 4 bits)
  • \(b_a\): bytes per activation (2 in BF16)
  • \(I\): arithmetic intensity, FLOPs per byte

When \(M\) is much smaller than \(K\) and \(N\), almost all the bytes are weights, and the intensity becomes \(2M/b_w\). In BF16 that gives \(I \approx M\) FLOP/B: intensity grows linearly with the number of tokens in the step. The GPU has a balance point, the ridge \(P/B\): on the GB10, \(125\times10^{12} / 273\times10^{9} \approx 458\) FLOP/B. Below it, the kernel is memory-bound: the cores wait for the bytes to arrive. Above it, it is compute-bound.

Take \(M\) from 1 to 42, the recipes' decode range: these are skinny GEMMs, almost GEMVs. The seesaw barely moves: the time is dominated by reading the weights, and processing 32 rows costs practically the same as processing one. This is the physical reason why continuous batching and speculative decoding are almost free: they fill the same weight read with more tokens. Now take \(M\) to 7168: the math starts to outweigh the bytes, and prefill becomes a compute problem.

Quantization changes the regime (and calls for dispatch by M)

Storing weights in 4 bits (\(b_w = 0.5\)) divides the bytes by 4 and multiplies the intensity by 4: in decode, which is memory-bound, the speed ceiling rises by almost the same proportion. In prefill, which is compute-bound, the story is different. A weight-only kernel has to dequantize each weight to BF16 before multiplying, which adds work without reducing the math. The same format that speeds up decode can slow down prefill, and the way out is to pick the kernel based on the size of \(M\): dispatch by M.

ParameterGLM53_KDA_BF16_LARGE_M=1 · boundary M > 512

GLM's dense projections are re-quantized to per-channel FP8 at load time and run on Marlin, a weight-only kernel that dequantizes the weights inside the kernel itself. The scheme is W8A16: weights in 8 bits, activations and math in 16 bits (BF16). It is great for small \(M\). For large \(M\), the recipe keeps a BF16 copy of the same logical matrix of KDA's in_proj ([12576×4096] per GPU) and switches to cuBLAS when \(M > 512\). Measured with \(M = 7168\): Marlin 27.21 ms, W8A8 (weights and activations in 8 bits) 12.57 ms, BF16 7.36 ms. Measured decode never went past \(M = 220\), and prefill stays at \(M \ge 768\), so the boundary at 512 falls in an empty band. Effect: TTFT (the time to the first token of the answer, dominated by prefill) −11.4% (16k) and −12.3% (100k). Cost: +3.3 GiB per GPU (98 MiB × 34 layers). The W8A8 attempt was dropped because quantizing the activations changed probabilities in a reproducible way.

MoE experts (chapter 11) add one more subtlety: the \(M\) that matters is that of each expert, because each token only visits 8 of the 288 experts in each GLM layer. In a 1024-token chunk, each expert receives on average \(1024 \times 8 / 288 \approx 28\) rows. With ~4-bit weights, the \(2M/b_w\) approximation gives \(\approx 4 \times 28 \approx 114\) FLOP/B, well below the 458. The GLM repository estimates ~112 FLOP/B and concludes that, at that chunk size, the MoE is memory-bound even in prefill. With the 7168-token chunk the recipe uses (the MAX_NUM_BATCHED_TOKENS), each expert receives \(7168 \times 8/288 \approx 199\) rows. At that point \(M\) is no longer that much smaller than \(K\) and \(N\), and the BF16 activations (4 times heavier per number than the 4-bit weights) enter the math: the full formula gives \(I \approx 500\) FLOP/B (the \(2M/b_w\) approximation would give ~800). That is the height of the ridge: the MoE prefill leaves the bottom of the ramp and reaches the corner of the roofline. That is why bigger chunks help, and why decode, with less than one row per expert (\(4 \times 8 \times 8/288 \approx 0.9\)), stays bound to bandwidth (our math; use the “1 expert” preset in Fig. 3.9). Hence the split between “thin” and “fat” experts (thin and fat) in chapter 28: in GLM, the exl3_moe kernel serves, in a single launch, the experts that receive up to EXL3_TEMP_ROWS_FUSED=32 rows (enough for 4 sequences × 8 decode tokens), and the ones that receive more go to the tier-E3 grouped GEMM, a single kernel that runs each fat expert's GEMM with its own \(M\).

In the recipe

Not every kernel specialized for small \(M\) wins. DeepSeek tested a packed decode path for \(M \le 8\) (B12X_W4A16_TC_DECODE=1) and measured 46 tok/s versus 55 for the default path with one sequence. It stayed off. The algebra tells you where to look; the benchmark tells you whether you found it.

The algebra playbook for a new recipe

(1) For each tensor, write [out, in] and what it means (and be wary of indexed tables and packed shapes). (2) Count total and active parameters and multiply by the bits: that is memory and traffic per token. (3) Estimate the decode \(M\) (sequences × (1 + drafts)) and the prefill \(M\) (the chunk) and compare \(2M/b_w\) with your GPU's ridge. (4) Look for low ranks and “odd” dimensions (48, 320, 4304) and check whether they pass the kernels' multiple-of-32/64/128 requirements. (5) Divide heads, groups, experts and vocabulary by the TP size. (6) If the recipe touches a cache layout, audit the strides. The next chapters give each of these steps its foundation.

One piece is missing from all the math in this chapter: the \(b\), the bytes per number. A weight in BF16, in FP8 or in 4 bits is not just “smaller”: it represents different values, with different errors. That is what the next chapter is about, How the computer stores numbers.
