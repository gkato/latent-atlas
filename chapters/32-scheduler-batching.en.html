A GLM-5.3 is writing a long essay for one person when someone else pastes a cold ~30K-token prompt. With the skip policy, the newcomer waited out the entire essay — 221 s in the original report, a “freeze” that in a coding session exceeded 20 minutes. With the opposite policy (0), it got in within 1 s, but during its ~41 s prefill the essay plummeted from 28.8 to 0.8 tokens/s. No weight changed, no kernel changed: the only thing that changed was who gets into each step. The component that decides this is the scheduler. In this chapter you'll learn to think like it — token budgets, sequence budgets, prefill slices, fairness, preemption, async scheduling — and to pick a recipe's numbers from your workload.

The traffic controller of every step

Recall the two phases of generation (prefill and decode chapter): in prefill the model processes the whole prompt in parallel (compute-bound); in decode it produces one token at a time (memory-bandwidth-bound, because every step rereads the weights). The vLLM engine runs in steps: at each iteration the EngineCore calls schedule(), then execute_model() on the GPUs, and finally update_from_output() (the anatomy of these processes is in the vLLM architecture chapter).

The conceptual trick of the V1 engine is that there are no separate phases. Each request \(r\) carries two counters: \(c_r\) = num_computed_tokens (how many positions already have their KV computed) and \(n_r\) = num_tokens_with_spec (prompt + output + pending speculative drafts; in async mode, the num_output_placeholders from section 8 are added as well). At each step the scheduler hands each request \(s_r\) new tokens so it can “catch up” to its total. Prefill, chunked prefill, prefix cache (a cache hit makes \(c_r\) start above zero) and speculative decoding (\(n_r\) includes the drafts) all become the same mechanism seen from different angles.

\[ \begin{gathered} \sum_{r \in \text{batch}} s_r \;\le\; B, \qquad \big|\text{RUNNING}\big| \;\le\; S \\ 0 \le s_r \le n_r - c_r \end{gathered} \]
  • \(s_r\): tokens scheduled for request \(r\) in this step
  • \(B\): the step's token budget (--max-num-batched-tokens)
  • \(|\text{RUNNING}|\): occupied slots — including those that get 0 tokens in this step
  • \(S\): maximum number of active sequences (--max-num-seqs)
  • \(n_r - c_r\): what is left for \(r\) (a whole prompt, or \(1+k\) in a decode with \(k\) drafts)

The algorithm that fills these inequalities is greedy and ordered: first the RUNNING list, in the order the requests were admitted; for each one, \(s_r = n_r - c_r\), clipped by the long-prefill threshold and by the remaining budget; then the KV cache is reserved block by block (allocate_slots). Then the WAITING queue: it admits new requests while there are free slots (\(S\)) and budget, consulting the prefix cache to skip tokens that were already computed. Step through the figure.

Analogy: the maître d'

The kitchen (GPU) prepares at most \(B\) portions per round. The tables that are already eating (decodes) ask for one portion each; the new tables (prefills) ask for hundreds. The maître d' always walks the dining room in the same order and keeps serving until the kitchen is full. Every policy in this chapter is a variation on in what order and how much each table gets served.

Static vs continuous: the bus and the conveyor belt

The naive way to serve several users is static batching: gather \(S\) requests, run until all of them finish, and only then form the next batch. It's a bus that only departs when full and only opens its doors at the last stop. Since output lengths vary a lot (one answer has 20 tokens, another 2,000), the slots of those who finished early sit idle — “bubbles” — and anyone who arrived later waits for the entire batch.

Continuous batching (iteration-level scheduling, popularized by the Orca system, OSDI 2022) re-decides the batch at every step: finished, out; the slot is filled in the next step. It's a sushi conveyor belt. In the figure, play with the output variability and compare slot occupancy.

\[ \begin{gathered} U_{\text{static}} = \frac{\sum_{i \in \text{batch}} L_i}{S \cdot \max_{i \in \text{batch}} L_i} \\ U_{\text{continuous}} \approx 1 \;\;(\text{while there is a queue}) \end{gathered} \]
  • \(U\): fraction of time the slots do useful work
  • \(L_i\): number of decode steps of request \(i\)
  • \(S\): batch slots

Why is filling slots worth so much for LLMs? Because decode is bandwidth-bound: a step with 8 sequences reads practically the same weight bytes as a step with 1. The tokens of the other seven “hitch a ride”. In the Qwen recipe (day-0 lane), the aggregate rises 3.8× from ×1 to ×8 streams, while per-stream speed drops to 49%. That's the fundamental trade-off you'll be tuning: aggregate throughput versus each user's speed.

Why this matters

With continuous batching, --max-num-seqs is not “the batch size”: it's the number of simultaneous slots. In the GLM README, --max-num-seqs 4 means “four generations in flight, not four parked sessions”: the API is stateless, and an idle conversation doesn't hold a slot.

In the TensorFold version

The GLM recipe now has a second version that swaps vLLM for TensorFold v0.6.0 + 53 patches (GLM-on-TensorFold recipe chapter). There the slot goes by another name: PARALLEL=4 means up to 4 requests decoded together, from 1 to 4, because the v1.3.2 code doesn't go past 4 (since v1.5, patch 0069, it goes from 1 to 8: on two Sparks, prose rises from 103.2 tok/s aggregate at 4 to 130.8 at 8). The fundamental trade-off shows up the same way in the measured ladder (sparkDash, prose, GPU clocks capped at 2,200 MHz): 60.4 tok/s with 1 request, 108.8 aggregate with 4, and each stream drops to 27.9 tok/s (46%). The contract, however, is stronger: replies served 4 at a time are bit-for-bit identical to the same requests served alone (11 of 11 staggered and 11 of 11 in a burst). Section 9 shows the scheduler that pulls this off.

Two budgets: sequences and tokens per step

Every recipe sets two ceilings. max_num_seqs (\(S\)) caps how many requests can be active; beyond that, they wait in the queue. max_num_batched_tokens (\(B\), “MNBT”) caps how many tokens the step processes, adding up decodes and prefill chunks.

With speculative decoding (speculation chapter), each sequence in decode doesn't cost 1 token: it costs \(1+k\) positions — the anchor token plus \(k\) drafts to verify. The number of verification rows in a decode step is therefore \(S\,(1+k)\).

Why can decodes and prefill chunks from different requests share the same forward? Because the flattened batch is a single matrix \(X\) of shape \([T \times d]\), with one row per scheduled token (\(T = \sum_r s_r\)) and \(d\) = the model's hidden dimension. Each linear layer and each MoE expert reads its weight \(W\) once and computes \(X\,W\): a GEMM \([M \times d]\cdot[d \times d']\) where \(M = T\) is the number of rows. For these layers, it doesn't matter which request each row came from. Only attention needs the boundaries: the query_start_loc vector marks where each request starts inside \(X\), so that each row sees only the KV of its own request (vLLM architecture chapter). In a speculative decode step, \(M = S\,(1+k)\): that's the “M” of the step's matrix multiplication. It dictates which CUDA graph sizes need to be captured: a decode in which every request has the same query length is uniform and can be replayed by a recorded graph; sizes above the largest captured graph run in eager mode, which is much slower.

\[ \begin{gathered} M_{\text{decode}} = S\,(1+k) \qquad \text{capture}_{\text{DS}} = 8\left\lceil \frac{S\,(1+k)}{8} \right\rceil \\ B_{\text{prefill}} = B - N_{\text{dec}}\,(1+k) \end{gathered} \]
  • \(M_{\text{decode}}\): the step's verification rows
  • \(k\): drafts per step (num_speculative_tokens)
  • \(N_{\text{dec}}\): sequences in decode in this step
  • \(B_{\text{prefill}}\): what is left of the budget for prefill chunks

Look at the DeepSeek preset: 6 sequences × 7 = 42 rows. The “raw” value 42 was truncated to 40 by the default capture grid, which left graphs of 7, 14, 21, 28 and 35 — the step with 6 sequences (42 tokens) ran eager. Rounding up to 48 brought the graph back, and the aggregate at 6 streams went from 122 to 139 tok/s. \(B\) also has side effects that don't show up in the inequality: memory profiling runs with the largest batch, so a larger \(B\) steals memory from the KV; persistent buffers are sized by it; and in kernels with fixed shared memory it may simply not fit.

Pitfall: “adding slots is free”

The DeepSeek .env is explicit: “no tested value of MAX_NUM_SEQS is established as safe in general”. With N=32 and MNBT 12288 the aggregate reached 325 tok/s at 32 streams — but the pair hung in bursts, and the mild form of the failure is silent truncation (no finish_reason, zero preemptions, no metric fires). One operator runs 13 slots (91 rows) in production, with no proof of safety. Throughput measured in a burst says nothing about stability.

Budgets without an MNBTMAX_WINDOW = 32 · MAX_ROWS = 16 · TF_GLM_PREFILL_ROWS = 2048

TensorFold has no max_num_batched_tokens, because it never puts prefill and decode in the same forward (section 9). The two budgets become three constants in the code. In a decode step, each stream verifies a window of at most 16 rows (MAX_ROWS: the pending token and up to 15 drafts, for long copy drafts), and the windows of all streams add up to at most 32 rows (MAX_WINDOW). That's the same 32 rows as the vLLM recipe, 4 slots × (1+7), but here the cap is global: each stream's depth changes every round, and whoever overflows the cap loses drafts. CUDA graphs are captured for every row count from 1 to 32, with positions and extents in device-side tables, so the 42 → 48 lesson doesn't apply: every window size has its own graph (CUDA graphs chapter). In a prompt step, the chunk has 2,048 rows (TF_GLM_PREFILL_ROWS) when nobody is decoding and 1,024 (TF_GLM_FILL_ROWS) when someone is. That's the role the MNBT and the long-prefill threshold play in vLLM.

Chunked prefill: slicing the prompt without stalling anyone

Without slicing, a prompt has to fit in a single step: \(B \ge\) max_model_len, which is infeasible with a 1M context. Worse: a 128K prefill step would freeze every decode for tens of seconds — head-of-line blocking. Chunked prefill cuts the prompt into chunks that share the step with the decodes. The vLLM documentation describes the policy as “decode first”: in V1 this comes from the scan order (RUNNING before WAITING, so pending decodes claim the budget before new prefills). Within the RUNNING list, however, whoever is in front gets served first — keep that in mind for the next section.

The chunk size is limited by two things: the remaining budget and the long-prefill threshold (--long-prefill-token-threshold, 0 = off), a per-request cap. The difference matters: MNBT is the size of the tray; the threshold is the biggest plate a single table can get per round.

The cost of a chunk: fixed + linear

Every step with prefill pays a fixed cost \(a\), independent of chunk size, plus a per-token cost \(b\). On GLM this was measured: ~0.3 s fixed + ~0.68 ms/token (82 tokens: 0.31 s; 3,584 tokens solo: 2.68 s). On DeepSeek, each 1024 chunk does 86 all-reduces of [1024×4096] bf16 (~690 MB per rank), prepares the indexer and compressor, and fires ~1.5K kernel launches; and, at 900K context, the sparse-attention indexer does ~80 TFLOP per 1024 chunk, duplicated on both GPUs. That term grows with the history (each token in the chunk scores the entire history to pick its top-k), so there the per-chunk cost is not constant: the \(b\) below rises with context — and that's precisely why 1024 slices interleave this nearly quadratic prefill with the decodes. On Qwen, which is MoE, an 8192 chunk touches all the experts: ~34 GB of weights read per GPU (~140 ms) plus 96 all-reduces (~150–180 ms). Tiny chunks repeat this fixed cost many times over.

\[ \begin{gathered} t_{\text{step}}(c) \approx a + b\,c \qquad \text{pause}_{\max} \approx a + b\,c \\ \text{TTFT}(P, c) \approx \Big\lceil \tfrac{P}{c} \Big\rceil a + b\,P \end{gathered} \]
  • \(c\): chunk size (threshold or remaining budget)
  • \(P\): prompt tokens to compute
  • \(a, b\): fixed cost and per-token cost of a step with prefill
  • pause: interval between tokens for a request already in decode during a mixed step

The two curves pull in opposite directions: large chunks amortize \(a\) and improve the TTFT of whoever arrives; small chunks shorten the pause for whoever is chatting. Drag the chunk size and watch the balance point shift.

Parameter--long-prefill-token-threshold 1024

1024 ≪ 8192: a chunk stays well below the budget and the decodes always get room (issue #27); 0 lets a single prefill eat the whole batch. 2048 was rejected: it cost ~1.5 GB of host RAM on the head node (MemAvailable 2.55 → 0.99 GB), because larger workspaces live in unified memory, outside the --gpu-memory-utilization budget. The adaptive chunk (DSPARK_ENABLE_ADAPTIVE_CHUNK=1: a 1024 cap only while a decode is active, otherwise the whole budget, so as not to pay 8 fixed costs on a cold 8K; estimated −10–25% TTFT up to 32K) was dropped for the same reason: MemAvailable 2.36/2.28 GB versus 3.8–5.2 GB. --enable-chunked-prefill is always on (turning it off would require \(B \ge\) 1M); the resulting mixed batches become ragged batches in DSpark, which needed stable per-request slots (DSpark chapter).

ParameterLONG_PREFILL_TOKEN_THRESHOLD= (empty)

Empty = stock scheduler, without the flag. An A/B/A with 3584 (= MNBT/2) measured: warm-session freeze 131/108 s → 15 s; contended prefill +6%; solo prefill +3…+11%, within drift. The tp3-throughput profile uses 2048 and the TP3 script defaults to 3584 — but (interpretation) the shared .env sets the variable to empty and suppresses that default. The recipe reminds you that the threshold “is not the fairness fix”: it also caps solo prefill. The fix is the mixed-step policy of section 6. Be careful when reproducing the numbers from there: the fair v5 retest ran with LONG_PREFILL_TOKEN_THRESHOLD=3584 (plus σ = 0.20 and 1000 ms), not with the empty threshold of the current .env.

Parameter--enable-chunked-prefill, no threshold

Chunked prefill always, chunks of up to 8192, required at 262K–1M. The audit suggests --long-prefill-token-threshold 2048 and --max-num-partial-prefills 2 to stabilize inter-token latency under concurrency; neither is used. Caution before copying the second one: in the V1 of the DeepSeek image (0.25.2) that field existed but the admission loop never read it (issue #27, section 5), and in v0.30's SchedulerConfig it doesn't even show up in this atlas's survey (unconfirmed). Before relying on it, check in your image's scheduler.py whether admission reads it — or use your own limit, such as DSPARK_MAX_INFLIGHT_PREFILLS. An architecture detail: the PLE layer (n-grams, n-gram and MTP chapter) needs the last 2 tokens of each request (ngram_context) to be kept across chunks — slicing the prompt forces the model to carry state from one chunk to the next.

Hybrids: the chunk has to stop at the state checkpoints

In models with linear attention (GLM's KDA, Qwen's Gated DeltaNet), each layer keeps a recurrent state that is overwritten at every token: you can't “go back to the middle”. In Mamba align mode, vLLM only saves this state at block boundaries — on GLM, every 3584 tokens, the least common multiple of the KV groups' page sizes (previous chapter). For the state to exist exactly at the boundary, the scheduler cuts the chunk so that it ends there. The stock code rounds the chunk down to a multiple of the block; a chunk smaller than one block becomes zero.

Overlaypatch_mamba_align_chunking.py (v2)

Three fixes: (1) shared checkpoints use the scheduler's LCM (3584), not the engine's smallest page (the drafter's), which misaligned the checkpoints; (2) smaller private Mamba pages force a stop at their own next boundary; (3) only offers of at least one block go through the rounding — smaller ones make sub-block progress. The EAGLE back-off only applies if a participating non-SWA group is EAGLE. Without (3), the fair policy (an initial 256 probe while there are no timing samples, rungs from 128 to 2048, all < 3584) would have its offers rounded down to zero: starvation — the .env warns that aligning 128/3584 “is not safe” without the overlay. The overlay also refuses decode-floor versions older than v5, which don't preserve the per-request alignment caps.

Starvation and fairness: who eats the budget first

The RUNNING loop in the V1 scheduler is, literally, while req_index < len(self.running) and token_budget > 0. When the budget runs out, whoever is further back gets nothing in that step; and a request with num_new_tokens == 0 is skipped with continue. It is not preempted: it keeps “running”, with its KV intact, just stalled. This is decode starvation, and it's invisible to the usual metrics — zero preemptions, zero queue.

That was exactly DeepSeek's issue #27. The vLLM 0.25.2 in the Anemll image defines max_num_partial_prefills, but V1's admission loop never reads it. With chunked prefill, async scheduling, many slots and threshold 0, several partial prefills admitted at the front of self.running consumed up to the entire budget, and the decodes further back got zero — starvation that grew with prompt length. In the figure, pick the scenario and the protections and watch the scheduler walk the list.

Three lessons. With threshold 0, the first prefill swallows the tray: the decodes behind it are skipped; the ones in front get their token, but the step lasts seconds and inter-token latency explodes. With threshold 1024 and several admissions, nobody is skipped, but the step carries several chunks and gets slow. With a badly tuned threshold (4096), two prefills are enough to bring the skips back — unless the #43 floor reserves room for those behind.

  1. #27 — cap on in-flight prefills. At the top of the admission loop, it counts the partial prefills from self.running (in exact parity with the stock _inflight_prefills set; the r3 repair, issue #211, fixed an undercount) and does a break upon reaching DSPARK_MAX_INFLIGHT_PREFILLS. A prompt that fits entirely in one chunk doesn't count as partial.
  2. Threshold 1024. Ensures that the single in-flight chunk leaves ~7K tokens of budget for the decodes.
  3. #43 — decode floor. When scheduling a prefill chunk, it reserves num_sampled_tokens_per_step for each active decode not yet visited behind it and limits the chunk to “budget − reserve” (or skips it). It logs each skip as (req_id, position, computed).

The repository's numbers tell the story: with #27 + a 1024 chunk, the worst decode in a ×8 burst of 8K/16K/32K went from 2.07/0.47/0.36 to ~15 tok/s, with zero preemptions; the spread between streams dropped from ~46× to ~1.05×; the worst inter-token latency, from 2790 to 67 ms. A per-request decode-rate dispersion remained (min/max 0.107–0.238), which motivated #43. With the current defaults (1024 / 8192 / cap 1) #43 is a no-op — it exists so that a wrong setting doesn't bring #27 back. A pure-Python simulator (tests/sim/test_issue43_scheduler_sim.py) proves, without a GPU, that no active decode is skipped while a prefill is scheduled in six cold cells (32K/62K × 2/4/8).

Deep dive: measuring fairness

Aggregate throughput hides starvation: three fast streams and one stalled stream still add up to a good total. That's why DeepSeek measures the spread (worst ÷ best stream in a burst, in TTFT or decode) in “gate26” — it's what decides between cap 1 and 2. And the .env warns: simultaneous heavy prefills remain limited by the scheduler (#80/#140); admission improves decode, not the TTFT contention among several huge cold prompts.

The mixed step: why mixing costs seconds

A step in which every request is in decode with the same number of positions is uniform, and the entire forward can be replayed by a FULL CUDA graph: zero CPU overhead, zero decisions. A mixed step — decodes + a prefill chunk — is not uniform. On GLM, the sparse attention backend (FLASHINFER_MLA_SPARSE_SM120) declares graph support only for uniform batches; the mixed step leaves the FULL graph (it can still use piecewise graphs) and adds work from the sparse indexer, the MoE and TP synchronization — plausible contributors whose individual weight the recipe did not isolate. Measured: every step with prefill costs ~0.3 s fixed on this kit. A counterintuitive consequence: a cap in tokens is not a budget in milliseconds. Even capped at 128 tokens, the mixed step left the incumbent at ~10 tok/s with 80k of KV.

This is GLM's fairness problem (issue #6). The GLM53_MIXED_PREFILL_CHUNK variable selects the policy:

Fair v5: split the time, not the tokens

The idea behind service-time fairness is to treat the contended prefill as a client entitled to a fraction \(\sigma\) of the engine's time. A credit accrues while the engine works; a mixed turn only happens when there's enough credit to pay for it; and the chunk size is chosen by a cost model learned online.

\[ \begin{gathered} C \leftarrow C + \sigma\,\Delta t \qquad \hat t(n) = \hat a + \hat b\,n \\ n^{*} = \max\{\, n \in \mathcal{L} : \hat t(n) \le T_{\max} \,\} \\ \text{turn if } C \ge \hat t(n^{*}) \;\Rightarrow\; C \leftarrow C - t_{\text{real}} \end{gathered} \]
  • \(C\): credit (s) of the contended prefill
  • \(\sigma\): GLM53_FAIR_PREFILL_SHARE
  • \(\hat a, \hat b\): linear fit over the last 24 samples (FIT_WINDOW)
  • \(\mathcal{L}\): the ladder 128, 256, 512, 768, 1024, 1536, 2048
  • \(T_{\max}\): GLM53_FAIR_PREFILL_MAX_STEP_MS
  • \(t_{\text{real}}\): measured cost of the whole mixed step

With the measured cost, \(0.3 + 0.00068 \times 1024 \approx 0.996\) s fits in 1000 ms and \(0.3 + 0.00068 \times 2048 \approx 1.69\) s fits in 2000 ms: that's why \(T_{\max}\) went up from 1000 to 2000 — “1000 ms pins this tuning” to the 1024 rung. Adjust the policy, the newcomer's size and the two knobs in the figure.

The complete algorithm, as documented in the patch_scheduler_decode_floor.py overlay (marker # [glm53-decode-floor:v5] in snapshot 94ae731; v7 keeps the steps, see just below):

  1. Decodes first. The base scheduler allocates the decodes' tokens, inputs and drafts before any prefill.
  2. Only contention creates debt. Solo prefill only provides cost samples; whatever competes with a decoder gets charged.
  3. Learned cost. Fit \(\hat a + \hat b n\) over solo and mixed samples, scaled by the p75 of actual/fit (clamped to [1, 1.5]); seed COLD_TOK_S = 1300 tok/s.
  4. Largest rung that fits, saving credit. v4 spent the credit on the smallest available rung, priced 1024 tokens at ~2.7 s (actual ~0.95 s) and got stuck at 128: ~70 tok/s of effective prefill.
  5. Newcomer probe and aging. A request that has never been served gets an immediate probe the size of the target rung \(n^{*}\) (capped to the step); while there are no timing samples yet, the probe uses GLM53_FAIR_PREFILL_CHUNK = 256. In practice the first mixed chunk sometimes comes out trimmed (78–117 tokens measured) and the following ones climb to 768–1024; after MAX_INTERVAL_MS an already-served prefill can borrow a chunk, but only after paying off its debt (aging). Selection by last service + round-robin; an async prefill in flight blocks the next mixed turn.

The live qualification (TP2, baseline → candidate → baseline) passed the KV-blocked progress and contention tests (5/5 per arm); decode, concurrency and temperature-0 identity came out inconclusive, because the two baselines differed from each other by more than the tolerance. Why this matters for your recipe

If your backend only has a FULL graph for uniform decode (like GLM's sparse kernels and KDA), every prefill that barges into the middle of a conversation pays the price of leaving the graph. Tokens don't measure that price; time does. The same reasoning explains why GLM's adaptive k picks the same k for the whole batch, and why Qwen uses FULL_DECODE_ONLY (graph only for uniform decode).

The other way out: don't mix

The TensorFold version of GLM attacks the same problem from the other side: a prompt chunk never shares a forward with decode rows. Each iteration runs a chunk or a decode round, never both. Every round is then a pure decode, replayed by the CUDA graph for its row count, and the mixed-step cost disappears. The cost moves elsewhere: while a chunk runs, nobody decodes. The question stops being “how many tokens fit in the mixed step” and becomes “how many iterations between chunks”. Section 9 shows that metronome and what it costs whoever is already in a conversation.

Preemption, recomputation and oversubscription

--max-model-len and --max-num-seqs are ceilings, not reservations: vLLM allocates blocks on demand, and the real constraint is \(\sum_{r\ \text{live}} L_r \le C_{\text{pool}}\) (the pool arithmetic is in the PagedAttention chapter). When allocate_slots can't find blocks, the scheduler performs a preemption: it removes the lowest-priority request (the last one in the list, under FCFS), frees its blocks and sends it back to the WAITING queue. In RECOMPUTE mode it will redo its prefill when it comes back — reusing whatever is still in the prefix cache.

The price of recomputation is the entire prefill all over again: \(t_{\text{recompute}} \approx L/R_{\text{prefill}}\). On DeepSeek, the 900K-token acceptance test had a TTFT of 1,028.85 s (~875 tok/s): preempting a request like that would cost on the order of 17 minutes of recomputation (an estimate, assuming nothing is reused from the cache). Oversubscription is legitimate — it's what makes it possible to offer a 1M ceiling with 6 slots — as long as the actual sum of live tokens fits almost all the time.

DeepSeek

A pool of 2,331,430 tokens (17.04 GiB at 0.835). 6×50k = 300k, easy; 6×200k = 1.2M, fits; 6×500k = 3.0M, close to or above; 6×1M = 6.0M, impossible — the extras wait. “Maximum concurrency 2.22×” only means that ~2 complete 1M requests fit at the same time.

Qwen

MAX_MODEL_LEN=1000000 × MAX_NUM_SEQS=8 asks for 8M tokens; the README says the pool “holds 2.48M” (a bf16-era number) → only ~2 resident 1M requests; the third is preempted and redone. vLLM doesn't OOM, it preempts. Hence the context × slots profiles.

GLM

DEFAULT_MAX_NEW_TOKENS=65536: without it, a client that omits max_tokens can grow its KV up to the end of the context and preempt other sessions. It is not an admission gate (“admission is per chunk”). The ladders count preemptions: the 2026-09-01 one had 1 per cell at ×16.

GLM·TF

A shared pool of 2,922,496 tokens at the measured start and a 1,048,576-token window per request: “one 1M-token conversation next to one more of 1M, or next to three of ~640k”. A request that doesn't fit waits at the head of the queue, and nobody jumps ahead of it. If no stream can grow, the youngest goes back to the queue and is redone from its prompt (section 9). The tokens it had already sent come out the same again, because sampling is keyed, and they are checked instead of resent.

Zero preemptions does not prove fairness

The #27 starvation had zero preemptions; so did the #141 silent truncation. Preemption measures a lack of memory; starvation measures a lack of step budget. Track both: the preemption counter in /metrics, the spread between streams, and responses without a finish_reason.

Async scheduling: the next step while the current one is in the oven

In synchronous mode, the GPU sits idle while the CPU schedules: the Python scheduler assembles the SchedulerOutput, the executor broadcasts it to the workers (shared memory within a node, ZMQ to the other node — an RTT of 0.33 ms in the DeepSeek recipe) and the workers prepare the inputs. With async scheduling, the scheduler is already planning step N+1 while the GPU executes step N.

\[ T_{\text{sync}} = t_{\text{CPU}} + t_{\text{GPU}} \qquad\qquad T_{\text{async}} \approx \max(t_{\text{CPU}},\, t_{\text{GPU}}) \]
  • \(t_{\text{CPU}}\): schedule + broadcast + prepare inputs
  • \(t_{\text{GPU}}\): the step's forward (~68–74 ms in a c=1 DeepSeek decode, according to the recipe's cost model)

The price: when planning N+1, the scheduler doesn't yet know which tokens N will generate. It reserves placeholders (num_output_placeholders) and, with speculation, fills the unknown drafts with -1. Everything that depends on content — structured-output grammars, end of reasoning, max_tokens counting — now has to deal with tokens that are “not yet revealed”.

Parameter--async-scheduling (DSPARK_ASYNC_SCHEDULING=1)

Explicit and identical on both ranks; 0 removes the flag on both — it serves #191's single-variable A/B (without async, the grammar bitmask rows come from real drafts) and costs ~4% single-stream; any other value is an error. The Stage-C overlay adds draft_token_lengths to ModelRunnerOutput (request → scheduled draft length): DSpark picks the length by confidence and the scheduler resizes spec_token_ids = [-1] * draft_len (clamped to [0, num_spec_tokens]) before the real ids reach the CPU; and, since use_eagle() includes DSpark, allocate_slots reserves num_lookahead_tokens = num_spec_tokens. The grammar bugs with drafts after the terminal token (#136) and at the reasoning boundary (#44993) live right there (API and structured outputs chapter).

Conceptmax_concurrent_batches = 1 synchronous, 2 async

With two batches in flight, the previous batch's Mamba state may still be needed. The single last_state_block_idx slot was overwritten before the block became freeable (7168 chunks crossing 3584 blocks), and the block stayed pinned until the end of the request; patch_mamba_align_state_free.py replaces it with a list and changes the reservation to \(1 + \texttt{max\_concurrent\_batches} + \texttt{num\_speculative\_blocks}\) (10, was 9). The drafter's block demand also counts the in-flight tokens: \(\lceil (2047 + 4096)/64 \rceil + 1 = 97\) blocks per 1M request, where 2047 = the drafter's 2048 sliding window (SWA) − 1, 64 = the drafter's block size and \(4096 = 2 \times 2048\) = tokens in flight with max_concurrent_batches = 2 (vLLM's arithmetic uses 2048 tokens per in-flight batch).

Overlap_Late · TF_GLM_MULTI_ASYNC=0

TensorFold overlaps CPU and GPU differently. It doesn't plan step N+1 blindly: the next window depends on which drafts were accepted, and keyed sampling runs on the host, in float64. What overlaps is the output. On the single-stream path (PARALLEL=1, as with DRAFTER=mtp), since patch 0013 a round's tokens are handed to the server (detokenize, check stop strings, write the stream) only once the next window is already queued on the GPU. That CPU work runs while the GPU verifies, not between one round and the next, and the window's time is measured with CUDA events, without synchronize(). On the multi-stream path, the recipe's, the scheduler thread only puts the tokens on each request's queue, and that work runs on the request's own thread. Our interpretation: since the overlap is on the output and not on the planning, the -1 placeholders from the figure above don't show up. With several streams, TF_GLM_MULTI_ASYNC=1 lets rank 0 send the iteration's message to rank 1 without waiting for the GPU. It's off by default, the recipe doesn't turn it on, and none of these options changes a reply's bits.

On Qwen, async turns itself on with MTP and the mp executor (since v0.28 it's automatic with draft models). And the from-scratch recipe tutorial for MiMo does the opposite — --no-async-scheduling — because of corrupted output with spec decode + async under concurrency (vllm#46669). Turning it on or off is a correctness decision before it's a performance one.

Update: chunk or round, the TensorFold scheduler

Everything so far is the vLLM scheduler. The GLM recipe now has a second version that serves the same EXL3 checkpoint on the same two Sparks with another engine: TensorFold v0.6.0 plus 53 patches. The engine's design is in the exact engine chapter, and the whole recipe in the GLM-on-TensorFold recipe chapter. This chapter's question stays the same, who gets into each step, but the conditions change. In v1.3.2 there are at most 4 requests at a time (PARALLEL=4; 1 to 8 since v1.5), each with a window of up to 1,048,576 tokens. The scheduler is a single thread on rank 0: it decides and sends rank 1 one message per iteration with the operations (ADMIT, FILL, ROUND, FINISH…), which both ranks apply in the same order. The pool of extents those operations move around is in the PagedAttention chapter. And there's a contract vLLM doesn't have: every stream must get exactly the bits it would get running alone. That limits what the scheduler may mix.

A metronome of iterations

Each rank-0 iteration runs the admissions and then one of two things: a prompt chunk from the oldest stream still filling (FIFO, with foreground before background), or a decode round for everyone already decoding. The two never go in the same forward. That's the chunk-or-round rule, and a credit picks between them. While someone is decoding and someone is filling, each iteration adds \(\varphi\) to the credit. When it reaches 1, a chunk goes out and the credit loses 1; otherwise, a round goes out. With the default \(\varphi = 0.5\) (TF_GLM_FILL_SHARE), the sequence is round, chunk, round, chunk… With \(\varphi = 1\), every prompt is filled before any decode continues. If nobody is decoding there's no contention: the chunk always goes out, with up to 2,048 rows (it also stops at kept points and at the prompt's end).

\[ \begin{gathered} C \leftarrow C + \varphi \qquad \text{chunk if } C \ge 1 \;\Rightarrow\; C \leftarrow C - 1 \\ f_{\text{time}} = \frac{\varphi\, t_c(n)}{\varphi\, t_c(n) + (1-\varphi)\, t_r} \end{gathered} \]
  • \(C\): the scheduler's credit (a single one, on rank 0), counted in iterations, not seconds
  • \(\varphi\): TF_GLM_FILL_SHARE, the fraction of iterations that go to chunks (\(0 < \varphi \le 1\); default 0.5)
  • \(n\): chunk rows, 1,024 when someone is decoding (TF_GLM_FILL_ROWS) and 2,048 without contention (TF_GLM_PREFILL_ROWS), in v1.3.2; since 2026-10-08 the recipe uses FILL_ROWS=2048 with decode too
  • \(t_c(n)\), \(t_r\): duration of an \(n\)-row chunk and of a decode round
  • \(f_{\text{time}}\): the fraction of time the prompt gets

Compare with section 6's fair v5. There the credit is measured in seconds: prefill is entitled to a fraction \(\sigma\) of the engine's time. Here it's measured in iterations, and a chunk iteration costs much more than a round. A toy model gives the order of magnitude. Patch 0049's docstring says a short forward's cost is mostly fixed, ~0.12 s on two Sparks, and sparkDash measured ~1,950 tok/s of solo prefill in 2,048-row chunks. Drawing a straight line through those two points, a 1,024-row chunk takes ~0.59 s. For the round, use ~70 ms (illustrative: the draft simulator's old cost table gives 29–66 ms for windows of 1 to 8 rows). With \(\varphi = 0.5\), half of the iterations turns into ~89% of the time for the incoming prompt. Whoever was already talking drops from 60.4 to around 6 tok/s while the prompt fills, and in exchange a 32K prompt is in after ~21 s, versus ~17 s if it were alone. With \(\varphi = 0.25\), the model gives ~16 tok/s to the ongoing conversation and ~25 s to the prompt. Try it in the figure.

These are two philosophies. GLM's fair v5 on vLLM protects whoever is already answering: in the retest, the incumbent stayed at 20.6–20.9 tok/s (24.7 alone), and the 30K newcomer waited 163.6 s. TensorFold's metronome, in the v1.3.2 default, protected the newcomer's TTFT: in the toy model, the ongoing conversation drops to about a tenth of its speed while the prompt fills (~21 s for 32K), and the prompt gets in close to solo prefill speed. The trade-off sits on a single knob, because a low \(\varphi\) gives time back to decode. The two engines don't compare point by point (different kernels, drafts and costs), but the design question is the same as in section 6: in what unit does the scheduler measure fairness.

Sliced fill: who pays for a new prompt

The paragraph above describes v1.3.2. In v1.4 (2026-10-03), the default flipped who pays. With the sliced fill (patch 0062, FILL_BUDGET_MS=200), while others are decoding, a new prompt's chunk runs a few layers at a time, about 200 ms per slice, with a drafted decode round between slices (FILL_DRAFTS=1); before, each 1,024-row chunk (~0.65 s) froze every other reply. And smooth streaming (patch 0061, STREAM_SMOOTH=1) sends each token as one event, at a steady pace, from a 400 ms playout buffer, so drafted bursts and short pauses vanish from the wire (text shows up ~0.4 s after it is decoded).

Measured on two Sparks (PARALLEL=4, DFlash2, 1,024-row chunks, two boots of each configuration): three 400-token replies already streaming when a fresh ~25k-token prompt arrives. The gaps between each reply's events went from 630 / 673–690 / 754–818 ms (p50 / p90 / max) to 81 / 149–150 / 230–231 ms; the three received 650–662 tokens during the fill, against 81; and the new prompt's first token went from 16.1 to 21.1–21.3 s. The newcomer pays ~5 s so that whoever is already answering doesn't freeze, the same philosophy as vLLM's fair with another mechanism (our interpretation). The bits don't change (22/22 concurrent cases equal their serial references), and a request alone fills as before; FILL_BUDGET_MS=0 goes back to the whole-chunk metronome. Since 2026-10-08 the recipe uses FILL_ROWS=2048, the whole chunk, whose buffers already exist (commit cefd1b0): on three Sparks, with a 35k-token reply streaming, a warm +20k-token turn on a 596k conversation went from 15.6–15.7 to 14.0–14.1 s, and a cold ~256k prompt from 132.2–132.6 to 113.4–114.0 s, with the same gaps in the reply.

Beyond the Spark, the same choice shows up in the chunked-prefill size of vLLM and SGLang (how long a prompt may pause the decodes per step) and, at the limit, in the prefill/decode disaggregation of Dynamo and llm-d, which puts new prompts on other GPUs so nobody's decode stops. The question is always the same: who pays for the new prompt.

What goes into each forward: the batched window and the multi-prompt chunk

In a round, each decoding stream proposes its drafts under its own policy: copy drafts first, then DFlash2 (speculation chapter). A stream's window is the pending token plus the drafts, up to 16 rows. Everyone's windows form the batched window, cut down to 32 rows: while the sum is over the cap, one draft comes off the longest window (the youngest stream's, on a tie). Then comes one verification forward over all the rows. What is row-independent (embeddings, projections, MoE, head) runs once over the whole window; KDA runs per segment, each one from its own stream's state; DSA reads each stream's extent. One packed all-gather carries the candidates to the samplers. Each stream accepts drafts as long as they equal its keyed sample and commits only its own rows. The default depth policy (TF_GLM_MULTI_DEPTH=policy) lets each stream choose as if it were alone. The joint alternative splits the round's rows among the streams by expected tokens per millisecond, on a cost curve measured at startup.

On the prompt side the problem is the reverse. Without grouping, prompts that arrive together are filled one stream at a time, interleaved with rounds. A 130-token prompt is already two chunks: one up to the grid point at 128, where the state is kept, and another for the 2-token tail. The last of four such prompts waits for eight chunks and the rounds between them. But at short lengths a forward's cost is mostly fixed, ~0.12 s on two Sparks, because each layer's weights are read once, whatever the rows. Multi-prefill (MULTI_PREFILL=1, patch 0049) puts the next rows of several prompts in the same chunk, side by side: [s1][s2][s3]…. The row-independent work runs once for all of them, with “one expert read for every prompt”. What reads or writes a stream's state (KDA recurrence, latent write, indexer, attention) runs piece by piece, through the very calls a chunk of its own makes, so each piece's bits are those of a chunk of its own.

The order inside the chunk follows a classic scheduling policy. Foreground comes before background; then fewest rows left first; then arrival order. Each prompt goes up to its next point (a kept point or its end) if it fits; otherwise it takes what's left of the budget in multiples of 64. That way a short prompt's first token doesn't wait behind a long one, and the long one takes the rest. The budget is that of a normal chunk: 1,024 rows with decode, 2,048 without. Image prompts and replays of a kept prompt don't join the group. In the figure, compare the two trays.

Measured with sparkDash, 4 prose requests at once: 103.4 → 108.8 tok/s aggregate and TTFT from 590 to 340 ms. Structured, with 3 and 4 requests: 175.2 → 196.3 and 196.3 → 227.9 tok/s. A single request doesn't change, and the replies are the same bit for bit. In vLLM, section 1's flattened batch already joins pieces of several requests in one forward; here, joining prompts and joining decodes are separate operations, each with its own tray.

Admission, pause and give-back: preemption that doesn't change the reply

  1. Slots. With 4 live streams, the next request waits in the queue, and foreground requests go before background ones.
  2. Room. A request is admitted only if the pool has rows for the prompt plus the verification window, with 2,048 of headroom for each decoding stream (the arithmetic from the previous chapter). If it doesn't fit, it is held at the head of the queue, and nobody jumps ahead, not even a smaller request that would fit. It's strict FIFO.
  3. Pause. Every round, each stream's extent must grow to fit the widest window. A stream that can't stays paused for that round, losing nothing.
  4. Give-back. If everyone decoding is paused and nobody is filling, the youngest stream without a grammar or images gives up its slot and extent and goes back to the queue. When it returns, it is redone from its prompt; the tokens the client already received “are owed, not sent again”. If every stream has a grammar or images, the youngest fails with NoRoom.
  5. Background yields. With all 4 slots taken and a foreground request waiting, a stream with priority: "background" is ended and goes back to the queue. When it returns, it too is redone from its prompt, and the final reply is the same as the solo run.

Compare with vLLM's RECOMPUTE preemption (section 7): the preempted request keeps the tokens already generated and rebuilds the KV of prompt + output in a prefill. In TensorFold, the stream given back decodes the same tokens again, and the code checks each one against what was already sent: if any differed, the request would end with an error. The code notes that in TensorFold prefill and decode don't give the same bits; hence, by our interpretation, redoing the output as a prefill could steer the continuation elsewhere. Re-decoding with the same sampling key, every token comes out identical (exact engine chapter). The price is time, because the tokens already generated are generated again.

Pitfall: the 0.9% shortcut that emptied the cache

TF_GLM_MULTI_LONE=1 makes a stream decoding alone use the single-stream CUDA graphs instead of the batched-window ones: +0.6–0.9% at 1 stream. But those graphs recorded slot 0 and base 0 of the pool, so the stream first has to move to the first rows, and whatever lives there gets evicted: other conversations' kept prompts. With three agent conversations taking turns (~4.5k tokens each, 6 warm turns), 0% of prompts came from the cache with 1, versus 98.1% with 0 (issues #12 and #13). Since v1.3.1 the recipe pins 0. The lesson holds for any scheduler: a scheduling decision touches memory, and the metric it breaks may live in the next chapter.

Serving profiles: how to pick the numbers

Putting it all together, a serving profile (like the GLM recipe's tp2-long-coding profile) is a coherent choice of context × slots × budget × policy, backed by measurements. The concurrency ladder (on GLM, tests/bench_concurrency.py) is the instrument that validates it: run 1, 2, 4, 8… simultaneous streams and record the aggregate, per-stream speed, TTFT and preemptions. Pick the recipe in the figure.

On Qwen v0.30 the aggregate grows up to ×8 and stops: ×16 exceeds MAX_NUM_SEQS=8, half the requests wait in the queue and mean TTFT jumps from 0.1–0.4 s to 6.5–9.7 s. On GLM (1M, MNBT 2048, 16 slots, mixed 512), the coding aggregate goes from 41.9 to 127.2 tok/s while each stream drops from 44.3 to 10.2. On the TensorFold version of GLM, which stopped at 4 streams in v1.3.2, the prose ladder goes from 60.4 to 108.8 tok/s (1.8×) and TTFT from 170 to 340 ms. The sweet spot isn't on the curve: it's in your contract with the user.

A roadmap for your numbers

  1. Describe the workload. Peak concurrency, prompt distribution (how many huge cold ones arrive together?), output size, prefix reuse.
  2. Does it fit? Estimate the typical \(\sum L_r\) against the pool; max_model_len is a ceiling, the sum is what triggers preemption.
  3. Slots. Pick \(S\) from the desired concurrency and the acceptable per-stream floor; check the verification rows \(S(1+k)\), the capture sizes and known stability limits.
  4. Budget. Start between 4096 and 8192; check the side effects (activations in profiling, shared memory, encoder cache, host RAM on UMA).
  5. Fairness. Prefill threshold (≪ MNBT), a cap on in-flight prefills and, on graph-sensitive backends, a time-based policy.
  6. Measure what matters. Concurrency ladder, spread between streams (not just averages), preemptions, responses without finish_reason. One variable per boot, A/B/A with a same-day baseline (benchmarking chapter).
In the recipe

DeepSeek's scheduler signature is “one prefill at a time, in 1024 slices, with a floor for the decodes”: --enable-chunked-prefill, --max-num-batched-tokens 8192, --long-prefill-token-threshold 1024, DSPARK_MAX_INFLIGHT_PREFILLS=1, hotfixes #27 and #43, --async-scheduling and 6 slots with capture 48. GLM on vLLM trades slices for time (fair, now v7) and takes care of the 3584 alignment, and the TensorFold version of GLM doesn't even mix: it alternates chunk and round and groups the prompts that arrive together; Qwen stays on stock with 8 slots, because there the cache is plentiful and the limit is the scheduler.

One piece is still missing: all of this assumed cold prompts. In agents and conversations, most of the prompt was already seen in the previous turn — and recomputing it is waste. The next chapter shows how vLLM reuses KV blocks that were already computed, and why, in hybrid models, hits only happen at block boundaries: on GLM, the same 3584 you just met; on Qwen, ~1600–1664 tokens.
