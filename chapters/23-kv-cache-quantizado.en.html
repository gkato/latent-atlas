On DeepSeek-V4 served across two DGX Sparks, the boot log announces 2,331,430 tokens of KV cache in 17.04 GiB per rank: just over two 1-million-token conversations fit at the same time. With a “plain” GQA-8 attention in BF16 and the same number of layers, the same memory would hold about a hundred thousand tokens. Most of the difference comes from the architecture, which you saw in the chapters on MLA and sparse attention. The last lever is to store each number in the cache with fewer bits. In this chapter you will quantize the KV for real: choose scales, find out where they enter the kernel, assemble 656- and 584-byte rows byte by byte, understand why a dtype called nvfp4_ds_mla has nothing 4-bit about it, and see why quantizing the keys of a sparse attention can change what the model reads, not just how precisely it reads it. At the end of section 6, the second version of the GLM recipe , on an engine written for the model's family, stores the same latent in a 528-byte row with a single power-of-two scale.

Store and read: the two costs of KV

Recall the math from chapter 10. Each attention layer stores, for every token already seen, one K vector and one V vector per KV head. In bytes:

\[ \text{bytes/token} = L_{\text{KV}} \times 2 \times H_{kv} \times d_h \times \frac{b}{8}, \qquad M_{\text{KV}}(N) = N \times \text{bytes/token} \]
  • \(L_{\text{KV}}\): layers that store KV
  • \(2\): one K vector and one V vector
  • \(H_{kv}\): KV heads (in GQA, far fewer than the query heads)
  • \(d_h\): dimension of each head
  • \(b\): bits per number (16 in BF16, 8 in FP8, ~4.5 in NVFP4)
  • \(N\): stored tokens of the sequence

That number is charged twice. The first charge is for capacity: the KV pool is whatever memory is left after weights, activations and CUDA graphs, and the \(M_{\text{KV}}\) of every live sequence has to fit in it. The second is for bandwidth. During decode, each new token consults the sequence's entire cache (in dense attention), so the step has to bring those bytes from memory to the SMs. Since decode is memory-bound, the time for this part is, to a good approximation, bytes read divided by bandwidth:

\[ t_{\text{KV}} \approx \frac{\text{KV bytes read in the step}}{B_{\text{mem}}} \]
  • \(B_{\text{mem}}\): effective memory bandwidth (the Qwen recipe estimates ~235–245 GB/s as realistic on a GB10, out of 273 GB/s theoretical)
  • bytes read: \(M_{\text{KV}}(N)\) in dense attention; in sparse attention, only what was selected plus what the indexer scans

In the figure below, an illustrative GQA model with 8 KV heads × 128 dims across 43 layers (the comparison the DeepSeek inventory uses) stores and reads its own KV. On top is the warehouse: the 17 GiB pool, split into 1 GiB shelves. Below is the conveyor belt: the little K and V boxes travel from memory to the SMs at a fixed speed, which is the bandwidth.

Look at the right end of the slider. With 1M tokens in BF16, a single sequence asks for ~185 GB of KV and each decode token would spend ~0.77 s just reading cache. FP8 halves both and NVFP4 gets to just over a quarter, but none of them makes 1M fit in 17 GiB. What made that possible in the recipes was the architecture: DeepSeek-V4 spends ≈3.85 KB per token at long context, about 2% of GQA-8 BF16. Quantizing the KV is the multiplier that comes on top of the architectural savings, and that's why every recipe fights for it.

Why the KV is not “just another tensor to quantize”

Weights are fixed: you quantize them once, offline, with calibration and KLD measurement (chapter 20). The KV is born during inference, so the scale has to be decided before the first token (calibrated) or computed at the moment of writing (dynamic, per block). On top of that, the error is written once and re-read in every following step of the conversation. And the two vectors err in different ways: an error in K goes into the softmax exponent and changes the attention weights (in sparse attention, it even changes which tokens are read); an error in V changes the content being summed.

Analogy: the detective's filing cabinet

Each token leaves an index card in the cabinet: the tab (K) says when the card is relevant and the back (V) holds the content. Quantizing is writing the cards in smaller handwriting. More of them fit in the drawer, and the detective flips through the whole drawer faster. But handwriting that's too tiny on the tabs makes the detective pull the wrong card, and that is exactly the risk the recipes measure.

FP8 in the KV: the scale, there and back

The working format is the FP8 E4M3 from chapter 21: 1 sign bit, 4 exponent bits and 3 mantissa bits, maximum 448, smallest normal \(2^{-6}\) and smallest subnormal \(2^{-9}\). Quantizing a KV number means dividing by a scale, rounding to the nearest E4M3 and storing 1 byte. Reading is the reverse path, dequantization:

\[ c = \operatorname{round}_{\text{E4M3}}\!\left(\frac{x}{s}\right), \qquad \hat{x} = s \cdot c, \qquad s = \frac{\max |x|}{448} \ \ \text{(or a fixed scale)} \]
  • \(x\): a number from K or V, produced in BF16
  • \(s\): the scale, which maps the range of \(x\) onto the range of the format
  • \(c\): the stored FP8 code (1 byte, allocated by vLLM as uint8)
  • \(\operatorname{round}\): round-to-nearest-even, saturating at ±448
  • \(\hat{x}\): the value the attention kernel actually uses

With 3 mantissa bits, the gap between two neighboring values is 1/8 of the value, so the rounding error stays below ~6% of each number (on average, far less: the Qwen recipe measures a relative RMSE of 2.4–2.7% on the weights it converts to per-channel FP8). The important decision is how many numbers share the same scale:

GranularityWhere the scale livesIn the recipesCostOutliers
per tensor1 scale for all of a layer's K and 1 for all of its V, fixed before serving k_scale/v_scale (1.0 when the checkpoint doesn't ship them)~0 B per tokenone extreme value stretches everyone's scale

Per-tensor scales must exist before the first token. Either they came calibrated in the checkpoint (the quantizer ran samples, measured the amax of K and V in each layer and wrote k_scale and v_scale), or vLLM uses 1.0. The Qwen recipe's NVFP4 checkpoint declares no KV quantization (kv_cache_quant_algo: None), so the kernel receives 1.0 scales (the inventory's inference from the code). The option that estimated per-tensor scales at runtime (calculate_kv_scales) was removed from vLLM in v0.28.

A scale of 1.0 sounds dangerous, but in FP8 it usually works. With \(s=1\), E4M3 directly stores values between ~0.002 and 448 with almost constant relative precision. K usually goes through QK-Norm (in Qwen, a per-head k_norm) and, in MLA, the latent goes through kv_a_layernorm, which bounds the magnitudes. V is not normalized, but it generally also stays in moderate ranges (interpretation). All of that fits comfortably inside the E4M3 range. In FP4 E2M1 there is no such slack: the format has only eight magnitudes (0, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4, 6), so without a per-block scale almost everything becomes zero or saturates. DeepSeek-V4 uses blocks with a UE8M0 scale, a power of 2 stored in one byte:

\[ s_b = 2^{\left\lceil \log_2 \left( \max\left(\max_{i \in b} |x_i|,\ 10^{-4}\right) / 448 \right) \right\rceil}, \qquad \text{byte}_b = \log_2 s_b + 127 \]
  • \(b\): a block of 64 dims of a token's K vector
  • \(\lceil\cdot\rceil\): the exponent is rounded up, so \(|x|/s_b \le 448\) always and nothing saturates
  • \(10^{-4}\): a floor that protects nearly-zero blocks
  • \(\text{byte}_b\): just the exponent, with bias 127; multiplying by \(s_b\) means adding to the exponent

The OCP MX format uses the floor instead of the ceiling: \(X = \lfloor \log_2 \text{amax} \rfloor - e_{\max}\). With that, the amax divided by the scale lands in \([256, 512)\) in E4M3 and can exceed 448, and then it gets clipped. DeepSeek's ceiling gives up as much as 1 bit of range so that it never saturates. NVFP4 (chapter 21) makes a different choice: each block's scale is an E4M3 number, not a power of 2, on top of an FP32 scale for the whole tensor. In the figure, a 16-number K vector makes the full round trip with each of these scales. The blocks hold 8 numbers so they fit on screen (in DeepSeek they hold 64).

Three lessons come out of this game. In FP8, “per tensor with scale 1” already gives an error of a few percent, and only the tiny numbers (below \(2^{-6}\), in the subnormal range) suffer more. In FP4, the per-tensor scale is wrecked by a single outlier: it stretches \(s\) and pushes its neighbors to zero (14 of the 16 numbers with “tensor · amax”), and that's why every 4-bit format uses blocks. With blocks of 8, 9 zeros remain, and only because the outlier still shares block 0 with seven neighbors.

The third lesson is about the floor, and it doesn't depend on the outlier. With the floor, \(\text{amax}/s\) lands in \([2^{e_{\max}}, 2^{e_{\max}+1})\) and saturates whenever the mantissa of the amax exceeds 1.75 in E4M3 (because 448 = 1.75·2⁸) or 1.5 in E2M1 (because 6 = 1.5·2²), outlier or not. In the FP8 example, the block that saturates is block 1, which has no outlier: \(x_{10}\) = −1.85 becomes −1.75. The outlier's block fits, because 26 = 1.625·2⁴ and 26/s = 416 ≤ 448. In FP4 both blocks saturate. DeepSeek's ceiling never saturates.

Be careful with SQNR on this vector

SQNR (the ratio between signal energy and error energy, in dB) weights each error by the square of the value, and almost all of this vector's energy sits in the 26: that's 676 of the ~683 in the sum of squares. In FP4 with the outlier, “tensor · amax” represents the 26 exactly and scores 23.1 dB, more than the UE8M0 blocks (21.0 dB with ceiling and 20.9 dB with floor), even though it zeroes 14 numbers instead of 9. That's why the figure uses the zeroed values and the median relative error as its main readouts. A metric that only looks at energy hides the destruction of the small numbers. NVFP4 mode shows the gain from blocks in dB too: the outlier's block gets exactly amax/6 (E4M3 scale code 448 times the global scale), block 1 gets a fine scale, and SQNR climbs to 26.8 dB with the same 9 zeros. Without the outlier, notice that rounding the E4M3 scale can leave it slightly too small and saturate the largest number in the block (\(x_3\) = 1.35 becomes 1.32).

Inside the kernel: the scale comes out of the product

The Qwen recipe shows the simplest path, per-tensor FP8, and the trick that makes it cheap. Qwen3.8-Flash-Next only has KV in 12 of its 48 layers (the QSA layers, one in every four) plus 1 MTP layer, with 2 KV heads of 256 dims: \(13 \times 2 \times 2 \times 256 \times 2\,\text{B} = 26{,}624\) B/token in BF16. The stock QSA backend advertised supported_kv_cache_dtypes = ["auto", "bfloat16"] and rejected FP8 with Qwen3.8-Flash-Next QSA requires a BF16 main KV cache. The limitation was in the kernel, not in the checkpoint: the old handoff from the single-Spark kit said “FP8 KV cache is NOT supported”, and the patch proved otherwise.

What the patch exploits is an algebraic identity. If the scale is the same for every term of a sum, it comes out of the sum:

\[ \text{score}_j = \frac{q \cdot \hat k_j}{\sqrt{d_h}} = \frac{q \cdot (s_k \tilde k_j)}{\sqrt{d_h}} = s_k \, \frac{q \cdot \tilde k_j}{\sqrt{d_h}}, \qquad o = \sum_j p_j \, \hat v_j = s_v \sum_j p_j \, \tilde v_j \]
  • \(\tilde k_j, \tilde v_j\): the FP8 codes of token \(j\) converted to BF16 (an exact conversion: every E4M3 value exists in BF16)
  • \(s_k, s_v\): the layer's per-tensor scales (layer._k_scale, layer._v_scale)
  • \(p_j\): attention weights after the softmax
  • \(d_h = 256\) in Qwen

Instead of rebuilding each \(\hat k_j\) in FP32 before the product, the kernel runs the tensor core product directly on the codes promoted to BF16 (upcast) and multiplies the result once by \(s_k\). On the V side, it multiplies the normalized output by \(s_v\) at the end. The patch's comment sums it up: it is algebraically equivalent before rounding, it avoids materializing FP32 dequantization tiles, and it lets FP8 use the same tile width as BF16 on the GB10. The figure compares the three ways of doing the math.

The third mode explains why DeepSeek and GLM, with per-dim-block scales, need kernels built for the layout (sections 6 and 7). With one scale per token per block, the scale doesn't come out of the product: the kernel has to apply it to each partial sum or dequantize beforehand. Qwen, with a per-tensor scale, only needs two scalar multiplications per tile. In exchange for that cost, the per-block scale errs less: in the figure, the four per-block scores are on average 0.033 away from the exact reference, versus 0.063 for the per-tensor scale (the fourth score gets worse, because in FP8 much of the difference is rounding luck). The big gain shows up when an outlier would push its neighbors into the subnormals, or when the format has few bits, as you saw with FP4. The per-block scale is also dynamic: it is computed at write time and needs no calibration. That's what DeepSeek and GLM buy with kernels built for the layout: less outlier error, in exchange for per-block partials. DeepSeek-V4 has one more reason, because it was trained with those blocks (section 7). In the implementation, files/patch_qsa_fp8_kv.py (401 lines, vendored from the single-Spark kit, with the approach credited to the lancelind/qwen3.8-Flash-DGX project) does six things:

  1. Advertises the dtype. supported_kv_cache_dtypes gains "fp8" and "fp8_e4m3". Without that, vLLM rejects the backend right at selection time (chapter 30).
  2. Reinterprets without copying. _qsa_as_fp8 does cache.view(torch.float8_e4m3fn) over the uint8 allocation, a byte reinterpretation. If FP8 mode is off and the cache is quantized, it raises an error: it would rather fail loudly than return plausible garbage.
  3. Guarantees an always-readable scale. _qsa_scale_ptr hands the kernel a 0-d fp32 tensor. If the layer has no scale, it uses a 1.0 cached per device, so the launch signature is the same in both variants.
  4. Compiles two kernels from one source. KV_QUANT_MODE is a tl.constexpr: with 0, Triton eliminates the cast and scale branches at compile time and the BF16 path comes out identical to the original (the patch is “inert” in BF16).
  5. Applies the scale after the dot. In both kernels, the MQA block-selection scorer and the GQA split-K sparse attention (_qsa_sparse_paged_gqa_splitk_kernel), it does keys.to(query.dtype), scores *= k_scale after the tl.dot and normalized_output *= v_scale at the end.
  6. Disarms the guards. The assert now accepts K/V in e4m3 with Q in BF16. The inherited FlashAttentionImpl.__init__ rejects quantized KV because FlashAttention doesn't read it, so the backend hands it "auto" and restores the real dtype right afterwards. That's safe because these layers only go through forward_qsa, which uses the Triton kernels.

The vLLM 0.30 lane uses another version of the same idea: files/patch_qsa_fp8_kv_v030.py (581 lines), a backport of vllm#55557 that embeds the upstream diff along with a mini hunk applier. The scales become host floats and are folded into constants the kernel already had. softmax_scale = head_dim**-0.5 × k_scale absorbs \(s_k\) (converted to base 2, × 1.4427, in order to use exp2). output_scale = v_scale goes into each row's inverse normalizer, inv_normalizer = output_scale / max(normalizer, 1e-20), and each partition's LSE stays unscaled, so the split-K merge remains exact. The constexpr is called IS_FP8. K is converted to Q's dtype because, according to the comment itself, doing QK in FP8 was measured to be slower and less accurate. V goes to FP16 in the PV product: since \(p_j \le 1\) in the online softmax, FP16's short range is enough, and its 10 mantissa bits are more precise than BF16's 7. The file warns that it must be deleted once the image reaches vLLM 0.31, which already includes #55557.

What doesn't shrink: recurrent state and the 1.70×

If the main KV drops by half, why does the Qwen recipe gain 1.70× instead of 2×? Because not everything that occupies the pool is main KV. The 36 GDN layers carry a recurrent state per request (a 128×128 matrix per value head; chapter 14), and the QSA indexer and compressor stay in BF16. vLLM's hybrid allocator requires the same page size in bytes for every group, so it grows the attention block until the attention page catches up with the state page:

\[ B_{\text{attn}} = a \cdot \left\lceil \frac{P_{\text{GDN}}}{a \cdot b_{\text{tok}}} \right\rceil, \qquad P_{\text{GDN}} = \underbrace{24 \cdot 128 \cdot 128 \cdot b_{\text{ssm}}}_{\text{recurrent}} + \underbrace{61{,}440\ \text{B}}_{\text{conv}} \]
  • \(B_{\text{attn}}\): attention block size in tokens
  • \(b_{\text{tok}}\): KV bytes per token per layer: 1,024 in BF16 and 512 in FP8 (1 KV head per GPU × 256 dims × K and V)
  • \(a\): block alignment; the ceiling takes the result up to the next multiple of \(a\). With \(a\) = 16, as in chapter 14 (interpretation), the math reproduces the repo's blocks, and \(a\) = 64 would give the same values. Example: 1,634,304 / 1,024 = 1,596 → 1,600
  • \(P_{\text{GDN}}\): the page of one GDN layer in TP2: 24 heads per GPU × 128×128 of state + conv
  • \(b_{\text{ssm}}\): 4 B in FP32 (the checkpoint's dtype) or 2 B in BF16
  • the repo records, in TP2, 1,600 (README, BF16 KV) and 1,664 (patch_block_drop.py, FP8 KV with BF16 state). The 3,200 with FP8 KV and FP32 state is computed for TP2; in the repo it shows up in TP1 (.env.sample), where the page and the bytes per token double together

The figure is a balance scale. The left pan holds the GDN page of one layer, and the right pan stacks slices of KV tokens until it balances. Below are the tokens measured for each combination.

Do the math with the measured tokens per GiB: 67,229 in BF16 and 114,060 in FP8 give ≈15.97 KB and ≈9.41 KB per token per GPU. Subtract the pure main KV (13,312 and 6,656 B per GPU) and ~2.7 KB remain in both cases: that's the part that doesn't shrink (QSA indexer and compressor in BF16, amortized GDN state and PLE, page rounding; our derivation). So the ratio is \((13.3+2.7)/(6.7+2.7) \approx 1.70\). Quantizing the KV always yields less than the bit ratio, and the difference is everything that lives outside the main KV.

ParameterMAMBA_SSM_CACHE_DTYPE=bfloat16

The checkpoint asks for float32, but the fused GDN kernel accepts BF16 (FUSED_GDN_STATE_DTYPES). The underlying risk is precision: the state is rewritten at every token, and rounding to 7 mantissa bits at every step can accumulate error. That's why the state dtype is validated with needles and long reasoning, just like the KV.

It's an optimistic approximation: it assumes a 2× gain when the real one, as you just saw, is ~1.7×, so it overestimates the tokens that fit. When K decides who gets read

In dense attention, a small error in K changes the softmax weights a little, and the output changes smoothly. In sparse attention with an indexer (chapter 13) there is a hard threshold: the indexer gives each block or compressed entry a score, only the top-k enter attention and the rest is ignored entirely. A tiny error in a score near the cutoff is enough to flip an entire block to the other side:

\[ \hat I_j = I_j + \varepsilon_j, \qquad j \text{ flips sides when } \operatorname{sign}\big(\hat I_j - \hat I_{(k)}\big) \neq \operatorname{sign}\big(I_j - I_{(k)}\big) \]
  • \(I_j\): the indexer's score for candidate \(j\), with exact K
  • \(\varepsilon_j\): the error that quantizing K introduces into that score
  • \(I_{(k)}\): the k-th largest score, the top-k cutoff
  • the more candidates, the more scores squeezed near the cutoff

The figure is a traffic light. Each dot is a candidate, with the exact score on the horizontal axis and the score with quantized K on the vertical one. The vertical line is the exact cutoff and the horizontal one is the cutoff with quantized K. Green dots were chosen in both cases. Red ones were chosen and fell out; amber ones are intruders that took their place.

Notice three things. With FP8, swaps are rare and always between neighbors of the cutoff. With MXFP4 without rotation, the outlier channels stretch the block scale and the scores scatter around the diagonal: several swaps, and more of them as the number of candidates grows. This is what the DeepSeek repo calls selection drift at deep context. In MXFP4, which uses a per-block scale, the Hadamard rotation on q and k helps a little: it spreads the outlier channels before quantizing, and the score error drops (averaged over 8 queries with N = 2,048, from ~23% to ~19%, with swaps going from ~9 to ~7; see the “score error” readout). In per-tensor FP8, which is the figure's default, it doesn't help in this example: the error rises from ~2.6% to ~4.7%. E4M3 already has relative precision in every number, so the errors that are large in absolute value were in the outlier channels, where this synthetic query puts little weight. The rotation spreads those errors across all dimensions, including the ones the query weighs more heavily. Rotating is a tool for low-bit formats with a shared scale, not an automatic improvement. Even in MXFP4 it doesn't work miracles: 1 mantissa bit is still 1 bit. And it's only exact if you rotate both sides. Pick “k only” and watch the selection collapse. That's the care GLM's indexer has to take: it writes the keys already rotated by an FWHT-128 before FP8 and has to rotate the query too (without that, “the needle drops out of the top-k”). DeepSeek applies Hadamard before FP4 in the indexer (chapter 22).

The Qwen recipe documents this risk carefully. In the reference kernel the approach came from, a long-reasoning benchmark dropped from 6/6 to 2/6 with FP8 KV, and the patch warns: “quantising keys perturbs which blocks the indexer selects, not merely the attention output”. On the dual kit, bench/reasoning_check.py scored 12/12 with FP8 and with BF16, but the Sep 16 rerun showed fewer accepted tokens per step in 8 of 8 cells (interpretation: the target distribution shifted a little and the draft is right less often) and −5.3% in mean throughput (−1.4% on prose, −9.2% on code, with ms/step +11% at 8 streams). Since the QSA indexer cache stays in BF16, the inventory's interpretation is that the perturbation comes in indirectly: the FP8 attention output changes the hidden states that feed the indexers of the following layers. There's one caveat. The patch also makes the MQA block-selection scorer ready to read FP8 (_qsa_as_fp8(k_cache, …, "selector") and scores *= k_scale). If that call receives the quantized main cache, the perturbation is direct. The repo doesn't let us confirm which cache the selector receives, because the image's original file (the .orig) is not versioned.

Silent failure: wrong answers, not crashes

Bad KV quantization doesn't bring the server down. The model stays fluent; it just answers wrong more often. That's why the Qwen recipe created bench/reasoning_check.py: 10 deterministic tasks plus needles (--needles 8000,64000), temperature 0, an X/12 scoreboard, and --compare exits with a code ≠ 0 if there's a regression. It's the quality gate for any KV change (chapter 25). The recipe itself treats FP8 KV as “a capacity trade-off, not a free win” and asks you to revalidate on your own workload.

Measuring the cache apart from everything else

A measurement trap: whoever turns on FP8 “in the KV” often turns on FP8 somewhere else at the same time, and the final score mixes the two. Mia's recipe for Qwen3.8-27B on one Spark, on TensorFold (a 4-bit affine MLX checkpoint, patch 0003), kept them apart. The FP8 cache stores the keys and values of the 16 attention layers in E4M3, 1 byte per value and no scale (it saturates at ±448): 32 KiB per token instead of 64. PREFILL_FP8=1, on the other hand, runs the prompt pass in FP8, which is faster (+52% prefill in one of the recipe's tests, with the NVFP4 checkpoint and a 12.6k prompt). Quality was measured inside the engine on 8 sequences of 4,096 tokens (4 from the wikitext-2 test set and 4 of CPython source code), each in two chunks of 2,048 rows so the second one reads keys already stored, against the BF16 cache with a BF16 prompt:

The cache costs far less than the prompt precision: the FP8 prompt has ~14× the KL of the FP8 cache. If quality matters more than prefill speed, the first knob to turn is PREFILL_FP8=0, not the KV dtype. Notice also that perplexity barely moves with the cache (3.316 → 3.315), while KL and top-1 show the difference: perplexity is an average, and averages hide token swaps (chapter 25). The recipe's own caveats: only 4k contexts were scored by KL, and long context was checked with needles only (195k, and 491k with YaRN). On an H200 the method is the same: when evaluating FP8 KV in vLLM or TensorRT-LLM, measure the cache in isolation, with the rest of the path in high precision, before blaming or acquitting the format.

Comparing two formats on tasks calls for a paired test. The three-Spark GLM-5.3 recipe on TensorFold, a version well after the v1.3.2 studied in chapter 49, checked its 4-bit caches with McNemar's test. It runs the same items in both formats and looks only at the items where they disagree (how many only A got right and how many only B got right), because the items both get right or both get wrong say nothing about the difference. If the formats were equivalent, each disagreement would be a coin flip, and the exact test computes the probability \(p\) of a split at least as lopsided as the one observed.

The formats: fp8 stores the latent in E4M3 with one power-of-two scale per row (rotary and indexer keys in BF16); fp4 stores the latent in 4 bits, at 0.69× FP8's bytes per token; fp4x also puts the rotary keys and the indexer keys in E4M3 with a power-of-two scale, 31,476 versus 39,072 bytes per token per rank, ~24% more tokens per GiB. Fixed, seeded suite with checkable answers (tools/quality.py), greedy decoding, one boot per format. FP8 versus FP4: no measurable difference, and every ledger miss in both was a reply that ran past the token limit. FP4X versus FP4: not significant, but ledger tracking leans toward FP4 (FP4X ran past the limit 8 times, against 5, and gave one finished wrong answer), and long-context recall wasn't measured with it. The recipe keeps FP4 as the long-context default and FP4X as opt-in. Two readings of ours: with only 10 disagreements, the test has little power, and “not significant” doesn't mean “equal”; and FP4X is precisely the format that also quantizes the indexer keys, the fragile spot of this section. The paired test works for any server A/B with right-or-wrong items, on a Spark or on a cluster.

The packed MLA record: fp8_ds_mla in GLM

In MLA, the cache doesn't store K and V per head. It stores, per token and per layer, the compressed latent \(c^{KV}\) (kv_lora_rank = 512) and, when there is decoupled RoPE, a 64-dim positional part \(k^R\). It's a single “row” per token, as in MQA, and under TP it is replicated on every rank. The fp8_ds_mla layout of the DeepSeek-V3.2 family (also used by GLM-5.1 and Kimi K2.5) packs that row into 656 bytes: the 512-dim latent in FP8, 4 FP32 scales (one per 128 dims, 16 B) and the 64 RoPE dims in BF16 (128 B). The head has 576 dims (512 + 64) and the kernel block has 64 rows.

Why does the RoPE stay in BF16? The RoPE dims carry position as rotation angles, and getting them wrong means getting the distance between two tokens wrong, which is exactly what the model needs to get right at 1M of context (chapter 8). The design for DeepSeek's FP4 KV ranks RoPE in FP4 as the option with the “highest quality risk”, because it degrades positional precision at 1M.

What about Qwen? There, attention is GQA with partial RoPE (partial_rotary_factor 0.25: only 64 of the 256 dims rotate), and per-tensor FP8 quantizes all of K, rotary dims included, with scale 1.0. Even so it passed the gate: 12/12 on reasoning_check (needles up to ~104k tokens) and, on the 0.30 lane, needles 3/3 at 200k. The MLA layouts keep the RoPE in BF16 for two reasons (interpretation based on DeepSeek's design). It's a small, separate part of the row, 128 B, so protecting it is cheap. And the target is 1M of context, where a position error weighs more than at 262k. In FP8 the risk is small. It's at 4 bits that the RoPE becomes the first thing to protect.

GLM-5.3-Flash complicates the story by being NoPE (qk_rope_head_dim: 0, \(d_{qk}\) = 512). On SM12x, the only sparse-MLA backend is FLASHINFER_MLA_SPARSE_SM120, and its impl requires the packed fp8_ds_mla record. The stock image dies on the first forward with pe_dim must be 64 for fp8_ds_mla. FlashInfer instantiates only three geometries: DSV3_2 and GLM_NSA with \(d_{qk}\) = 576 and 656 B (top-k ∈ {128, 512, 1024, 2048}), and DSV4 with 512 dims and 584 B (top-k ≤ 1024). GLM-5.3 needs 512 dims with top-k 2048, a combination no kernel instantiates. The overlay's way out is to dress GLM up in the GLM_NSA geometry with zeros:

\[ q' = [\,q_{\text{NoPE}}\,;\ \mathbf{0}_{64}\,], \qquad k' = [\,c^{KV}\,;\ \mathbf{0}_{64}\,] \quad\Longrightarrow\quad q' \cdot k' = q_{\text{NoPE}} \cdot c^{KV} + \mathbf{0} \cdot \mathbf{0} = q_{\text{NoPE}} \cdot c^{KV} \]
  • \(q'\): the query with 64 zeros at the end (q = F.pad(q, (0, 64)), rope_pad = 64)
  • \(k'\): the stored record with k_pe zeroed
  • the QK product doesn't change, the softmax doesn't change, and V comes from the NoPE region (\(d_v\) = 512): the result is exact

The price is space: 656 B per token per layer instead of the ~528 actually needed (512 + 16), ≈24% more KV in the target's 11 DSA layers. In the figure you assemble each layout byte by byte, with 48 (wide screens) or 32 (narrow screens) bytes per grid row, and compare the row sizes.

Compare the “GLM” and “GLM without padding” rows: the 128 hatched bytes are the exact price of fitting into a kernel that already exists, without writing any kernel at all. It's a typical recipe decision, trading bytes for not having kernel work to do, and it's safe because the math guarantees exactness. MLAAttentionSpec.real_page_size_bytes already allocates block_size × 656 for fp8_ds_mla in models that aren't deepseek_v4, so the allocation matches without further patches.

The other lane: 528 bytes and one scale per row

The GLM recipe now has a second version : the same checkpoint and the same two Sparks, but a different inference engine, TensorFold v0.6.0 with 53 patches (the engine is covered in chapter 48; the recipe, in chapter 49). TensorFold is a per-family engine: GLM brings its own attention kernels, so there is no foreign geometry to dress up in. The cache stores exactly the 512-dim NoPE latent, with no RoPE to fill in, which comes to 1,024 B per token and layer in BF16. Patch 0038 (glm-kv-fp8) takes that row to FP8 in the kv8 format: 512 E4M3 codes, one FP32 scale and 12 zero bytes that align the row to 16 B, for a total of 528 B. That is the size of the “GLM without padding” row in the figure above, only with one scale instead of four (pick “GLM · TensorFold kv8” in the selector). And the scale is always a power of two:

\[ s = 2^{\lceil \log_2 \text{amax} \rceil - 8}, \qquad \frac{\text{amax}}{s} \in (128,\ 256], \qquad c_i = \operatorname{round}_{\text{E4M3}}\!\left(\frac{x_i}{s}\right) \]
  • \(\text{amax}\): the largest \(|x_i|\) among the row's 512 numbers (one token, one layer)
  • \(8\): the code's SHIFT. The largest value lands between 128 and 256, below E4M3's maximum of 448, so nothing saturates
  • \(s\): stored as FP32 (4 B), but always of the form \(2^k\), with the biased exponent clamped between 1 and 254
  • compare with DeepSeek's UE8M0 in section 2: \(\lceil \log_2(\text{amax}/448) \rceil\) puts the amax in \((224,\ 448]\). The kv8 scale equals DeepSeek's or is twice it, so there is almost always one extra bit of headroom at the top

A single scale per row, rounded to a power of two on top of that, looks coarse next to the four FP32 scales of fp8_ds_mla. The comment in the patch's code explains why it isn't: in a floating-point format, relative precision does not depend on the scale. Inside E4M3's normal range (from \(2^{-6}\) to 448, almost 15 octaves), every number keeps the same 3 mantissa bits wherever it lands. A finer or exact scale only changes where the numbers land on that ruler. The power of two loses only what it pushes below \(2^{-6}\), that is, values under ~\(2^{-14}\) of the row's largest. On GLM's real rows (the output of kv_a and kv_a_layernorm, with amax ~3–5× the RMS), FP32 scales per token, per 128-block and fixed per layer all came within 0.001 of the power of two's 2.65% relative error per row, according to the measurement recorded in the code itself. Compare with FP4 in section 2 (interpretation): E2M1 has only ~2.6 octaves of normal range (from 1 to 6), which is why there the per-block scale decides almost everything.

In the kernel, the per-row scale repeats the trick from section 3, now at token granularity. A scale that covers the whole row of token \(j\) comes out of its product with the query. And since in GLM's MLA the latent is K and V at the same time (chapter 12), the same scale comes back in the sum of the values:

\[ \text{score}_j \propto \tilde q \cdot (s_j\, \tilde c_j) = s_j\,(\tilde q \cdot \tilde c_j), \qquad o = \sum_j p_j\,(s_j\, \tilde c_j) = \sum_j (p_j\, s_j)\, \tilde c_j \]
  • \(\tilde c_j\): the 512 codes of token \(j\) converted to BF16 (an exact conversion)
  • \(s_j\): the power of two of row \(j\)
  • \(\tilde q\): the absorbed query of one head (chapter 12)
  • \(p_j\): the attention weight after the softmax
  • multiplying by \(2^k\) only touches the exponent: in FP32 nothing is rounded, barring underflow or overflow

The readers do scores *= ks before the softmax and p * ks before the product with the latent, in FP32. With the four scales per row of fp8_ds_mla this would not work: they change inside the 512-term product and require per-block partials (the third mode of the figure in section 3). The design has one more consequence, which the docstring sums up as lossy (the owner's call), never inexact. Lossy, because FP8 is not BF16, and accepting that is the recipe owner's decision. But never inexact: a single helper (store_row) quantizes both the prefill rows and the decode-window rows, so a row's bytes depend only on the BF16 row that produced them, and the multiplication by the scale is exact. Whatever path writes a token (fresh prompt, resumed prompt, a round with drafts), the bytes are the same. That is why the engine's contract (a reply with drafts equals serial decoding, a resumed prompt equals the prompt computed from scratch) still holds with the KV in FP8 (chapter 48).

The engine's default with the patch is bf16, which is exact. The recipe picks fp8 so that the whole native window fits. With KV=bf16 and DFlash2, the window per request drops to 196,608 tokens (163,840 with vision and dense weights in fp8 or bf16). With KV=fp8, each of the 4 concurrent requests can grow to 1,048,576 tokens, and the four share a pool of 2,922,496 tokens at the measured startup: two 1M conversations fit together, four don't. The pool also holds the conversations' kept prompts and varies from ~2.1 to ~2.9M depending on the free memory at startup. The vLLM lane, with its fixed 11 GiB pool, reports 1,233,779 tokens and uses an 850k context per request (the TP2 default). This is not a controlled A/B: the two engines differ in almost everything besides the KV, so don't read the difference as an effect of the layout alone. Quality published in the v1.3.2 README, with the TR3 checkpoint of the time: GSM8K 98.0% (250 problems), HumanEval 97.6% (164) and the needle found in a 981,841-token prompt. These were measured with FP8 KV and 4-bit dense weights together, so they don't isolate the cost of the KV. Another run, quoted in the recipe's config.sh (one boot per setting), gives 95.1% on HumanEval with the same 4-bit weights; they are different builds, so treat the gap as run-to-run variation. FP8 requires the latent cache (TF_GLM_LATENT=1, the default), and DENSE=bf16 KV=bf16 KDA_CHUNKED=0 serves the checkpoint as is, with a smaller window.

GLM · vLLM: dressing up the latent

656 B per row: 4 FP32 scales (one per 128 dims) and 128 B of zeros in place of the RoPE. The reader is FlashInfer's sparse-MLA kernel that already exists on SM12x, with no kernel to write. The price is 24% more bytes and a geometry decided by another model.

GLM · TensorFold: a tailor-made layout

528 B per row: 1 power-of-two scale and 12 B of alignment. The scale comes out of the product exactly, and the bytes don't depend on the path that wrote the row. The price is the family's own kernels, which only this engine has. kv8 is one of the 53 patches the recipe applies on top of a fixed TensorFold version and has to rebase on every release.

DeepSeek-V4: 584 bytes, UE8M0 and a misleading name

DeepSeek-V4's MLA goes further: K and V are the same vector of 512 dims (448 without position + 64 with RoPE, in the last 64 dims, interleaved), and there is no per-head reconstruction. The row for each entry (raw in the SWA window or compressed in the C4/C128 layers) has 584 bytes: 448 B with the NoPE dims in FP8 E4M3, in 7 blocks of 64; 128 B with the 64 RoPE dims in BF16; and an 8 B footer with the 7 UE8M0 scales and 1 padding byte. Compared to V3.2 (656 B), the gain comes from the 1-byte scales and from having only 448 dims in FP8.

The kernel that writes this row, quantize_and_insert_k_kernel (overlay common/ops/cache_utils.py), applies exactly the UE8M0 equation from section 2 to each 64-dim block: scale = 2^ceil(log2(max(|x|, 1e-4)/448)), writes x/scale in FP8 (with clamp) and stores the exponent + 127 in a byte. The 64 RoPE dims are copied in BF16. Inside a page block, the data of all tokens comes first, \([0,\ bs \times 576)\), followed by the scales of all of them, \([bs \times 576,\ bs \times 576 + bs \times 8)\). In prefill, the way back is dequantize_and_gather_k_cache: it gathers the pages scattered across the block table and dequantizes them into contiguous BF16 (CuTeDSL when available, otherwise Triton, with 128 workers per request). The same file fixes integer overflows that only show up at 1M: block_idx × block_stride exceeds \(2^{31}\) with ≥57K blocks, and the pointer arithmetic switched to int64.

QAT: serving in FP8 is the training regime

DeepSeek-V4 was trained with simulated quantization (QDQ) in FP8 on the 448 NoPE dims of the KV, in blocks of 64 with a power-of-2 scale, and in FP4 in the indexer after Hadamard. Storing the KV this way is not an extra approximation imposed by the recipe: the model learned to live with that rounding. It's the difference between PTQ and QAT from chapter 20. The VLLM_DSPARK_REFERENCE_KV_QUANT_DEQUANT knob (default 0) reproduces that QDQ on the NoPE dims of the DSpark drafter's internal KV, to check numerical parity against the reference.

Now, the name. The recipe passes --kv-cache-dtype nvfp4_ds_mla, but in this image the record is byte-for-byte identical to fp8_ds_mla: the same 584 B. What changes is the kernel dispatch and the spec's alignment (584 for nvfp4 versus 576 for fp8). In flashmla_sparse.py there was a string comparison:

# stock (≈ line 880)
use_fp8_cache = self.kv_cache_dtype == "fp8_ds_mla"
# hotfix #22 (patches/hotfix-nvfp4-ds-mla-issue22.sh)
use_fp8_cache = self.kv_cache_dtype in ("fp8_ds_mla", "nvfp4_ds_mla")

With the name nvfp4_ds_mla, the test returned False and attention fell into _forward_bf16_kv, a slow BF16 path, instead of _forward_fp8_kv. At short context nobody noticed (~66 tok/s for both). At 600K+ tokens of context, decode collapsed to ~1 tok/s. In the figure, the request reaches the switch and the string decides the track.

The lesson generalizes: layout and dispatch are separate things. vLLM picks the kernel by the dtype's name, and two names with the same bytes can land on different kernels. When a recipe renames a dtype, the first test is long context, because that's where a slow path shows itself.

Parameter--kv-cache-dtype nvfp4_ds_mla · --block-size 256

Fixed in the compose file. It selects the “Stage C padded NVFP4” path: the 584 B envelope, not the “true” 416 B layout that was abandoned (section 9). Since the bytes are those of fp8_ds_mla, “there is no ‘FP4 KV’ quality risk, and there is no memory gain either” (report fable5-1, §3.8). --block-size 256 makes a C4 page hold 256/4 = 64 rows × 584 B = 37,376 B, equal to the SWA page (64 tokens × 584 B).

The indexer's K cache and MXFP4

There's a second KV hidden inside sparse attention: the keys of the indexer. In DeepSeek-V4, in the 21 C4A layers, the lightning indexer (64 heads × index_head_dim 128) has its own compressor that writes one key every 4 tokens. This indexer K cache takes 132 B per compressed entry: 128 B in FP8 plus 4 B of scale, a composition inferred by analogy with V3.2. Since the indexer is ReplicatedLinear (“no tensor parallel, just replicated”), each rank stores the entire cache: ≈1.6 GB (1.5 GiB) per rank, in our own calculation over the allocator's 2,331,430 tokens (2,331,430 / 4 × 21 × 132 B), which are an accounting figure, not a physical cost. GLM has the same piece: 132 B per pool of 4 tokens, or 33 B/token/layer, with FWHT-128 before FP8.

The problem shows up at read time. To choose the top-512, the indexer has to score all compressed entries for every query token. The figure computes the bytes read per query token in DeepSeek-V4, with the inventory's formulas: C4A reads 512 chosen entries + 128 from the window; C128A reads everything compressed 128:1 + the window; the indexer scans \(N/4\) keys across 21 layers.

At very long context, what dominates the attention bytes is the indexer, not attention (and it also dominates the prefill FLOPs: ≈80 TFLOP per 1024-token chunk at 900K, duplicated on both GPUs). That points to a quantization unlike anything we've seen so far: quantizing only the selection keys. vLLM already shipped the full path, with AttentionConfig.use_fp4_indexer_cache, the Triton FP4 insert and the DeepGEMM fp8_fp4_*_mqa_logits kernels that read K in MXFP4 (e2m1 values with a UE8M0 scale per 32). But the builder locked it with assert is_device_capability_family(100), “requires Blackwell datacenter GPUs”, and the GB10 belongs to family 120.

GLM's indexer in TensorFold

In the TensorFold lane, the indexer's pooled keys (one per pool of 4 tokens) use the same kv8 format as in section 6: 128 E4M3 codes plus 16 B of power-of-two scale and alignment, or 144 B per pool (36 B/token/layer), a bit more than the vLLM lane's 132 B. Scoring runs on the tensor cores directly over the codes, with each pool's scale applied to the products. The patch also makes a saving that isn't about bits. The indexer's per-token keys and gates are only read while the pool of 4 that the token falls into is being completed. So instead of context-sized planes, each stream keeps those rows in a small BF16 ring, slightly larger than the widest window of rows processed at once, and a kept prompt carries along only the tail of the incomplete pool. It isn't quantization, but it answers the same question as the end of the chapter, “what grows with the context?”: short-lived data doesn't need space proportional to it.

The uniform page and the design of a true FP4 KV

If nvfp4_ds_mla is FP8, why hasn't anyone built a true 4-bit KV? The answer lies in the allocator. In DeepSeek-V4 the boot shows four KV groups: 1× MLAAttentionSpec (block 256) and 3× SlidingWindowMLASpec (blocks 64, 4 and 8; the design document attributes the 4 and 8 ones to the drafter). The uniform page rule requires all of them to have the same page size in bytes, currently 64 rows × 584 B = 37,376 B. Recall the reason from chapter 14: pages come out of a single pool, and any free page can serve any group, so they all need to be the same size. The SWA rows are written by a C++ op (fused_deepseek_v4_qnorm_rope_kv_rope_quant_insert) that can't change without recompiling vLLM's _C, so they stay at 584 B. The design document states that a smaller compressed row is only cheap for the allocator if a whole number of them, and a power of 2, fits in the same page: 37,376 = 584 × 64 = 292 × 128 = 146 × 256. The source doesn't explain why it has to be a power of 2. One interpretation: the row count defines the layer's block in tokens (rows × 4 in C4), and that block has to match the kernel block and the other groups' blocks (256, 64, 4 and 8 tokens), which powers of 2 do naturally.

With that, the story of the recipe's attempts becomes clear:

  1. Stage A (recipe/nvfp4/Dockerfile.stage-a). This is the plumbing: it registers "nvfp4_ds_mla" in CacheDType, maps it to torch.uint8, wires get_kv_quant_mode → KVQuantMode.NVFP4 and sizes the page as storage_block_size × 416. A dtype has to exist in the engine before it has a layout.
  2. Stage B (recipe/nvfp4/Dockerfile.stage-b). A probe with the 416 B NVFP4 layout inherited from GLM-5.2's sparse-MLA path (interpretation of the arithmetic: 256 B E2M1 + 32 B of E4M3 scales, one per 16, + 128 B of BF16 RoPE). It sets head_bytes = 416, relaxes the “fp8 only” assert, converts nvfp4 → nvfp4_ds_mla and defines get_kv_cache_shape → (blocks, block_size, 416). It broke the hybrid MLA/SWA grouping and was abandoned.
  3. Stage C (recipe/nvfp4/Dockerfile.stage-c). It goes back to the proven 584 B envelope (real_page_size = storage_block_size × 584 when model_version == "deepseek_v4") “so that the hybrid MLA/SWA grouping proceeds”. It's what runs today (lane B, applied by build-dspark-vllm-runtime.sh), and the official-main lane does the same on vLLM main (nvfp4 + MLA becomes nvfp4_ds_mla instead of an error; a sanity test with shape (3, 256, 584)).
  4. gb10 variant (Dockerfile.gb10-dsv4-dspark). Experimental, on top of a vLLM 0.26 image (mia-vllm-gb10-linear-b12x), with a 352 B row: 256 B E2M1 NoPE + 32 B of E4M3 scales, one per 16, + 64 B of RoPE in FP8 (kStride = 352). It's not the default path and it contradicts the 584 B one: two different layouts with the same name in the same repo, in different images.

The recommendation is to prototype option A, starting with an offline numerical check (the reference knob from section 7 was made for that). The estimated effort is 2–3 weeks of engineering, with a new dtype (nvfp4c_ds_mla) so as not to change the meaning of nvfp4_ds_mla or break #22. The fable5-1 report estimates it differently, with an NVFP4 row of ~390 B, and arrives at “+50% KV tokens”. Today, though, DeepSeek's NVFP4 KV is the FP8 envelope.

The accounting of 1 million tokens

Now put it all together in a single calculation. In DeepSeek-V4, the layers compress the KV along the sequence (compress_ratios): 2 window-only layers, 21 C4A layers (1 entry every 4 tokens) and 20 C128A layers (1 every 128), all with a window of 128 raw tokens (CSA/HCA). The cost that grows with context is:

\[ \frac{\text{bytes}}{\text{token}} = \underbrace{21 \cdot \frac{584}{4}}_{3{,}066\ \text{(C4A)}} + \underbrace{20 \cdot \frac{584}{128}}_{91\ \text{(C128A)}} + \underbrace{21 \cdot \frac{132}{4}}_{693\ \text{(indexer)}} \approx 3.85\ \text{KB}, \qquad + \underbrace{46 \cdot 128 \cdot 584}_{\approx 3.4\ \text{MB per sequence (SWA)}} \]
  • 584: the FP8 row from section 7; 132: the indexer entry
  • /4 and /128: the compression ratios
  • 46 windowed layers (43 from the target + 3 from the drafter, per the design document's math) × 128 rows: a constant cost per sequence
  • everything replicated on both TP2 ranks (there is a single KV “head”, nothing to shard)

At 1M tokens that comes to ≈4.04 GB per sequence. Without compression (43 layers × 584 B) it would be 25.1 KB/token, and with a typical GQA-8 BF16, 176 KB/token (~185 GB at 1M). The boot, however, announces 2,331,430 tokens in 17.04 GiB, ≈7.8 KB/token, twice the physical cost. The difference is the allocator's accounting: four groups with same-size pages, added together. That's why the “maximum concurrency 2.22×” (2,331,430 / 1,048,576) is a conservative quota. The number says “~2 full 1M requests fit”, and that's the number vLLM uses to admit requests, even though the physical cost would accommodate ~4. A smaller compressed row moves capacity more than linearly. In the calculator, compare the three recipes. There, 1M = 1,048,576 tokens (2²⁰), the same convention as the 2.22×.

Notice how the three recipes reach similar numbers by different paths. GLM spends 11 × (656 + 33) = 7,579 B/token per rank. That's 7.06 GiB per 10⁶ tokens, the number that matches the README's “~7.1 GiB per 1M”, or 7.40 GiB per 2²⁰ tokens, which is what the figure shows. The README also adds ~7.4 GiB of fixed memory; its composition (KDA state, drafter window, etc.) is the inventory's interpretation, and the figure doesn't model it. The fixed 11 GiB pool gives 1,233,779 tokens (1.45× at 850k) with compact drafter pages. In the vLLM lane at TP2 the configured context is 850k (MAX_MODEL_LEN = 850,000), so 1M is not accepted. The lane of the same model spends, by the estimate in section 6, 11 × (528 + 36) = 6,204 B/token per rank, ≈6.1 GiB per 2²⁰ tokens, and accepts the whole native window in each request; the 4 requests share a pool of 2,922,496 tokens (for example, two 1M conversations, or one of 1M next to three of ~640k, according to the README). Qwen spends ~9.4 KB/token/GPU measured and has very few layers with KV. DeepSeek spends ~3.85 KB thanks to compression. In all three, quantizing the KV multiplies by 1.7–2× a much larger architectural saving: in DeepSeek, compression and MLA bring the KV down to ~2% of a GQA-8 BF16.

A caveat about DeepSeek's drafter. DSpark's main_kv_cache is its own buffer, outside the vLLM pool: it doesn't register KV layers. The drafter's 3 windows counted in the equation and the SWA groups with blocks of 4 and 8, on the other hand, follow the design document's accounting. The repo doesn't reconcile the two descriptions, so read the ~3.4 MB per sequence as the design's calculation, not as a measurement.

For a new recipe, the sequence of questions that comes out of this chapter is:

  1. Who reads the KV on your GPU? Find the attention backend that vLLM picks for the architecture and its supported_kv_cache_dtypes list. A dtype without a kernel doesn't exist, and a dtype with a slow kernel is worse than BF16. In a per-family engine like TensorFold, the answer can be “a kernel you write yourself”: then the layout is yours (528 B instead of 656), at the price of maintaining that kernel.
  2. What's the byte layout? Add up values, scales, BF16 parts and padding. Check whether the row divides the hybrid allocator's page and whether it matches the other groups.
  3. Where do the scales come from? Calibrated in the checkpoint, 1.0 for lack of them, or dynamic per block. A 4-bit format requires blocks.
  4. What doesn't shrink? Recurrent state, indexers, drafters and rounded pages define the real gain (1.70× and not 2×).
  5. Does the dispatch check out? Look for string comparisons on the dtype name and measure speed at long context, where a slow path gives itself away.
  6. Does selection survive? In sparse attention, run needles, long reasoning and the recipe's gate (reasoning_check, ruler-lite), because the symptom is a wrong answer, not a crash.
