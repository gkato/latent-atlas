A 4-bit floating-point number can only say fifteen things: \(0, \pm 0.5, \pm 1, \pm 1.5, \pm 2, \pm 3, \pm 4, \pm 6\). Even so, Qwen3.8-Flash-Next uses those fifteen values to store 96% of the parameters of its main model (120.8 of 125.7 billion; the N-gram/PLE table, with another 51.2 billion, stays in FP8), and DeepSeek-V4-Flash stores the 256 experts of every layer the same way. The trick is not in the 4 bits. It is in the scales: who shares the same scale, how many bits it is stored in and how the hardware consumes it. In this chapter you will open up minifloats bit by bit (FP8 E4M3 and E5M2, FP4 E2M1), see how a per-tensor, per-channel or 128×128 per-block scale changes the error, understand why MXFP4 and NVFP4, both "4 bits", are not the same thing, and find out what happens when a kernel looks at the shape of a matrix and says no.

Minifloats: 8-bit floating point from the inside

In the chapter Numbers in the computer you saw the anatomy of a float: a sign bit \(S\), an exponent \(E\) stored with an offset (the bias) and a mantissa \(M\) that carries the digits. In the previous chapter you saw integer quantization: a ruler with equal steps, stretched by a scale. Here we put the two ideas together. A minifloat is a float with very few bits (8, 6 or 4), and its ruler does not have equal steps. Values are dense near zero and more spread out far from it, and the step doubles at every power of 2.

\[ \begin{gathered} x = (-1)^{S}\cdot 2^{\,E-b}\cdot\Big(1+\frac{M}{2^{m}}\Big)\quad (E>0) \\[4pt] x = (-1)^{S}\cdot 2^{\,1-b}\cdot\frac{M}{2^{m}}\quad (E=0,\ \text{subnormal}) \end{gathered} \]
  • \(S\): sign bit (0 = positive, 1 = negative)
  • \(E\): exponent field with \(e\) bits, read as an unsigned integer; \(b\) is the bias (7 in E4M3, 15 in E5M2, 1 in E2M1)
  • \(M\): mantissa field with \(m\) bits; the "1 +" is the implicit bit of normal numbers
  • \(E=0\): subnormals, which fill the gap between the smallest normal and zero with uniform steps

Why does this ruler suit neural networks? Because a float's rounding error is relative. Each range \([2^k, 2^{k+1})\), called a binade, holds the same \(2^m\) values, so the maximum error is always about \(2^{-(m+1)}\) of the number itself. On the integer ruler the error is absolute and constant: great for large numbers, bad for small ones. LLM weights and activations have long tails (lots of small values and a few huge ones), and that is why the float's logarithmic ruler usually wastes fewer bits.

E4M3 and E5M2: range versus precision

With 8 bits there are two popular splits. FP8 E4M3 uses 4 exponent bits and 3 mantissa bits (bias 7). FP8 E5M2 uses 5 and 2 (bias 15). One more exponent bit doubles the number of binades, that is, the range. One more mantissa bit halves the relative error, that is, the precision. The E4M3 used in inference is the float8_e4m3fn variant. The "fn" stands for finite: the format has no ±∞ and reserves a single pattern for NaN (S.1111.111). The other codes with exponent 1111 are still normal numbers. That is why the maximum is \(1.75\times 2^{8}=448\), and not the 240 of an IEEE-style E4M3.

Notice three things in the figure. First, the E5M2 ruler is almost twice as long as the E4M3 one (about 32 binades versus 18), but it has half the ticks per binade. Second, E2M1 is a little stub of just over 3 binades, and we will come back to it in section 4. Third, click π and compare: in E4M3 it becomes 3.25 (0.1000.101, or \(2^{8-7}\times(1+5/8)\)), in E5M2 it becomes 3.0 (0.10000.10), in E2M1 it becomes 3, and in BF16 it becomes 3.140625.

Deep dive: why 448, and why the upcast is exact

In E4M3 fn the largest usable exponent is \(E=15\), or \(2^{15-7}=2^8\). The largest allowed mantissa is 110 (1.75), because 111 with that exponent is NaN. That gives \(1.75\times256=448\). Another property is worth its weight in gold: every E4M3 fits exactly in BF16 and in FP16 (4 significant bits, exponent from −9 to 8). A kernel can read FP8 bytes and do the math in 16 bits without any extra conversion error. Qwen's FP8 KV patch relies on this ("e4m3 → Q dtype is exact"), and GLM uses the same argument to prove that its BF16 copy of an FP8 matrix has only one rounding. That is the subject of the chapters Quantized KV cache and Kernels.

In the recipes

E4M3 shows up everywhere: GLM's dense weights, Qwen's MTP experts and FP8-dense, DeepSeek's linears and KV, the NVFP4 scales. The constant FP8_MAX = 448.0 is written, under that name, in Qwen's FP8-dense converter (make_fp8_dense_checkpoint.py:30) and in DeepSeek's KV quantization (cache_utils.py:174). GLM uses the same number in amax/448. E5M2 only appears as the fp8_e5m2 option in vLLM's list of KV dtypes, and no recipe uses it. In the training tradition it is used for gradients, where range matters more than precision.

FP8 scales: per tensor, per channel, per token and per 128×128 block

A format on its own is a fixed-size ruler, and the weights of a layer rarely fall in the right range. The fix is the same as in integer quantization: divide by a scale before rounding and multiply back afterwards. With the group's amax (largest absolute value), the scale takes the largest element exactly to the top of the ruler:

\[ s = \frac{\max_i |x_i|}{448}, \qquad \hat{x}_i = \mathrm{E4M3}\!\Big(\frac{x_i}{s}\Big)\cdot s \]
  • \(x_i\): the real values of the group that shares the scale
  • \(s\): the scale (FP32 or BF16), stored alongside the FP8 bytes
  • \(\mathrm{E4M3}(\cdot)\): rounding to the nearest E4M3 value (ties to even), saturating at ±448
  • \(\hat{x}_i\): the dequantized value, which is what the computation actually uses

The engineering question is who shares the same scale, and that is the granularity topic you already saw with integers. With FP8, five variants appear in the recipes:

per tensor
one scalar for the whole matrix. Qwen's KV in the QSA layers; the 51 GB PLE table of the nvidia checkpoint ("FP8 shards + one global weight_scale").
per channel
one scale per output row of the weight (amax(dim=1) in the [out, in] layout). This is GLM's dense FP8 and Qwen's FP8-dense.
per token
one scale per row of the activation, computed at runtime. This is the "A8" half of Qwen's FP8_PER_CHANNEL_PER_TOKEN.
per 1D block
one scale every 128 values along K, for activations. This is DeepSeek's act_quant(x, 128) before every FP8 GEMM.
per 2D block
one scale per 128×128 tile of the weight: DeepSeek's linears and Qwen's MTP experts (FP8 with block scales).

The experiment shows the central lesson of the chapter. The fewer binades a format has, the finer the scale needs to be. E4M3 covers about 18 binades, so you can live with one scale per tensor (Qwen's KV does this) or per channel. E2M1 covers only 3.6 binades, so it needs a scale every 16 or 32 values. That is why every 4-bit format in this chapter is a microscaling format.

The cost of scales: effective bits

Every scale also takes up memory and bandwidth. The effective bits per weight are \(b_{\text{elem}} + b_{\text{scale}}/g\), with \(g\) elements per scale. Per channel, with K = 4096 columns: \(8 + 16/4096 \approx 8.004\) (GLM's BF16 scale) or \(8 + 32/4096\approx 8.008\) (Qwen's FP32 scale). Per 128×128 block: \(8 + 32/16384 \approx 8.002\). In FP8 the scales cost almost nothing. In FP4, as you will see, they cost from ≈6% (MXFP4: 0.25 of 4.25 bits) to ≈11% (NVFP4: 0.5 of 4.5 bits) of the bytes.

A GEMM with block scales

The math of a quantized GEMM is done by the tensor core: the GPU unit that multiplies a whole block of matrices in a single instruction (an MMA, for matrix multiply-accumulate), in low-precision formats (FP16/BF16, FP8, FP4), and accumulates the result in FP32. It knows nothing about per-group scales: it takes codes and returns sums. The chapter Inside the GPU opens up that box. What matters here is a simpler question. Why a 128×128 2D block on the weight and a 1D block of 128 on the activation? Because the scales can be pulled out of the GEMM's inner sum when both sides share the same cuts along K:

\[ \begin{gathered} y_{mn} = \sum_{k} x_{mk}\,w_{kn} \\[2pt] \approx\; \sum_{\kappa} \underbrace{s^{x}_{m\kappa}\; s^{w}_{\kappa\nu(n)}}_{\text{2 scalars}} \;\sum_{k\in\kappa} \hat{q}^{x}_{mk}\,\hat{q}^{w}_{kn} \end{gathered} \]
  • \(\kappa\): a slice of 128 consecutive positions of K
  • \(s^{x}_{m\kappa}\): the activation scale of row \(m\) in that slice (a 1×128 block, computed at runtime)
  • \(s^{w}_{\kappa\nu}\): the scale of the 128×128 weight tile that contains column \(n\) (\(\nu(n)=\lfloor n/128\rfloor\))
  • \(\hat{q}\): the FP8 codes. The 128-term inner product runs on the FP8 tensor core and is multiplied by the two scales before the next slice is accumulated

Read the equation from right to left. Inside a slice \(\kappa\), all 128 terms have the same activation scale (row \(m\) in that 1×128 block) and the same weight scale (the 128×128 tile that contains column \(n\)). That is why the two scales can be pulled out of the sum: the tensor core adds up 128 products of FP8 codes, and only the result of that sum is multiplied by \(s^x\cdot s^w\) (two scalars) before it goes into the FP32 accumulator. If the activation block and the weight tile did not cut K at the same places, the factorization would not exist and the scale would have to be applied term by term. The figure shows the computation happening, one slice at a time.

This is the scheme DeepSeek-V3 popularized, with the weight in 128×128 tiles and the activation in 1×128 blocks (DeepSeek-V4's act_quant(x, 128), which shows up in the checkpoint as weight_block_size = [128, 128] and weight_scale_inv scales). The price of the factorization is geometric: every rank must see dimensions that are multiples of 128 after the tensor parallel split. That requirement comes back in section 7, where it explains why Qwen turns on expert parallel.

Why quantize the dense part, if the experts are almost everything?

Here lies the argument that decides what to quantize in a new recipe, and it is not obvious. In an MoE, the experts dominate memory: in Qwen they are 96% of the main model's parameters and 63.28 GiB in NVFP4. But stored memory is not what limits decode. In decode, the time of a step is almost entirely the time to read from memory the weights the step uses (the memory-bound regime from the chapter Prefill, decode and KV). And each token reads only the experts the router picked, while every dense projection is read in full, at every step. Qwen's PLE table is the extreme case: 39% of the stored bytes, and each token fetches only 16 of its rows.

The Qwen recipe report does the math per GPU (TP2 with EP2, one request, MTP with 3 drafts). One step reads ≈ 9.2 GiB: the lm_head [248,320 × 2560] 4 times (once in the verification step and three times in the drafts), 2.36 GiB or 26%; the routed experts, ~2.1 GiB or 23% (about 32 unique experts per layer in the verification step); the GDN projections, 1.95 GiB; the HyperConnection, 1.19 GiB (replicated on both GPUs); attention with the indexer, 0.60 GiB. Added up, the BF16 dense parts are ~6.9 of the 9.2 GiB. At 235–245 GB/s effective, that is 40–42 ms per step, exactly what the recipe measures. In FP8 the dense parts drop to ~3.45 GiB and the step to ≈ 5.7 GiB, or ~25 ms. That math is where the "~1.5× at batch 1" of FP8_DENSE comes from. In GLM the logic is the same, with different weights: see the figure.

The general lesson holds for any recipe: add up the bytes read per step, not the bytes stored. Quantizing the experts solves memory (it is what makes the model fit on two nodes). Quantizing the dense part solves decode, because that is what is read in full at every step. In GLM the math is different, because DFlash2 verifies 8 tokens with top-8 and touches ~57 of the 288 experts per layer: the experts add up to ~15 GB read per rank per step, and the BF16 dense parts ~8 GB. Even so, converting KDA and the dense MLPs to FP8 cuts ~2.6 GB, ≈ 11 ms at 230 GB/s, the same order as the measured −11 ms (the calculation is the inventory's own, and it is flagged as such).

Context. The GLM recipe gained a second version: the same checkpoint, on the same two DGX Sparks, served by the TensorFold v0.6.0 engine with 53 patches instead of vLLM (chapters An exact engine and Swapping the engine). What it does. With DENSE=fp8, each matrix is quantized once, at load time, without calibration, with one FP32 scale per row and 128-column block: \(s = \mathrm{amax}/448\) over the 128 values, float8_e4m3fn codes rounded to nearest, and scales shaped [n, k/128]. The kernel. Compared with the vLLM recipe. GLM53_DENSE_FP8 uses one BF16 scale per output channel, in the format Marlin consumes: \(8 + 16/4096 \approx 8.004\) bits per weight. The 1×128 block with an FP32 scale costs \(8 + 32/128 = 8.25\) bits, the same as an MXFP8: about 3% more bytes read, in exchange for an outlier only affecting its 128 K-neighbors in the same row. By the lesson of the granularity figure, in E4M3 this error difference is small (interpretation). That number does not compare directly with the −11 ms per step of GLM53_DENSE_FP8: it is a different engine, a different set of matrices (here the head is included too) and a different measurement, not a controlled A/B.

Why it is not the default. Scales that are only an exponent: E8M0, UE8M0 and rounding

So far the scale has been "any" number in FP32 or BF16. There is a radical alternative: store only the exponent. The E8M0 format has 8 exponent bits, no mantissa and no sign. The byte \(e\) is worth \(2^{\,e-127}\), and 0xFF is NaN. "UE8M0" is the same format, with the U for unsigned to make it clear there is no sign. Multiplying by \(2^k\) means adding \(k\) to the exponent: the operation is exact and costs almost nothing. That is why microscaling hardware adopts this kind of scale.

Analogy

An FP32 scale is a continuous zoom: it frames the block exactly. An E8M0 scale is a zoom that only moves in 2× clicks. Sometimes the framing is snug, sometimes half the screen is left empty, and sometimes the block does not fit and the edges get cropped. It all depends on which way you round the click.

With a power-of-2 scale, rounding the exponent is a design decision. The recipes show two opposite rules:

\[ \begin{gathered} \underbrace{X = \lfloor \log_2 \mathrm{amax} \rfloor - e_{\max}}_{\text{OCP MX (floor)}} \\[6pt] \underbrace{s = 2^{\,\lceil \log_2(\mathrm{amax}/448)\rceil}}_{\text{DeepSeek KV (ceiling)}} \end{gathered} \]
  • \(e_{\max}\): exponent of the element's largest value (2 in E2M1, because \(6 = 1.5\cdot 2^2\); 8 in E4M3, because \(448 = 1.75\cdot2^8\))
  • Floor: \(\mathrm{amax}/2^X \in [2^{e_{\max}}, 2^{e_{\max}+1})\), which can exceed the element's maximum. The excess values saturate (they get clipped)
  • Ceiling: \(\mathrm{amax}/s \in (224, 448]\), so it never saturates, but in the worst case it uses only half the range (losing up to 1 bit)

The inventory example (DeepSeek's KV): with amax = 3.7, \(\log_2(3.7/448) = -6.92\), whose ceiling is −6, and the scale is \(2^{-6}=0.015625\). The stored byte is \(-6+127 = 121\). The largest value becomes 236.8 out of a possible 448, so almost one bit of range goes unused, but nothing gets clipped. Play with the figure to see the three rules side by side.

The bit trick in the figure is literal in DeepSeek's overlay: _ue8m0_uint8_to_float(sf) = (sf.int32 << 23).view(float32). Shifting the byte 23 positions puts it exactly in the exponent field of an FP32 with sign 0 and mantissa 0, which is \(2^{\,e-127}\). No floating-point math is done at all.

Real-world trap: numerically converting an E8M0 scale

In the checkpoint, DeepSeek's expert scales are stored as float8_e8m0fnu, but the MoE parameter in vLLM is uint8. A copy_() would do a numeric conversion: \(2^{-7}=0.0078\) would become the integer 0 and the original exponent byte would be gone. The code comment gives exactly this example ("2^-7 → 0"). The loader has to reinterpret the bits with loaded_weight.view(torch.uint8). That is why quantization_config.scale_fmt matters: it tells the loader that those bytes are exponents.

A third rule: GLM's FP8 KV on TensorFold

The TensorFold version of the GLM recipe stores the latent cache of the DSA layers (512 values per token and layer, chapter MLA) as E4M3 rows with one power-of-2 scale per row. The rule is OCP's with the rounding flipped, from floor to ceiling:

\[ s = 2^{\,\lceil \log_2 \mathrm{amax} \rceil - e_{\max}}, \qquad \frac{\mathrm{amax}}{s} \in \big(2^{e_{\max}-1},\ 2^{e_{\max}}\big] = (128,\ 256] \]
  • \(\mathrm{amax}\): largest absolute value of the 512-value row (one row per token and layer)
  • \(e_{\max} = 8\) in E4M3. The code calls this 8 SHIFT
  • \((128, 256]\): where the row's largest value lands. Since 256 < 448, nothing saturates, and the binade \([256, 448]\) is never used

It looks wasteful: the largest value sits between 128 and 256, on a ruler that goes up to 448, and DeepSeek's ceiling rule at least guarantees it exceeds 224. The patch's argument, written in the code itself, is that a float's relative precision does not depend on the scale. Every binade holds the same \(2^m\) values, so changing the scale only slides the row along the binades, and the relative error of every normal value stays bounded by the same 6.25%. What the power of 2 loses to an exact FP32 scale lies at the bottom: the values it pushes below E4M3's smallest normal (\(2^{-6}\)) become subnormals, and those are the ones below about \(2^{-14}\) of the row's largest value. On GLM's real latent rows (outputs of kv_a and kv_a_layernorm, with amax 3 to 5 times the RMS), FP32 scales per token, per 128-block and fixed per layer all landed within 0.001 of the power-of-2 scale's 2.65% relative error per row.

This sharpens how to read the ceiling rule's "loses up to 1 bit". For a float element, one bit less of range at the top is one binade less at the bottom, not one bit less of precision. With E4M3's ~18 binades this costs almost nothing, because almost no value of a row lives 14 binades below the maximum. With E2M1's 3.6 binades, it costs a lot. Interpretation: that is a good reason for the OCP MX rule, which also serves FP4, to prefer the floor and accept saturating the top. The figure shows both situations.

The rest of the format is a deliberate choice too. The scale comes from integer operations on amax's bits alone, like DeepSeek's byte << 23 trick (the step-by-step is in Numbers in the computer), and its inverse is also a power of 2. Quantizing and dequantizing therefore round nothing beyond the E4M3 code itself. Readers convert the codes to BF16 (exact) and multiply the scale into the attention's FP32 products. In the code's words, the format is lossy, never inexact: a row's bytes depend only on the row itself, and a drafted reply stays identical to the serial one.

ParameterKV=fp8 · TF_GLM_KV=fp8 · SHIFT = 8

Bytes. Each row holds 512 E4M3 codes, the scale in FP32 (4 B) and 12 zero bytes that keep rows 16-byte aligned: 528 B per token and layer, or \(528\cdot 8/512 = 8.25\) bits per value. It is the cost of an MXFP8, spent differently. MXFP8 would spend those 16 bytes on 16 E8M0 scales (one every 32 values), while TensorFold spends them on a single scale and alignment. Storing the scale in one E8M0 byte would save nothing, because the row would still be 16-byte aligned (our arithmetic).

Compared with the vLLM recipe. The vLLM recipe's fp8_ds_mla spends the same 16 bytes on 4 FP32 scales, one per 128-block, and adds 128 B of a RoPE region that stays zeroed in GLM: 656 B. By the patch's measurement, FP32 scales per 128-block land within 0.001 of the single scale's error, so the finer granularity buys almost no precision on these rows (interpretation: the measurement used block scales inside TensorFold itself, not vLLM's fp8_ds_mla). With its own kernels, TensorFold stops paying for RoPE and ends up at 528 B, 19.5% less. In exchange, it maintains its own attention kernels, while vLLM uses a ready-made one. The full layout, the indexer keys and the 1M window are left to Quantizing the KV cache.

FP4 E2M1: fifteen values and a lot of nerve

FP4 E2M1 has 1 sign bit, 2 exponent bits (bias 1) and 1 mantissa bit. There are eight magnitudes: with \(E=00\), \(\{0,\ 0.5\}\) (the second is subnormal); with \(E=01\), \(\{1,\ 1.5\}\); with \(E=10\), \(\{2,\ 3\}\); with \(E=11\), \(\{4,\ 6\}\). There is no ∞ and no NaN. That is 16 codes and, since +0 and −0 are worth the same, 15 distinct values. The code is (S << 3) | index, and dequantizing needs no multiplication: an 8-entry table (the LUT) is enough, followed by a multiplication by the scale.

On its own, E2M1 is nearly useless. The maximum relative error is 25% and it covers only 3.6 binades (from 0.5 to 6). With a scale per small block, though, the whole block is pushed inside that short ruler, and E2M1 becomes a real format. In the figure, drag the value and see which code it goes to. The thin lines are the decision boundaries, and ties go to the even code.

This packing explains the odd shape of FP4 tensors in checkpoints. They show up as U8 with half the columns. In Qwen, an experts.E.gate_proj.weight of 640×2560 FP4 values is stored as [640, 1280] U8. In DeepSeek, vLLM's loader declares w13_weight as [E, 2I, H/2] uint8. Anyone reading a safetensors header (chapter Checkpoints) needs to know that the logical width is double.

Why this matters

The E2M1 ruler is the same in NVFP4, in MXFP4, in DeepSeek's MXFP4 indexer cache and in the proposed FP4 KV layouts. What changes between these formats is only the scale: the block size and the numeric format the scale is stored in. With the ruler in your head, you can compare formats by thinking only about the scales.

Microscaling: OCP's MX and the NVFP4 nesting doll

A microscaling format pairs a low-bit element with a scale shared by a small block, and it is defined by both. Two standards dominate.

OCP Microscaling (MX): block of 32, E8M0 scale

The Open Compute Project's MX v1.0 specification defines blocks of 32 elements that share an E8M0 scale. The elements can be FP8 (E4M3 or E5M2, the MXFP8), FP6 (E3M2 or E2M3, the MXFP6), FP4 (E2M1, the MXFP4) or INT8 (MXINT8). The scale uses the floor rule from the previous section:

\[ \begin{gathered} X = \big\lfloor \log_2 \max_{i\in\text{block}} |x_i| \big\rfloor - e_{\max} \\[4pt] P_i = \mathrm{elem}\!\big(x_i / 2^{X}\big), \qquad \hat{x}_i = P_i\cdot 2^{X} \end{gathered} \]
  • \(X\): shared exponent, stored as the byte \(X+127\)
  • \(e_{\max}\): 2 for E2M1, 8 for E4M3
  • \(\mathrm{elem}(\cdot)\): rounds to the element format, saturating at the maximum
  • Cost: \(4 + 8/32 = 4.25\) bits per value in MXFP4; \(8+8/32 = 8.25\) in MXFP8

NVFP4: block of 16, E4M3 scale and one more global scale

NVIDIA's NVFP4 makes two different choices. The block has 16 elements, half of MX's, and the block scale is a positive E4M3 (sometimes called UE4M3), which has 3 mantissa bits and so is not stuck on powers of 2. Since an E4M3 only covers 2⁻⁹ to 448, a second FP32 per-tensor scale comes in to place the block scales inside that range. It is a two-level nesting doll:

\[ \begin{gathered} g = \frac{\mathrm{amax}_{\text{tensor}}}{6\cdot 448}, \qquad s_b = \mathrm{E4M3}\!\Big(\frac{\mathrm{amax}_b}{6\,g}\Big) \\[6pt] q_i = \mathrm{E2M1}\!\Big(\frac{x_i}{s_b\,g}\Big), \qquad \hat{x}_i = q_i\cdot s_b\cdot g \end{gathered} \]
  • \(g\): the tensor's FP32 global scale. In ModelOpt/vLLM it is called weight_scale_2 (global scale)
  • \(s_b\): the E4M3 scale of block \(b\) of 16 values, the weight_scale tensor
  • 6 and 448: the maximums of E2M1 and E4M3. The tensor's largest block gets scale 448, and the others fall below it
  • Cost: \(4 + 8/16 = 4.5\) bits per value (the 32 bits of \(g\) per tensor are negligible)

The format is not everything: the quantizer matters

NVIDIA reports that NVFP4 stays within 1% or less of FP8 in accuracy on DeepSeek-R1-0528 (external source). From format to tensor core: W4A16, W4A4 and the GB10

A format is only worth what the kernel can do with it. There are two ways to multiply an activation by a 4-bit weight, and the difference between them decides the performance of each recipe.

  1. W4A16 (only the weight is quantized). The kernel reads the 4-bit codes, unpacks and dequantizes them in registers and feeds a 16-bit MMA (BF16/FP16) with the untouched activation. It saves bandwidth, because it reads a quarter of BF16's bytes, but it does not save FLOPs. This is Marlin (W4A16 and W8A16), GLM's EXL3 and DeepSeek's b12x.
  2. W4A4 (weight and activation in FP4). An extra step quantizes the activation to NVFP4 (block scale computed on the spot) and the multiplication runs on the FP4 tensor core. It saves bandwidth and FLOPs, and adds the error of quantizing the activation. This is the CUTLASS/FlashInfer path of Qwen's experts.

Which of the two wins depends on the number of rows \(M\) of the GEMM, that is, on how many tokens pass through the layer in that step. In decode, \(M\) is small (1 to a few dozen) and the time is dominated by reading the weights: the regime is memory-bound. It does not matter what precision the math runs in, and W4A16 avoids the extra step. In prefill, \(M\) reaches the thousands, compute dominates (compute-bound) and the FP4 tensor core can deliver several times what BF16 does. A roofline model sums it up:

\[ t(M) \approx \max\!\Big(\frac{\text{weight bytes}}{\beta},\ \frac{2MKN}{\pi_{\text{fmt}}}\Big) + t_{\text{extra}} \]
  • \(\beta\): memory bandwidth (GB10: ~273 GB/s theoretical; 230–250 GB/s effective in the reports)
  • \(\pi_{\text{fmt}}\): the tensor core's peak in the MMA format. The DeepSeek report estimates a class of ~125 dense BF16 TFLOPS on the GB10 and "~4× the dense rate" for FP4×FP8. These are the report's estimates, not microbenchmarks. Our interpretation, based on the shape of the instructions: kind::mxf8f6f4 (which covers E2M1 × E4M3) uses k = 32 and runs at the FP8 rate, ~2× BF16, which matches GLM's "GB10 FP8 tensor rate is 2× BF16"; the ~4× applies to FP4×FP4 via kind::mxf4nvf4, with k = 64. That is why the report's +30–60% estimate is optimistic for the MXFP4×MXFP8 path
  • \(K, N\): the weight's dimensions; \(t_{\text{extra}}\): quantizing the activation (W4A4) or reading/writing activations

In hardware, the block-scaled MMA is Blackwell's novelty: the instruction itself receives the scales and dequantizes inside the tensor core. Datacenter Blackwell (sm_100) uses tcgen05.mma, with operands in shared memory and TMEM. "Consumer" Blackwell (sm_120, and the GB10's sm_121, compute capability 12.1) uses per-warp mma.sync with operands in registers. According to Colfax (external source) and the DeepSeek report (in the repository), NVFP4 on SM12x is mma.sync.aligned.kind::mxf4nvf4.block_scale.scale_vec::4X.m16n8k64…e2m1.e2m1.f32.ue4m3: 4 scales per K = 64, because the block is 16. The MX mixes (E2M1 × E4M3 with ue8m0 scales) use kind::mxf8f6f4. The architecture details are left for Inside the GPU. One number from the GLM repository helps calibrate your intuition: "GB10 FP8 tensor rate is 2× BF16".

Look at the traffic light. That is exactly what happens in DeepSeek. b12x has an NVFP4 tensor core path, but its validation says "the NVFP4 kernels currently support only source_format='modelopt_nvfp4'". The recipe asks for --moe-backend flashinfer_b12x (with VLLM_USE_B12X_MOE=1), but DeepSeek's experts are fp4_e8m0_k32 and fall into W4A16FusedMoeKernel (the boot log confirms it: Using 'B12X_MXFP4' Mxfp4 MoE backend). In decode this costs nothing, because the regime is bandwidth-bound. In prefill, each 1024-token chunk sends ~6,144 token-expert pairs per layer through a BF16 MMA instead of an FP4 one. The recipe report proposes two ways out: an MXFP4 × MXFP8 MoE via kind::mxf8f6f4 ("the checkpoint's format is the hardware-native one") or re-quantizing the experts to ModelOpt NVFP4, which would require a quality gate. The estimate is prefill +30–60% on prompts of up to 32K, and, by the interpretation in the roofline legend, it is optimistic for the first way out: an E2M1 × E4M3 MMA (kind::mxf8f6f4, k = 32) runs at the FP8 rate, not the FP4×FP4 one.

When the kernel says no: shapes, dispatch and fallbacks

Quantized kernels work in tiles and blocks, and so they demand dimensions that are multiples of those sizes. The catch is that the dimensions that count are the ones on each rank, after the TP split: a matrix that passes at TP=1 can be rejected at TP=2.

To predict the per-rank shape, just remember how tensor parallel cuts a weight stored as [N × K] (N outputs, K inputs; the math is \(y = xW^\top\)). In a column-parallel layer (q/k/v, gate/up), each rank keeps a slice of the outputs: the shape becomes [N/TP × K], and each GPU computes a slice of \(y\) without talking to the others. In a row-parallel layer (o_proj, down), each rank keeps a slice of the inputs: the shape becomes [N × K/TP], each GPU computes a partial sum, and an all-reduce puts it all together. The name is confusing because it comes from the transposed matrix \(W^\top\) [K × N]: its "columns" are the outputs, its "rows" are the inputs. What matters here is which dimension shrinks. Column-parallel divides N, and row-parallel divides K, which is precisely the dimension the scale blocks run along (16, 32 or 128 values). Expert parallel, on the other hand, does not cut any expert: it distributes whole experts across the ranks. These are the rules that show up in the recipes:

It is the concrete example of section 5's "the two files can disagree", and config.json is the one the MoE dispatch reads. The helper _fp8_block_scales_config looks up the entry in quantized_layers by the prefix candidates and by the parent prefix, requires an integer group_size greater than 0 and builds Fp8Config(is_checkpoint_fp8_serialized=True, activation_scheme="dynamic", weight_block_size=[g, g]). The result goes to Fp8MoEMethod, which already existed in vLLM and already names the scales weight_scale_inv, exactly as the checkpoint writes them. Despite the name inherited from DeepSeek-V3, weight_scale_inv is the factor that multiplies in dequantization.

Why refuse to guess. "A wrong block shape does not fail loudly, it silently applies misaligned scales." A wrong weight_block_size does not break anything: each tile is multiplied by its neighbor's scale. The symptom would be a drafter with near-random acceptance. With the block read from group_size, MTP=3 worked: 24.5 → 52.1 tok/s, 72.8% acceptance (823/1131), with a per-position curve of 89% / 74.5% / 60%.

The hierarchy of errors

From best to worst: (1) refuse on the spot, with a clear message; (2) die late, 7 minutes later, in the profile run; (3) run with wrong numbers without warning. The recipes' patches push everything to level 1. They read the block instead of assuming it, check the post-TP shape, log one line per fallback and use exact anchors (fail-closed). When you write your own recipe, always prefer an exception to a guess.

How to choose the formats for your recipe

  1. Inventory the checkpoint without downloading it. Read config.json, quantization_config and hf_quant_config.json, and the safetensors headers. Note down, per component, the dtype, the quant_algo, the group_size and the name of the scales (weight_scale, weight_scale_inv, weight_scale_2, input_scale). A U8 tensor with half the columns is packed FP4.
  2. Separate memory from bandwidth. With the inventory in hand, do the two calculations from the bytes figure (section 2): how much each component occupies (the experts decide whether the model fits on your nodes) and how much each decode step reads (the dense parts, read in full at every step, decide the speed). In Qwen, the experts are 51% of what is stored and ~23% of what is read; the BF16 dense parts are ~6.9 of the 9.2 GiB of a step. Quantize first whatever dominates each calculation.
  3. Ask who consumes each format on your hardware. Is there a kernel for sm_121 in decode (small M) and in prefill (large M)? If the format has no tensor core path (MXFP4 in b12x, for example), you will get W4A16: good decode, slower prefill.
  4. Do the per-rank shape math. Divide N by TP in the column-parallel layers and K in the row-parallel ones (with EP, the experts stay whole) and check each kernel's multiples of 16, 32 and 128. Decide up front what happens when it fails: emulate, replicate or change the granularity.
  5. Choose W4A16 or W4A4 (or both) by M. Decode calls for fast weight reads; prefill calls for low-precision FLOPs. A dispatch by M (128 in the GB10 plugin, 512 in GLM's KDA copy) gets the best of both.
  6. Keep what is small or sensitive in high precision. By the bytes math, whatever is small barely weighs on the step, and quantizing it only adds error. Routers, gates, norms, kv_b_proj (MLA absorption), indexers and, if acceptance drops, the drafter. The TensorFold version of the GLM recipe shows the other side of the drafter: it keeps DFlash2 in 4-bit copies (0.63 GiB per rank), because every draft is checked against the target's sample and a worse draft only costs acceptance, never the reply.
  7. Confirm in the logs and measure. Look for the lines that prove the format (B12X_MXFP4, W4A16FusedMoeKernel, the MXFP8 fallback WARNING, "building them with Fp8MoEMethod") and measure KLD against the reference. A draft acceptance close to random is a sign of a misaligned scale. Also measure outside the benchmark's distribution : with the dense weights in 4 bits, English GSM8K and HumanEval matched FP8, but short French coding prompts lost the end of turn in 12 of 48 replies (1 of 48 with FP8 or BF16).

One question remains. If even NVFP4, with a block of 16 and a fractional scale, loses to GLM's EXL3 at the same size, what does EXL3 do differently? The answer has two parts: rotate the weights before quantizing, so that no block has an outlier, and swap the per-block scale for a code with memory, a trellis.
