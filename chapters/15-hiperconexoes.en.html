Every Transformer has a backbone that few people bother to draw: the residual stream, the vector each token carries from layer to layer and into which every sublayer adds whatever it has to say. In the three models behind the recipes, that backbone has been widened. GLM-5.3-Flash and DeepSeek-V4-Flash carry four streams per token, a \(4 \times 4096\) matrix instead of a vector, mixed by \(4\times4\) matrices that an algorithm from the 1960s, Sinkhorn-Knopp, forces to be doubly stochastic. Qwen3.8-Flash-Next carries four branches of 2,560 and reads each channel through a gate of its own. In this chapter you will see why widening helps, why free mixing explodes and how a constraint tames it. Then come the costs in bytes and in kernels. By the end it will be clear why a drafter hook has to call hc_post and contract four streams down to 4096 before handing over any hidden state.

A single-lane road

Start with the standard design. In a decoder-only Transformer each token becomes a vector \(h \in \mathbb{R}^{d}\), with \(d\) = 4,096 in GLM and DeepSeek and 2,560 in Qwen, and that vector crosses the whole stack without ever being replaced. Each sublayer (the token mixer, then the MLP or the MoE) reads a normalized copy and adds its output to the vector:

\[ h_{\ell+1} = h_\ell + F_\ell\big(\mathrm{RMSNorm}(h_\ell)\big), \qquad \mathrm{RMSNorm}(u) = \frac{u}{\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{d}\sum_k u_k^2 + \varepsilon}} \odot \gamma \]
  • \(h_\ell \in \mathbb{R}^d\): the residual stream before sublayer \(\ell\)
  • \(F_\ell\): the sublayer (attention, linear attention, MLP or MoE)
  • \(\gamma \in \mathbb{R}^d\): learned gain of the RMSNorm
  • \(\varepsilon\): rms_norm_eps, which avoids division by zero. It is 1e-6 in Qwen; 1e-5 in GLM (vLLM forces the vision tower to 1e-6); 1e-20 in the DeepSeek-V4-Flash-Vision-Exp that the recipe serves (1e-6 in Flash/0731)

Unrolling the sum, what reaches the lm_head is \(h_L = e + \sum_{\ell} y_\ell\): the embedding plus the contribution of each writer. This structure has one huge virtue, the identity path. The gradient flows from the output all the way back to layer 0 through additions, without crossing a single multiplication, and that is why networks with hundreds of sublayers can be trained at all. The Transformer block chapter builds this structure piece by piece, and abliteration shows how to edit what each writer puts into the stream.

But a single lane has limits, and the Qwen3.8 tech report sums up the main one in a sentence: with pre-normalization every block reads the same stream, so a feature written early has to compete with everything written after it. This shows up in three ways:

  1. Dilution. The reader divides by the RMS of the whole sum, so each new writer shrinks the share of the older ones. If everyone writes vectors of the same size in independent directions, layer \(v\) receives from layer 0 a share of only \(1/(v+1)\) of what it reads.
  2. One decay rate for everyone. A layer's output reaches every later reader with the same relative weight. There is no way to send a loud message to the next-door neighbor and a quiet one to whoever sits fifteen layers down the road. The tech report measures exactly this (we will come back to those numbers in the Qwen section).
  3. The normalization seesaw. Normalizing after the addition (post-norm) weakens the gradient of the early layers. Normalizing before it (pre-norm) stabilizes training, but leaves the representations of neighboring layers too similar. The original hyper-connections paper (Zhu et al., 2024) calls this dilemma the seesaw.

In the figure below, a token, drawn as a little character, travels through 16 sublayers on a single-lane road. At each station the sublayer tosses a package into its backpack, and the bars at the bottom show where each piece of what the chosen layer reads comes from. Move the reader deep into the stack, switch to four lanes and then turn on the uniform gate.

Look at layer 0's number. With one lane it plummets: at reader 15 just over 6% is left. With four lanes layer 0 still makes up about a quarter of what is read, for two reasons the figure lets you pull apart. The first is the separation itself: its lane barely receives new writes, so that lane's RMS doesn't grow. Turn on the uniform gate and about 11% is left at reader 15, almost double the single-lane figure. The rest comes from the reader's gate, which here gives weight 1 to the long lane and 0.25 to the others. In a trained model these gates are not chosen by hand: they are the learned read operators that the next sections introduce. Widening doesn't create information. It creates separate places where information can wait without being diluted.

Analogy: a single backpack

The classic residual is a traveler with just one backpack. Every station tosses an object inside, and to find anything he has to dump the whole backpack onto the table (the normalization). By the tenth station the gift from the first one is buried. With hyper-connections he carries four backpacks, with learned rules about which one to store each object in and which one to pull it out of.

Why this matters to whoever serves the model

Widening changes tensor shapes everywhere. The 4,096-wide “hidden state” of GLM and DeepSeek becomes a [T, 4, 4096] tensor and Qwen's 2,560-wide one becomes [T, 4, 2560] (10,240 numbers per token). Persistent buffers, fused kernels, drafter hooks and even the quantization of the dense weights all need to know about it. Sections 7 to 9 show each of these points in the recipes.

Widening the road: read, write, mix

Hyper-connections (HC) swap the vector \(h\) for a matrix \(H \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}\) per token: \(n\) parallel streams, each with the original width. \(n\) is the expansion rate, called hc_mult in the code (GLM and DeepSeek) or hc_count (Qwen), and it equals 4 in all three models. At the entrance of the stack the embedding is simply repeated, \(H^{(0)} = [e, e, e, e]\) (the hc_expand). From then on, each sublayer interacts with the streams through three learned operators:

\[ x = \sum_{i=1}^{n} h^{\text{pre}}_i\, H_i, \qquad y = F\big(\mathrm{Norm}(x)\big), \qquad H'_j = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \big(H^{\text{res}}\big)_{ij}\, H_i \;+\; h^{\text{post}}_j\, y \]
  • \(H_i \in \mathbb{R}^d\): stream \(i\) (one row of \(H\))
  • \(h^{\text{pre}} \in \mathbb{R}^n\): read, how much of each stream goes into the sublayer (\(n \to 1\))
  • \(h^{\text{post}} \in \mathbb{R}^n\): write, how much of the output \(y\) goes to each stream (\(1 \to n\))
  • \(H^{\text{res}} \in \mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\): mix, how much of stream \(i\) flows into stream \(j\) (\(n \to n\))
  • \(F\): the usual sublayer, which still receives and returns vectors of \(d\) numbers

The detail that makes everything click: the sublayer doesn't change. Attention, the MoE and linear attention still receive a 4,096-wide vector and return another one. Only the plumbing around them got wider. And the classic residual is the special case \(n = 1\), \(h^{\text{pre}} = h^{\text{post}} = 1\), \(H^{\text{res}} = [1]\), in which the equation goes back to \(h' = h + F(\mathrm{Norm}(h))\).

The three operators can be static (fixed parameters, the same for every token) or dynamic, predicted per token from the state itself: \(h^{\star} = h^{\star,s} + \lambda_\star \odot \phi(\bar H\, W_\star)\), with \(\bar H\) a normalized version of \(H\), \(W_\star\) a small projection and \(\phi\) an activation (tanh in the original HC, sigmoid in mHC). The dynamic version is the one all three recipe models use: each token decides, on its own and in every sublayer, where it reads from, where it writes and how it shuffles the lanes.

The stepper below works these computations out by hand for one token, with \(n = 4\) streams and only 6 channels (the model has 4,096). The numbers in the cells are the real values at each step.

Three things jump out. The read collapses four rows into one, so the sublayer never sees the streams separately, only the combination the token chose. The write is an outer product: the same \(y\) goes into all four streams, at a different volume in each. And the mix is the only piece that exchanges information between streams without going through any sublayer. It is also the piece that can go wrong, as the next section shows.

Three vocabularies for the same pieces

The translation table is worth memorizing, because every source uses a different one. The HC paper calls read, write and mix \(A_m\), \(B\) and \(A_r\). The Qwen tech report uses \(H_{\text{mix}}\), \(H_{\text{combine}}\) and \(H_{\text{res}}\). In the GLM and DeepSeek code they are pre, post and comb (in DeepSeek's vLLM, post_mix and res_mix). Qwen calls the read Mix (input_mix_weight_*) and the write Combine or inject (block_inject_weight).

Width alone already pays off

The Qwen tech report tested the simplest possible version, a variant of AltUp (Baykal et al., 2023): 4 branches, a read that is a sum with 4 fixed scalars, and the output written into a single branch picked round-robin by depth. There is no new matrix multiplication, only the traffic of carrying 4 branches around. Even so, the loss of a 25B-A3B MoE trained on 400B tokens drops by about 0.01. Simply having more places to store information already helps. The following variants decide how much machinery is worth stacking on top of that.

Why free mixing explodes

Ignore the writes for a moment and follow only the residual path. At every sublayer the streams are multiplied by that sub-block's mix. After \(L\) sub-blocks the signal that left the embedding has crossed a product of \(L\) matrices, and the gradient, on its way back, crosses the product of the transposes:

\[ H^{(L)} = A_L^{\top} \cdots A_2^{\top} A_1^{\top}\, H^{(0)} \;+\; (\text{writes}), \qquad \big\lVert A_L \cdots A_1 \big\rVert_2 \;\le\; \prod_{\ell=1}^{L} \lVert A_\ell \rVert_2 \]
  • \(A_\ell = H^{\text{res}}_\ell\): the mix of sub-block \(\ell\) (each layer has two, one for attention and one for the FFN)
  • \(\lVert\cdot\rVert_2\): spectral norm, how much the matrix can stretch a vector
  • \(L\): 90 sub-blocks in GLM (45 layers × 2) and 86 in DeepSeek (43 × 2)

In the classic residual \(A_\ell = 1\) and the product is 1 at any depth. With free mixes, any deviation compounds. A matrix that stretches by 10% already gives \(1.1^{90} \approx 5{,}300\) by the end of GLM, and one that shrinks by 10% gives \(0.9^{90} \approx 10^{-4}\). It is the old problem of exploding and vanishing gradients sneaking back in through a new door. The mHC paper (DeepSeek, arXiv 2512.24880) measures this in a model trained with free HC: the compound signal gain reaches about 3,000, and the constraint cuts it by roughly three orders of magnitude.

The constraint is to require each \(A_\ell\) to be a doubly stochastic matrix: non-negative entries, with every row and every column summing to 1. This set has four properties that solve the problem in one go:

\[ A \ge 0,\;\; A\mathbf{1} = \mathbf{1},\;\; \mathbf{1}^{\top}\!A = \mathbf{1}^{\top} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \lVert A \rVert_2 \le \sqrt{\lVert A\rVert_1\,\lVert A\rVert_\infty} = \sqrt{1\cdot 1} = 1 \]
  • \(\lVert A\rVert_1\): largest (absolute) column sum, equal to 1
  • \(\lVert A\rVert_\infty\): largest row sum, equal to 1
  • \(\mathbf{1}\): vector of ones, an eigenvector with eigenvalue 1
  1. It doesn't stretch. The spectral norm is at most 1 (the inequality above), so no mix amplifies the signal.
  2. Closed under products. The product of two doubly stochastic matrices is also doubly stochastic. The guarantee holds for 90 sub-blocks, or 900.
  3. It preserves the mean. Since the rows sum to 1 (each input stream hands out exactly all of its content), \(\sum_j H'_j = \sum_i \big(\sum_j A_{ij}\big) H_i = \sum_i H_i\), that is, \(\tfrac1n\sum_j H'_j = \tfrac1n\sum_i H_i\). And since the columns sum to 1, each output stream is a weighted average of the input ones. It is the “conservative” version of the identity path.
  4. It is a weighted shuffle. By the Birkhoff-von Neumann theorem, every doubly stochastic matrix is a weighted average of permutation matrices. The mix only redistributes the streams and never creates new energy. The set of these matrices is called the Birkhoff polytope, and it is the “manifold” in the name mHC, manifold-constrained hyper-connections.

The figure composes random mixes across the recipes' real depth and plots the largest and smallest stretch factors of the product (the extreme singular values). Turn up the mix deviation and compare the free version with the projected one.

mHC's solid line stays pinned at 1, whatever the draw, because the vector of ones always passes through unscathed. The free version, on the other hand, fans out. With \(\sigma = 0.3\) the largest factor comfortably exceeds 1,000 (from \(10^4\) to \(10^5\), depending on the draw) and the smallest drops below one millionth: the product stretches some directions and crushes others at the same time. Notice also that mHC's dashed line goes down. That is not a defect, it is the math: away from the direction of the mean, a doubly stochastic mix that is not a permutation contracts the differences between streams. The writes (post) and reads of each sub-block restore that diversity, and learned mixes tend to stay close to the identity. That last sentence is our interpretation, but it is what makes the constraint cheap.

Pitfall: “normalizing” is not “bounding”

Dividing each row by its sum (a row-wise softmax, for example) guarantees that rows sum to 1, but not columns. A column can sum to 2.5, and that output stream then receives 2.5 times the signal. Multiply that across 90 sub-blocks and the explosion is back. Only the constraint in both directions delivers the guarantee \(\lVert A\rVert_2 \le 1\).

Sinkhorn-Knopp: taming the mix

One practical problem remains: how does a neural network produce a doubly stochastic matrix from free numbers, in a differentiable and cheap way? The answer comes from a theorem by Sinkhorn. Every matrix with strictly positive entries can be turned into a doubly stochastic one by multiplying its rows and columns by positive factors, \(D_1 A D_2\), and the algorithm that finds those factors is as naive as it gets: alternate. Normalize the columns, then the rows, then the columns again… Each step slightly undoes what the previous one fixed, but the error shrinks geometrically. That is Sinkhorn-Knopp.

DeepSeek's reference kernel (the same scheme as GLM's) does exactly this, for every token and every sub-block:

\[ M \leftarrow \mathrm{softmax}_{\text{row}}(Z) + \varepsilon, \qquad M \leftarrow \frac{M}{\mathbf{1}^{\top}M + \varepsilon}, \qquad \underbrace{\Big[\, M \leftarrow \frac{M}{M\mathbf{1} + \varepsilon},\;\; M \leftarrow \frac{M}{\mathbf{1}^{\top}M + \varepsilon} \,\Big]}_{\texttt{hc\_sinkhorn\_iters} - 1 \;=\; 19 \text{ times}} \]
  • \(Z \in \mathbb{R}^{4\times4}\): the 16 mix logits predicted for the token
  • \(\mathrm{softmax}_{\text{row}}\): makes everything positive and the rows sum to 1
  • \(\varepsilon\) = hc_eps = 1e-6: keeps the entries strictly positive (the theorem's condition) and guards the divisions
  • \(\mathbf{1}^{\top}M\): column sums; \(M\mathbf{1}\): row sums

With hc_sinkhorn_iters = 20 there are 39 normalizations after the softmax, and the last one is always by columns. This has a beautiful consequence. Since \(H'_j = \sum_i \text{comb}_{ij} H_i\) uses column \(j\), each output stream is exactly a weighted average of the input streams, with weights summing to 1 up to the precision of \(\varepsilon\). The rows sum to 1 only approximately, with an error that depends on how well the iteration converged. That is why the mean of the streams is preserved only approximately, which comes back in the deferred capture of section 8. Play with the figure to see just how approximate.

With soft logits, the matrix is already nearly doubly stochastic after three or four steps, and 20 iterations are more than enough. With sharp logits, when the token wants a mix close to a permutation, convergence gets visibly slower. If two rows fight over the same column, the iteration needs many steps to decide which one backs off. The mHC paper reports convergence “within 20 iterations” in the trained models. Our interpretation: that is probably where the config.json value comes from.

Checkpoint confighc_mult: 4 · hc_sinkhorn_iters: 20 · hc_eps: 1e-6

The three fields live in DeepSeek-V4-Flash's config.json (with the same values in GLM's). The recipe does not parametrize them: vLLM reads them in model.py:1175-1178 and passes them to the kernels. Still, they add to the cost. 20 iterations are 39 sequential normalizations per token, which cannot run in parallel with each other. That is why Sinkhorn lives inside mHC's fused kernel, in shared memory, and not in loose PyTorch calls. Fewer iterations would yield a comb that preserves the norm only approximately. More iterations would cost latency; our interpretation: since the paper reports convergence within 20, the gain would be marginal.

Deep dive: the geometry of the polytope

For \(n = 4\) the matrix has 16 entries, but the sum constraints pin down \(2n - 1 = 7\) of them. What remains is a polytope of dimension \((n-1)^2 = 9\) whose vertices are the \(4! = 24\) permutation matrices. Sinkhorn-Knopp is a projection (in the KL divergence sense) of any positive matrix onto this polytope. During training the gradient flows through the 20 unrolled iterations. At inference only the forward pass matters.

Inside mHC: 24 numbers per token

Now we can read GLM's and DeepSeek's mHC line by line. Both implement the same recipe, which is the reference published by DeepSeek. In each sub-block (attention or FFN) the token's state \(H\) is flattened into a vector of \(4 \times 4096 = 16{,}384\) numbers (the hc_dim), normalized by its RMS and projected by a small matrix into 24 coefficients:

\[ \begin{aligned} u &= \mathrm{vec}(H) \in \mathbb{R}^{16384}, & m &= \frac{W_{fn}\, u}{\sqrt{\overline{u^2} + \varepsilon}} \in \mathbb{R}^{24}, \\ \text{pre} &= \sigma(\alpha_0\, m_{0:4} + b_{0:4}) + \varepsilon_{hc}, & \text{post} &= 2\,\sigma(\alpha_1\, m_{4:8} + b_{4:8}), \\ \text{comb} &= \mathrm{Sinkhorn}_{20}\Big(\mathrm{softmax}_{\text{row}}\big(\alpha_2\, m_{8:24} + b_{8:24}\big)\Big) \in \mathbb{R}^{4\times4}, && \end{aligned} \] \[ x = \sum_{j} \text{pre}_j\, H_j \;\longrightarrow\; y = F\big(\mathrm{RMSNorm}(x)\big), \qquad H'_j = \sum_{i} \text{comb}_{ij}\, H_i + \text{post}_j\, y \]
  • \(W_{fn} \in \mathbb{R}^{24\times16384}\): hc_attn_fn or hc_ffn_fn; 24 = \((2+n)\,n\) = 4 pre + 4 post + 16 comb (mix_hc)
  • \(\alpha \in \mathbb{R}^3\): hc_*_scale, one scale per group
  • \(b \in \mathbb{R}^{24}\): hc_*_base, the static term
  • \(\varepsilon_{hc}\) = hc_eps = 1e-6; the RMS \(\varepsilon\) is rms_norm_eps (1e-20 in the Vision-Exp checkpoint served by the DeepSeek recipe)
  • \(\sigma\): sigmoid; the factor 2 in post is hc_post_alpha / mhc_post_mult_value = 2.0

Every choice has a reason. The RMS of \(u\) in the denominator makes the coefficients depend on the direction of the state, not its size. pre is a sigmoid plus \(\varepsilon\), always positive and bounded: “read a little from each lane”, never subtract one of them. The Qwen tech report also found that positive sigmoid gates train better than tanh, and cites mHC in agreement. post is \(2\sigma(\cdot) \in (0, 2)\), which equals exactly 1 when the logit is zero. Our interpretation: that way, a neutral post writes just like the classic residual. And comb is the softmax followed by the Sinkhorn you have just seen.

In parameters this is almost nothing. Each layer has two sets (attention and FFN) of \(24 \times 16384 + 24 + 3\), that is, 786,486 parameters per layer, 35.4 M across GLM's 45 layers, 0.035 B out of a 321 B model. DeepSeek has the same ~0.79 M per layer. Being tiny and sensitive, they are kept out of every quantization: they sit in the modules_to_not_convert of GLM's official FP8 (BF16 in the checkpoint, loaded in FP32 by vLLM) and are FP32 in DeepSeek.

GLM-5.3-Flash

  • mHC in all 45 trunk layers; the MTP layer (index 45) uses a plain residual
  • after pre: input_layernorm and post_attention_layernorm
  • sublayers: KDA (34) or MLA NoPE + indexer (11); dense MLP (0–2) or MoE with 288 experts
  • stack output: hc_contract = mean of the 4 streams → model.norm → lm_head

DeepSeek-V4-Flash

  • mHC in all 43 layers and also in the 3 stages of the DSpark drafter
  • attn_norm and ffn_norm fused inside the mHC kernel
  • sublayers: SWA / CSA / HCA; MoE with 256 experts (hash in layers 0–2)
  • output: hc_head, a 4 → 1 read with learned \(\sigma(\cdot)+\varepsilon\) weights (no Sinkhorn), then norm and lm_head

The deferred post and the post + pre fusion

Look at a token's sequence in vLLM: attention post, FFN pre, FFN, FFN post, the next layer's attention pre… Each post reads the whole \(4 \times 4096\) state and writes a new one, and the following pre reads that new state right back. That is two trips to DRAM per boundary to touch the same bytes. That is why both engines fuse one sub-block's post with the next one's pre into a single kernel (MHCFusedPostPreOp). It reads \(H\) once, applies comb and post, computes on the spot the 24 coefficients of the next sub-block (Sinkhorn included) and the normalized input of the next sublayer, and writes \(H'\) once.

The consequence for anyone reading the code is important. A layer doesn't return the finished residual. Each layer's forward returns the tuple (hidden_states, residual, post, comb): the raw FFN output, the state before the last post and the coefficients needed to apply it. The one that applies it is the next layer, inside the fused kernel. Only after the last layer does vLLM materialize the state (final_hc_post) and perform the contraction. Keep this tuple in mind. Section 8 revolves around it.

In the subway map below, the four streams are train lines. At the pre station the passengers of each line board an express train that heads to the sublayer, with the token's boarding weights. At the post station the train comes back and drops people off on all four lines. On the comb stretch there is a transfer: passengers switch lines with the probabilities of the \(4\times4\) matrix. The colors of the dots show which line each passenger came from.

Notice how the colored dots spread out after each transfer, yet never pile up on a single line. Each comb column sums to 1, so each output line receives “one full line's worth” of passengers. Notice also that the express train carries a different mix in each sub-block. The attention and the FFN of the same layer can read different lanes, and that is where hyper-connections gain expressiveness.

Qwen's Gated Residual: reading with a magnifying glass

Qwen3.8-Flash-Next also widens the residual to 4 branches, but spends the expressiveness somewhere else. The tech report starts from hyper-connections and runs a careful ablation on a 25B-A3B MoE trained on 560B tokens, always with 4 branches. Five observations shaped the final design:

  1. Positive, bounded gates. Sigmoid beats tanh in both loss and stability (as in mHC).
  2. Data dependence. Dynamic operators reduce the loss only slightly (0.002 lower than static ones), but add 1.98 points to the benchmark average. It is a case where looking at the loss alone would have been misleading.
  3. Read granularity matters more than write granularity. Replacing the read's per-branch scalar with a weight per branch and per channel helps. Refining the write the same way barely changes anything, so the write stays scalar.
  4. Read all branches. Predicting the operators from all branches beats using only the last one or an average. Normalizing each branch separately (a grouped RMSNorm) helps a bit more.
  5. \(H_{\text{res}}\) adds almost nothing. With an expressive read and write, the \(n\times n\) mixing matrix brings no significant improvement.

One piece was still missing, and it came from another work by the same group: GatedNorm, an RMSNorm followed by a low-rank elementwise gate that proved decisive for training stability:

\[ \mathrm{GatedNorm}(u) = \mathrm{RMSNorm}(u) \odot \sigma\Big(W_2\, \mathrm{SiLU}\big(W_1\, \mathrm{RMSNorm}(u)\big)\Big) \]
  • \(W_1, W_2\): a low-rank bottleneck (down to \(r\) dimensions and back up)
  • \(\odot\): element-wise product, the multiplicative gating

The read the ablation converged on (elementwise, data-dependent, sigmoid) is exactly a GatedNorm applied to the widened residual. Qwen merged the two ideas into a single operator and named it Gated Residual (GR):

\[ \hat R_i = \mathrm{RMSNorm}(R_i;\, \gamma_i), \qquad G = \sigma\Big(W_u\, \mathrm{SiLU}\big(\tfrac14\, W_d\, \mathrm{vec}(\hat R)\big)\Big) \in \mathbb{R}^{4\times2560}, \qquad x = \tfrac14 \sum_{i=1}^{4} G_i \odot \hat R_i \] \[ s = 2\,\sigma\big(\tfrac14\, W_w\, \mathrm{vec}(\hat R)\big) \in \mathbb{R}^{4}, \qquad R_i \leftarrow R_i + s_i\, y, \qquad y = F(x) \]
  • \(R \in \mathbb{R}^{4\times2560}\): the 4 branches (hc_count = 4), 10,240 numbers per token
  • \(\gamma_i\): per-branch gain, applied as \((1+w)\) (hc_norm, [10240])
  • \(W_d \in \mathbb{R}^{320\times10240}\), \(W_u \in \mathbb{R}^{10240\times320}\): bottleneck of rank hc_lowrank = 320 = 2560/8
  • \(G\): one gate per branch and per channel; \(W_w \in \mathbb{R}^{4\times10240}\): block_inject_weight
  • \(s_i \in (0,2)\): the write, one scalar per branch

Compare it with mHC. The read is no longer one scalar per stream: it has become a magnifying glass of 10,240 gates, one per branch and channel, produced by a rank-320 bottleneck. The write stays scalar. The mixing matrix is gone: the branches never exchange information directly, and each one is a pure accumulator, \(R_c^{(v)} = R_c^{(0)} + \sum_{u<v} s_c^{(u)} y^{(u)}\). And since the read already normalizes and applies the gate, GR replaces the block's pre-normalization (there is no input_layernorm). At the end of the stack, a read-only GR, the hyper_connection_mixer (use_combine=False), plays the role of the final RMSNorm before the lm_head. Each layer has two GRs (attn_hyper_connection and mlp_hyper_connection): 48 × 2 + 1 = 97 modules.

What the magnifying glass buys you shows up in step 4. With the elementwise gate, the input \(x\) can take a few channels from one branch, others from another, and ignore the rest. In the example, the token “de” lights up channel 3 of branch 0, channel 17 of branch 2 and three more, and its \(x\) ends up containing practically only those channels. With one scalar per branch, you either read the whole branch or none of it: \(x\) becomes a faint smear across all channels, and the figure shows the relative difference between the two reads. Switch tokens and the gate pattern changes completely, because it is predicted from the state itself.

The ablation and stress-test numbers are in the next figure. GR ties with dynamic mHC in quality and wins on two practical fronts. It is more stable, because GatedNorm itself removes training spikes and because, without \(H_{\text{res}}\), there is no manifold constraint to maintain. And it is cheaper at inference: dropping \(H_{\text{res}}\) eliminates one full read of the residual state per block, and the tech report identifies the traffic of this wide state as the dominant inference cost of the widened residual.

What the 4 branches are for, in practice

Since GR doesn't mix branches, you can decompose exactly which earlier block each piece of a block's read came from. The tech report did this on a 20-layer MoE, comparing it against a twin without GR. In five checkpoints, one branch always became a long-range highway, with a typical hop of 10.9 layers, versus 3.4–3.9 on the other three. The output of layer 0 (GDN) reaches layer 15's attention with a share of 0.138, versus 0.020 in the single-lane model. And layer 0's MLP reaches a neighbor (0.139 → 0.192, layer 2) and a distant reader (0.008 → 0.058, layer 15, a relative gain of ~7×) with different intensities, something a single lane, with its one decay rate, cannot express. The readers that lean on this branch the most are the softmax attention layers, which act as hubs for the long-range context that the GDN layers compress.

What didn't work (and is a lesson for anyone optimizing inference)

Since, after training, the write tends to be dominated by two branches, the team tried reading only the 2 branches with the highest gate. Pre-training loss and benchmarks barely moved, but quality dropped clearly after post-training, and the idea was abandoned. Pre-training metrics alone would have led to the wrong decision. What did work was storing the branches in FP8 (the gates bound what gets written, so the values stay within a narrow range), at half the bytes and with almost no loss, plus fusing read and write into one kernel each. The chapter on measuring quality discusses this kind of care in detail.

One last link to the next chapter: Qwen's N-gram memory (the PLE, in the layer with index 1) writes into the 4 branches, and does so branch by branch. The key projection goes from 2,560 to 10,240, one key per branch, with a shared value and a scalar gate per branch. Chapter 16 takes this module apart.

The price of width: memory, bandwidth and kernels

Widening has a cost, and it shows up on three different bills.

1. Activations: the state got 4× bigger

Per token, the residual state goes from \(d\) to \(n\,d\) numbers. In BF16 that is 32 KiB per token in GLM and DeepSeek (\(4 \times 4096 \times 2\) B) and 20 KiB in Qwen (\(4 \times 2560 \times 2\) B), versus 8 and 5 KiB with a single lane. It looks small until you multiply it by the number of tokens in a step. vLLM pre-allocates several fixed-address buffers at the maximum step size, --max-num-batched-tokens (8,192 in DeepSeek), so that CUDA graphs can reuse them. And then:

\[ \text{bytes} = T \times n \times d \times b = 8192 \times 4 \times 4096 \times 2\ \text{B} = 268{,}435{,}456\ \text{B} = 256\ \text{MiB} \]
  • \(T\): tokens in the step (the ceiling is max_num_batched_tokens)
  • \(n\,d\) = hc_dim = 16,384; \(b\): bytes per number (2 in BF16)

That is exactly the size of DeepSeek's _mtp_hidden_buffer in vLLM, [max_num_batched_tokens, hc_dim] in BF16, allocated on the last pipeline rank. It stores the wide pre-hc_head state of every step for the MTP/EAGLE drafter, and every target forward does a copy_ into it. But the recipe uses DSpark, which doesn't read that buffer. DSpark reads the means of layers 40–42 into a buffer of its own, _dspark_hidden_buffer, [8192, 3 × 4096] (192 MiB in BF16, by the same math). The hotfix hotfix-dsv4-mtp-buffer-50312.sh (a backport of vLLM PR #50312) allocates the MTP buffer only if use_eagle() or uses_draft_model() ask for it, skips the copy_ when the buffer is None and adds two None guards in v1/worker/gpu/model_runner.py that upstream lacks. Promised savings: 256 MiB per rank (448 MiB by upstream's count), which turn into KV cache.

Caveat recorded in the inventory

The hotfix comment says use_eagle() is false for dspark, but the image's pinned code shows use_eagle() including "dspark". If so, the condition stays true and the buffer stays allocated. Only the boot log can settle it. It is a good example of why you should check the “GPU KV cache size” line before and after every patch (the script itself has --before and --after modes).

2. Bandwidth: small weights, heavy traffic

In decode with one token per step the bottleneck is reading weights (chapter 10), and here the three models part ways. The mHC weights are tiny: 35.4 M parameters in GLM, about 135 MiB in FP32 (our math, from the shapes). Qwen's GR weights, in contrast, add up to 0.641 B parameters and 1.19 GiB in BF16, because of the two \(10240 \times 320\) bottleneck matrices in each of the 97 modules. And they are replicated: the fused projection is created with disable_tp=True, which means that instead of each GPU keeping half of the matrix's rows, as in the tensor parallelism of the parallelism chapter, each of the two GPUs stores and reads the full 1.19 GiB on every step. That is why a module holding 0.5% of the parameters accounts for 13% of the step's bytes (section 9).

With many tokens in the step (prefill, large batches) the cost that grows is the activations. Each kernel pass over the wide state of an 8,192-token DeepSeek step moves 256 MiB, and there are a few passes per layer, across 43 layers. The figure runs this math with a deliberately simple model.

Try the DeepSeek setting. With 8,192 tokens in BF16 a step's state is 256 MiB, the same size as the _mtp_hidden_buffer, and the schematic traffic exceeds 40 GiB per step. That is a visible fraction of a prefill, but irrelevant in a 7-row decode, which moves less than 1 MiB of state per pass. Switch to FP8 and everything halves. It is the optimization Qwen describes in the tech report, but none of the three recipes stores the residual in FP8 in vLLM, and the TensorFold version of the GLM recipe also keeps the 4 streams in BF16. The weights follow the opposite logic: they don't depend on \(T\) and they dominate decode.

3. Kernels: specialized, and with strings attached

mHC has its own kernels in vLLM (vllm.model_executor.layers.mhc): MHCPreOp, MHCPostOp, the fused MHCFusedPostPreOp and the output's HCHeadOp, implemented in TileLang on the DeepSeek path. DeepSeek's Stage-C image (lane B, historical/optional “200K/16”) ships a second path, in b12x (CuTeDSL for consumer Blackwell), behind two variables:

# recipe/overlay/vllm/models/deepseek_v4/nvidia/model.py (Stage-C)
def _use_b12x_mhc():            # VLLM_USE_B12X_MHC (envs.py:1023, default 0)
    ... requires CUDA and the SM120 family, otherwise RuntimeError
def _b12x_mhc_max_tokens():     # B12X_MHC_MAX_TOKENS, default "16"
    return int(os.environ.get("B12X_MHC_MAX_TOKENS", "16"))
def _should_run_b12x_mhc(self, tokens):
    return max_tokens <= 0 or tokens <= max_tokens   # <= 0: always b12x
# at layer construction:
#   hc_mult == MHC_MULT, hidden_size == 4096,
#   (hc_mult*hidden) % block_k == 0 and split_k == MHC_DEFAULT_SPLIT_K,
#   otherwise NotImplementedError / ValueError

It is a dispatch by M, the number of rows in the step. Up to 16 tokens (a decode with a k=6 draft has 7 rows per sequence) the b12x kernel runs, tuned for small steps. Above that, the code's own comment says TileLang “provides the fallback path for mixed/prefill capture sizes when b12x mHC is limited to decode”. And the conditions say a lot about how specialized kernels work: b12x mHC was compiled for exactly \(n = 4\) and \(d = 4096\), with a fixed split-K. A new model with hidden size 7,168 or 8 streams won't run on it. Construction fails right away, which is better than failing silently. The Stage-C overlay also warms up TileLang mHC at every capture size, so that no JIT compilation happens in the middle of a request (kernels chapter).

Qwen takes a different route. GR's elementwise operations are fused Triton kernels (hc_combine_norm, hc_gate_mix, grouped_gemma_rmsnorm), and the three linears are cuBLAS BF16 GEMMs. In vLLM, the down projection and the inject projection become a single matrix, input_mix_weight_down_block_inject, with shape (336, 10240): 320 from the bottleneck + 4 from the write + 12 of padding. The up projection is (10240, 320).

4. Two Sparks: replicate or split the glue

So far we have counted the cost of width inside a single GPU. With two tensor parallel ranks a new question shows up: who does the glue? That is the name TensorFold and the GLM·TF recipe give to everything that happens between two sublayers. It is the second version of the GLM recipe: the same checkpoint, on the same two DGX Sparks, served by the TensorFold v0.6.0 engine with 53 patches instead of vLLM (An exact engine explains the engine). The glue is the post of the sublayer that just finished plus the pre of the next one. Per row, that is, per token, it means the 24 dot products over 16,384 columns and the sum of squares for the RMS, the sigmoids, the softmax and Sinkhorn's 39 normalizations, the weighted read and the RMSNorm, plus one read and one write of the 4 streams (32 KiB per row in BF16, each). Hyper-connections are too small to be worth the tensor-parallel cut, so they stay replicated. In GLM's TP=2, attention and the FFN (the MoE, or a dense MLP in the first 3 layers) each end with one partial per rank, shaped \([R, 4096]\) in fp32. After gathering them, both ranks do the same glue on the same \(R\) rows, at 90 exchange points per pass (45 layers × 2).

In decode this costs little in bytes, because a TensorFold verify window has at most 16 rows per stream. It costs latency. The glue is a chain of small, dependent steps (Sinkhorn's 39 normalizations cannot run in parallel with one another), and DRAM sits idle while it runs. TensorFold answers with kernel engineering. The first half of hc_pre (the 24 products and the sum of squares) got a CUDA kernel that launches 48 blocks per row instead of 16 and reproduces, bit for bit, the reduction tree of the Triton kernel it replaced (kernels chapter). And an L2 prefetch (TF_GLM_L2PF), fired on a side stream at the two exchanges of each layer, uses the wait for the wire and the glue, phases that leave DRAM idle, to bring in the weights that come next ahead of time. The glue weights stay in BF16, as in the checkpoint: the decode kernel requires fn [24, 16384] in BF16, ~67.5 MiB across the 90 exchange points, half of the ~135 MiB that vLLM loads in FP32 (our count, from the shapes). A third-party measurement gives the order of magnitude of the time. In a community fork (jayleaton: TensorFold 0.3.4 with 77 patches of its own and a different checkpoint), the glue costs about 15.6 µs per boundary, with 90 boundaries per round: ~1.4 ms in a ~53 ms round with 1 stream, about 2.6% (our arithmetic on their numbers).

In prefill the math changes. A prompt chunk has up to 2,048 rows (TF_GLM_PREFILL_ROWS), and each rank redoes the glue for all 2,048 rows, at the 90 exchange points, only to reach the same result as its peer. TensorFold's way out uses a property the previous sections left in plain sight: the glue does not mix tokens. A row's 24 coefficients, its Sinkhorn and its RMS depend only on that row. So the work can be split by rows, and that is what SPLIT=1 does. Rank \(r\) owns rows \([rH, rH+H)\), with \(H = R/2\) (an odd chunk gets one row of zeros that nothing reads). At each exchange point:

  1. Send the peer only what is its own. The fp32 partial of the peer's rows goes over ncclSend/ncclRecv (chapter 39 details the exchange), instead of the all-gather of the whole partial.
  2. Add up its own rows, rank 0 first. It is the same kernel as the unsplit path, in the same order, so the bits do not change.
  3. Run the glue only on its own rows. The hc_post, the drafter taps, the final stream mean and the next hc_pre.
  4. Swap the normalized rows in BF16. Both ranks end up with the whole input of the next block, one 4,096-wide vector per row.

Per row and per exchange point, each rank now sends \(\tfrac12 \cdot 16 + \tfrac12 \cdot 8 = 12\) KiB instead of 16 KiB, and does half of the glue (our count). As we read it, the split is sequence parallelism applied to the glue only. It also comes with overlap (TF_GLM_PREFILL_OVERLAP=2), which has two levels. At level 1 the exchanges run on a second CUDA stream, in 2 pieces of rows: each rank computes the peer's rows of its partial first, and each piece goes out on the wire as soon as it is ready, while the GPU moves on to its own rows. Level 2 fills the wait that was left: while the normalized rows travel, the main stream gets ahead on the “front” of the next block for its own rows (the KDA or DSA input projections, the shared expert or the dense MLP). The glue kernels and the prompt matmuls are row-independent, so everything yields the same bits as the replicated path. The figure compares the three ways at one exchange point.

The recipe's numbers come with conditions. Prefill of a 50,000-token prompt went from ~1,270 to ~1,730 tok/s, measured together with two prompt-kernel patches (0009 and 0020), so it is not the isolated effect of the split. Level 2 of the overlap took a 149,000-token prompt from 92.8 to 90.7 s (two boots on each side, with COPY_CODE on). In exchange, decode rounds pay ~1.5%, and chunks with fewer than 64 rows, like the tail of a prompt, are not split at all (TF_GLM_HC_SPLIT_MIN_ROWS). Our own arithmetic shows why the wire is worth fighting for. At 2,048 rows, the whole partial of one exchange point is 32 MiB. That is a prompt chunk's all-gather, which the recipe measured at 1.9 ms (“32 MB”) with NCCL over the two rails of the cable between the Sparks. Unsplit, across the 90 exchange points, that would be ~170 ms of wire per chunk with no overlap, close to one sixth of the ~1 s a 2,048-row chunk takes today. That ~1 s comes from the ~1,950 tok/s sparkDash measured with the split already on and the GPU clocks capped at 2,200 MHz, so the count only holds as an order of magnitude.

vLLM × TensorFold: two ways to pay for the same glue

The two engines attack the same cost from different sides. The GLM recipe's vLLM works inside each GPU: it fuses a sub-block's post into the next one's pre to touch the wide state fewer times (section 5) and leaves the glue replicated across ranks. It is simple and adds no exchange. The GLM·TF recipe also has its own glue kernels (tensor cores in prefill, CUDA in decode) and, on top of that, splits the work across GPUs in prefill. It pays with a second exchange per point (the BF16 rows), an extra stream with events to order everything, a 64-row threshold and a demanding contract: every glue kernel has to be row-independent, which TensorFold already requires to guarantee that a drafted reply equals the serial one. Our interpretation: in decode the split would not pay off, because with at most 32 rows in the window the message is so small that the fixed latency of one more exchange would cost more than the half of the glue it saves. A 16 KiB all-gather over RoCE takes ~11 µs, and the whole glue of one boundary ~15.6 µs in the fork cited above (numbers from different engines). That is why decode stays replicated. And neither recipe publishes a controlled A/B between the engines, so no share of the prefill gap between them can be pinned on the glue.

Drafter hooks: from 4 streams to 4096

Modern drafters such as EAGLE-3, DFlash2 and DSpark don't look only at the target model's last state. They read auxiliary hidden states from a few intermediate layers, from shallow to deep, and condition the draft on them (chapter 36 and chapter 37). In a classic Transformer this is trivial: after layer \(\ell\), copy the residual. With mHC there are two problems. The “state of layer \(\ell\)” is not a 4096-wide vector, it is [T, 4, 4096]. Worse: because of the post+pre fusion, it doesn't even exist in memory. What exists is the tuple (hidden, residual, post, comb) the layer returned, with hc_post still pending.

The GLM recipe solves this in overlay/patch_glm_eagle3.py, a patch that teaches Glm5NextModel the auxiliary hidden-state interface that DFlash2 uses:

for idx, layer in enumerate(self._active_layers, start=self.start_layer):
    hidden_states, residual, post, comb = layer(positions, hidden_states, residual, post, comb)
    if idx + 1 not in self.aux_hidden_state_layers:
        continue
    # Mid-stack mHC defers hc_post; materialize then contract
    # 4 streams -> [tokens, hidden] (deepseek_v4 eagle3 pattern).
    if post is not None and hasattr(layer, "hc_post"):
        value = hc_contract(layer.hc_post(hidden_states, residual, post, comb), layer.n)
    else:
        value = hidden_states
        if value.ndim == 3:
            value = value.mean(dim=1)
    if self.is_sequence_parallel:
        value = sp_all_gather(value)[:full_num_tokens]
    aux_hidden_states.append(value)

Three details matter. Materialize: layer.hc_post(...) applies \(\text{comb}^{\top} H + \text{post} \otimes y\) on a separate copy. The fused chain stays intact, because the original tuple moves on to the next layer, and the cost is one extra post per tap. Contract: hc_contract takes the mean of the 4 streams, the same operation GLM uses at the end of the stack, and delivers [T, 4096], which is what DFlash2 expects. Parallelism: with sequence parallel (the activations outside the TP-sharded matmuls are split across ranks by token), each rank holds only a slice of the tokens, so an sp_all_gather is needed to assemble the full [T, 4096]. The patch also gives the model the EagleModelMixin and SupportsEagle3 interfaces. The taps are the outputs of layers 5, 14, 24, 33 and 42, and the log shows them as (6, 15, 25, 34, 43) because the test is idx + 1.

DeepSeek does the same for DSpark, in three variants. The code you can actually read is the Stage-C overlay's (lane B, historical/optional “200K/16”), because lane A's code (Anemll 0.1.1, the production lane) isn't in the repo. In this code's default mode (flags off), when passing through target layers 40, 41 and 42 the forward calls layer.hc_post, clears residual, post, comb and writes hidden_states.mean(dim=1) into that layer's slice of _dspark_hidden_buffer. At layers 40 and 41, clearing the tuple breaks the fused chain, and the next layer runs an isolated hc_pre. At 42, the last one, this costs nothing, because final_hc_post would materialize the state anyway. Our interpretation: lane A probably follows equivalent logic, but the repo doesn't let us verify it. The three slices add up to 12,288 numbers per token, which the drafter's main_proj (12,288 → 4,096) projects. The Stage-C overlay also offers deferred capture, which breaks nothing:

\[ \frac14\sum_{k=1}^{4} H'_k \;=\; \Big(\frac14\sum_k \text{post}_k\Big)\, y \;+\; \sum_{i=1}^{4} \Big(\frac14\sum_k \text{comb}_{ik}\Big) H_i \;\approx\; \overline{\text{post}}\; y + \frac14\sum_i H_i \]
  • \(H'_k\): stream \(k\) after the target layer's post (what the capture needs)
  • \(\sum_k \text{comb}_{ik}\): sum of row \(i\), which is ≈ 1 after Sinkhorn
  • the exact kernel computes the left-hand side in FP32 without materializing the 4 streams

With VLLM_DSV4_DSPARK_DEFER_TARGET_CAPTURE=1, the capture is done by the next layer (41 captures 40 and 42 captures 41), inside its mhc_fused_post_pre. Layer 42, the last one, has no successor (the code only defers a target if layer id + 1 exists) and stays on the default path, which costs nothing there because final_hc_post would materialize the state anyway. In “fused” mode the next layer copies residual.mean(dim=1) from the state the fused kernel has just materialized. With ..._EXACT=1 it first calls dspark_hc_post_mean, a Triton kernel (_dspark_hc_post_mean_kernel, blocks of 64 channels) that computes the mean of the equation above directly from (x, residual, post, comb), in FP32, without writing the 4 streams. Our interpretation: both modes compute the same quantity and differ only in rounding (the mean of a state already rounded to BF16, versus an FP32 sum rounded once). Both variables default to 0 in the Stage-C override.

Notice the cost of each strategy. GLM pays one extra post per tap and keeps the fusion. Default DeepSeek pays for two broken fusions (40→41 and 41→42), each with one extra isolated pre and one extra trip through the wide state. Exact deferred capture pays one extra read of the wide state per target (a separate kernel that rereads x, residual, post and comb), but it doesn't write the 4 streams, writes only 4,096 numbers per token and doesn't break the fusion.

Contract the way the drafter was trained

Why the mean, and not hc_head, or stream 0, or the sum? Because the drafter was trained on that contraction. The DSpark reference stores mean(H, streams) at layers 40–42, and GLM's hc_contract is also a mean. Any other choice produces 4096-wide vectors that look valid but have the wrong distribution. Nothing breaks, no exception is raised, only the acceptance rate drops. It is the kind of bug that only an A/B acceptance benchmark catches. For the same reason the speculative configuration goes into the compilation hash: capturing features changes the target's graph.

The same hook inside a purpose-built engine

The GLM·TF recipe has no hook patch. TensorFold writes GLM's forward by hand, and the DFlash2 hook is a stage of the glue itself. At each exchange point the engine applies hc_post and then, in this order, stores the drafter taps requested for that layer, the final stream mean (only after the last layer) and computes the next hc_pre. The tap is glue.stream_mean, the mean of the 4 streams: the same contraction as hc_contract, because the drafter is the same DFlash2, trained on means. With SPLIT=1, each rank computes the taps of its own rows and swaps them with the peer together with the normalized rows. The full recipe is in Swapping the engine.

Two details for reading the drafters' code. DSpark itself is built from V4 blocks with mHC: DeepSeekV4DSparkModel.draft repeats the embedding across hc_mult streams (x.unsqueeze(1).repeat(1, self.config.hc_mult, 1)), each DeepSeekV4DSparkLayer does mHC pre → DSpark attention → mHC post → mHC pre → MoE → mHC post (averaging the streams at the attention input when it arrives as 3D), and the head uses HCHeadOp. GLM's MTP layer, on the other hand, uses a plain residual, without mHC, and Qwen's MTP has its own GRs and mixer. To measure how much all of this costs on the target, VLLM_DSPARK_TARGET_TIMING=1 times the stages layer_attn_mhc, layer_ffn_mhc, dspark_capture, final_hc_post and hc_head_norm.

  1. Is the residual wide? Look for hc_mult, hc_count or mhc in config.json.
  2. Where does the state exist? Read the layer's forward: if it returns (hidden, residual, post, comb), the post is deferred.
  3. Materialize with the model's own function (layer.hc_post), never with a reimplementation of your own.
  4. Contract as in training (mean, hc_head or mixer, depending on the drafter).
  5. Gather the pieces the parallelism split (sp_all_gather under sequence parallel).
  6. Measure: acceptance rate and target time before and after, because breaking a fusion costs bandwidth.

The checkpoint MiaAI-Lab/Qwen3.8-Flash-Next-NVFP4-FP8dense keeps the experts in NVFP4 and converts 591 dense linears to FP8 E4M3 with a scale per output channel and activations quantized dynamically per token (FP8_PER_CHANNEL_PER_TOKEN). A per-output-channel scale means one scale per row of the weight matrix: each row is divided by its own maximum before becoming FP8. The activation gets a per-token scale on the fly (one per input row). Block scaling, by contrast, stores one number per tile of 128 rows × 128 columns, which requires both dimensions to be divisible by 128. The FP8/FP4 chapter details these formats. Three reasons not to use 128×128 blocks: per-channel scaling needs no calibration; GR's rank-320 bottleneck and the sharded shared expert (320 rows per GPU) are not divisible by 128; and the ParallelLMHead loader shards block scales incorrectly, while per-channel scales get sharded along with the weight. The hc_norm tensors stay in BF16 (the exclusion list also mentions hyper_connection_mixer.block_inject_weight, but the read-only mixer has no such tensor). Relative error measured over the 591 tensors: 0.0240 / 0.0262 / 0.0268 (min/median/max), with the small block_inject_weight gates as the worst ones, and cosine ≥ 0.9996. The ~1.5× gain at batch 1 is analytical, never measured on a GPU. The inventory also records integration rough edges: the apply_patches.py that generates the overlay's .py files is not versioned, there are two mounts on the same modelopt.py, and the overlay conflicts with MTP_DRAFT_VOCAB.

Replicating avoids communication (no all-reduce for GR), but each GPU pays the full bandwidth. It is the same trade-off the parallelism chapter discusses for routers, indexers and the KV latent.

In the GLM recipe

mHC doesn't appear in any vllm serve argument, and yet it shapes the recipe. The hc_* weights stay in full precision: EXL3 quantizes only the routed experts, which are 97% of the parameters. patch_glm_eagle3.py is baked into the image so that DFlash2 receives 4,096-wide hidden states from layers 5, 14, 24, 33 and 42. And the hc_post(ℓ) + hc_pre(ℓ+1) fusion explains why each layer's forward returns four tensors. Details in Rebuilding the GLM recipe.

In the GLM·TF recipe

The same mHC and the same checkpoint, on another engine. TensorFold keeps the 4 streams and the glue weights in BF16, as in the checkpoint, and has its own glue kernels: tensor cores in prefill and a 48-blocks-per-row CUDA hc_partial in decode. Between the two Sparks, the decode glue stays replicated, and the prefill glue is split by rows with SPLIT=1, on by default in the recipe, with the exchanges overlapped with the next block's front. The DFlash2 hook is the same mean of the 4 streams, computed inside the glue. Details in Swapping the engine; the bit contract that makes the split safe is in An exact engine.

In the DeepSeek recipe

In the default lane (Anemll 0.1.1, production) mHC runs on the mHC kernels of the image's vLLM, and hotfix #50312 tries to hand back the MTP buffer's 256 MiB. That image's code isn't in the repo. Our interpretation: the DSpark capture follows the same logic as the Stage-C overlay's default mode (flags off) and breaks the fusion at the 40→41 and 41→42 boundaries. The knobs VLLM_USE_B12X_MHC, B12X_MHC_MAX_TOKENS and VLLM_DSV4_DSPARK_DEFER_TARGET_CAPTURE(_EXACT) exist only in the Stage-C image (lane B, historical/optional). In the Anemll image, the ones starting with VLLM_ only trigger the “Unknown vLLM environment variable” warning; B12X_MHC_MAX_TOKENS, lacking that prefix, is simply ignored, with no warning at all. Details in Rebuilding the DeepSeek recipe.

In the Qwen recipe

GR is BF16, replicated, and costs 13% of the step. That is why it made it into FP8-dense's QUANT_PATTERNS (down, inject and up of both GRs; down and up of the mixer). And that is why two diffs of a few lines each were needed for vLLM to even consult the quantization method of these linears. Details in Rebuilding the Qwen recipe.
