In all three recipes there is a switch that speeds nothing up, saves no memory and touches no kernel: ABLIT=1 in GLM and Qwen, ABLITERATED=1 in DeepSeek. When it is on, it changes the model's behavior (safety refusals stop happening) without a single training step. In DeepSeek, the whole change is a vector of 4,096 numbers applied to a 284-billion-parameter model, and the file that stores it is 18 KiB (18,372 B: 16 KiB of fp32 data plus the pickle wrapper from torch.save). How can something so small change something so large? The answer is one of the most beautiful ideas in interpretability: many concepts live as directions in latent space, and a direction can be erased with a projection. In this chapter you will learn to find a direction, project it out of the activations or the weights, choose where to cut, distrust a direction that fails a statistical test and, above all, measure and document the price of altering what the model has learned.

Concepts are directions in the residual stream

Recall the Transformer block. Each token carries a vector \(h \in \mathbb{R}^d\), the residual stream, which runs through the stack of layers. No layer replaces that vector: each sublayer reads a normalized version of it and adds its own contribution, \(h \leftarrow h + \mathrm{Attn}(h)\) and then \(h \leftarrow h + \mathrm{FFN}(h)\). The addition is always done by an output matrix: the attention's o_proj (or the out_proj of a linear layer such as GDN), the down_proj of each MLP or expert and, at the very beginning, the embedding. Let's call these matrices residual writers.

In the three models, \(d\) is 4096 (GLM and DeepSeek) or 2560 (Qwen, per branch). With hyper-connections the residual becomes 4 streams (mHC in GLM and DeepSeek, Gated Residual in Qwen). Even so, each sublayer produces one output vector, which is spread across the streams with scalar weights. That is why “the direction of a layer's output” is still well defined, and that is what the recipes edit. (To read a 4-stream residual you need one more choice, which the next section explains.)

The linear representation hypothesis says that many concepts the model uses (“this is in French”, “this is a list”, “this request should be refused”) are encoded as directions in that space. To read a concept with unit direction \(u\), a dot product is enough: \(c = h\cdot u\) is the strength of the concept in that token. Writing the concept means adding a multiple of \(u\), and erasing it means removing the component along \(u\). This family of techniques, which reads and edits behaviors by manipulating directions, is called representation engineering.

Analogy

Picture the residual as a shared whiteboard. Each layer walks up, reads the board and adds scribbles with colored markers, without erasing anything. A concept is a marker color. The dot product works like a pair of glasses with a filter in that color: through them you only see how much red is on the board, and everything else becomes invisible. Abliterating means swapping, in some writers, the red marker for one that doesn't write (or one that writes “anti-red”).

In the figure below, a token climbs the stack. Each writer adds an arrow to the vector \(h\), tip to tail: the embedding, then the o_proj and the down_proj of each layer. The gold line is a direction \(r\), and the shadow of the tip of \(h\) on it is the reading \(h\cdot r\). Switch the kind of request and watch the bars at the bottom.

Notice two things. First, the reading along \(r\) is the sum of the readings of each writer, \(h\cdot r = \sum_k (\text{write}_k\cdot r)\). That's pure linearity. Second, in the toy, almost all of the rise for the request that triggers refusal comes from a few middle layers, and the biggest writer is attention. In a real model, the MLPs also write along \(r\), which is why classic abliteration also edits the down_proj matrices (we come back to this in the section on weights). If those writers stopped writing along \(r\), the concept would never accumulate. That is the whole plan of abliteration, and the rest of the chapter is engineering around it.

Deep dive: why so many directions fit

In 4096 dimensions, two random vectors are almost perpendicular. The cosine between them has mean 0 and standard deviation \(\approx 1/\sqrt{d} = 1/64 \approx 0.016\). This is concentration of measure. It is why a model can store far more “nearly orthogonal” concepts than it has dimensions (the idea of superposition, from Toy Models of Superposition, 2022). Keep this number in mind: later on, “random” will become a statistical yardstick for judging whether a published direction means anything.

Why this matters

None of this is training. There is no gradient, no loss function and no fine-tuning data (what learning is). The weights stay frozen, and the edit is linear algebra: done at load time (GLM), baked into a derived checkpoint (Qwen) or applied in the forward pass (DeepSeek). That bounds what the technique can do. It can only erase (or invert) something the model already represents as a direction, and that is why the data fits in 16 KiB: 4096 numbers in fp32.

Finding the direction: difference of means

How do you find the direction of a concept without training anything? The classic method is almost naively simple. Build two sets of prompts: \(\mathcal{D}^+\), requests the model refuses, and \(\mathcal{D}^-\), similarly shaped requests it fulfills. Run the forward pass on each prompt and save the residual vector \(h_\ell\) at a layer \(\ell\) and a fixed position. Typically that is the last token of the prompt, already wrapped in the chat template, where the model “decides” how to start its answer. (In a 4-stream residual, choosing which vector is \(h_\ell\) takes extra care, covered just below.) Then subtract the means:

\[ \mu^{\pm}_\ell = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}^{\pm}|}\sum_{x \in \mathcal{D}^{\pm}} h_\ell(x), \qquad r_\ell = \mu^{+}_\ell - \mu^{-}_\ell, \qquad \hat r_\ell = \frac{r_\ell}{\lVert r_\ell \rVert} \]
  • \(h_\ell(x)\): residual vector at layer \(\ell\), at the chosen position, for prompt \(x\)
  • \(\mu^{+}_\ell, \mu^{-}_\ell\): means over the refused (+) and fulfilled (−) requests
  • \(r_\ell\): the refusal direction obtained by difference of means
  • \(\hat r_\ell\): the same direction normalized (norm 1), ready to project

Why does the mean work? Everything that varies from prompt to prompt and has nothing to do with refusal (topic, length, language) shows up on both sides and vanishes in the subtraction. What remains is what separates the groups. It is a simple relative of linear discriminant analysis: no covariance to invert and nothing to optimize, just sums. The quality of the direction is measured by how well the projections \(s(x) = h_\ell(x)\cdot \hat r_\ell\) of the two groups separate:

\[ d'_\ell = \frac{\bar s^{+} - \bar s^{-}}{\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\sigma_{+}^2 + \sigma_{-}^2\right)}} \]
  • \(\bar s^{\pm}\): mean of each group's projections onto \(\hat r_\ell\)
  • \(\sigma_\pm\): standard deviation of those projections within each group
  • \(d'_\ell\): “distance in standard deviations” between the groups; the larger it is, the better the direction separates refusal from non-refusal

The figure walks through the whole procedure. The dots are activations from one layer, projected onto a plane (toy data). Use the stepper to follow the steps and the slider to change layers.

The last step previews the next section: when we remove from each point its component along \(\hat r\), the two clouds overlap along the direction that used to separate them, and all the rest of the structure (the vertical axis) is still there. Choosing the layer is empirical. You compute \(r_\ell\) for several layers, ablate each candidate and measure (1) how much refusal drops on a validation set and (2) how much the model drifts on neutral text. The candidate with the best trade-off between the two wins.

Deep dive: where the idea comes from

Zou et al. (2023) gave the family of techniques the name representation engineering. Turner et al. (2023) showed that adding a direction changes behavior (activation steering). Arditi et al. (2024) showed, across thirteen open chat models, that refusal is mediated by a single direction obtained exactly by difference of means: removing it makes the model stop refusing, and adding it makes the model refuse even harmless requests. The nickname abliteration (ablation + obliterate) caught on in the community in 2024 for the version that erases the direction from the weights.

Where to measure \(h_\ell\) in a 4-stream residual

So far, \(h_\ell\) has been “the residual vector”. In the recipe models, however, the per-token residual is a stack of 4 streams: \([4 \times 4096]\) in GLM and DeepSeek (mHC, hc_mult = 4) and \([4 \times 2560]\) in Qwen (Gated Residual). The previous section dealt with writing: each sublayer produces a vector and spreads it across the streams with scalar weights. To read, you need to decide which vector of \(d\) numbers goes into the mean. There are two good choices:

  1. The mean of the streams, \(\bar h_\ell = \tfrac14\sum_j H_{\ell,j}\). This is the same reading the model itself uses when it needs to “contract” the residual into a single vector: the hc_contract at the end of GLM, GLM's DFlash2 taps (which materialize the hc_post and take the mean) and the features DSpark takes from DeepSeek's layers 40–42 (mean(H, dim=streams)).
  2. The writer's output before spreading: the \(x\) that attention returns before hc_post. This is exactly the vector the DeepSeek hook projects. It measures what that writer writes at that layer, not what has accumulated up to that point.

But is the mean still additive if mHC's comb matrix mixes the streams at every sublayer? It is, and the math is short. The derivation is ours, based on the update formulas in the architecture inventories (GLM and DeepSeek). Each sublayer computes

\[ H'_i = \sum_{j} \mathrm{comb}_{ji}\,H_j + \mathrm{post}_i\,y \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \bar h' = \frac14\sum_j \Big(\underbrace{\textstyle\sum_i \mathrm{comb}_{ji}}_{=\,1}\Big) H_j + \bar p\, y = \bar h + \bar p\,y \]
  • \(H_j\): stream \(j\) (a vector of \(d\) numbers); \(\bar h\) is the mean of the 4
  • \(\mathrm{comb}\): doubly stochastic \(4\times 4\) matrix (Sinkhorn-Knopp, 20 iterations): rows and columns sum to 1
  • \(\mathrm{post}_i \in (0, 2)\): weight with which the sublayer output \(y\) is written into stream \(i\); \(\bar p = \tfrac14\sum_i \mathrm{post}_i\)
  • \(y\): the sublayer output (the \(x\) of attention or of the MoE, before hc_post)

All the math relies on is this: each source stream redistributes exactly 100% of what it holds (\(\sum_i \mathrm{comb}_{ji} = 1\)), and a doubly stochastic matrix guarantees that. With 20 Sinkhorn iterations the equality is approximate, but very good. Taking the dot product with \(r\), you are left with \(\bar h_\ell\cdot r = \bar h_0\cdot r + \sum_k \bar p_k\,(\text{write}_k\cdot r)\). Mixing changes which stream holds each piece, but it never rotates \(r\) into another direction, nor does it create or destroy mass along it. The weights \(\bar p_k\) are positive scalars that vary per token and per sublayer. In Qwen it is even simpler: Gated Residual writes \(R_i \leftarrow R_i + s_i\,y\) with no mixing matrix at all, and the mean accumulates \(\big(\tfrac14\sum_i s_i\big)\,y\). The figure shows the bookkeeping at work, and what goes wrong when the mixing is unconstrained (as in the original hyper-connections, before mHC).

In practice, the capture is a forward hook on the decoder block, run in a research script (PyTorch + transformers, outside vLLM). The sketch below is illustrative: module names and shapes vary from one implementation to another.

# illustrative sketch: capture h̄ (mean of the 4 streams) at the last template token
import torch
capt = {}                                            # layer -> [d] vector from the last forward
def tap(i):
    def hook(mod, args, out):
        H = out[0] if isinstance(out, tuple) else out     # mHC / GR: [B, T, 4, d]
        capt[i] = H[0, -1].float().mean(dim=0)            # last token, mean over streams
    return hook
for i, layer in enumerate(model.model.layers):
    layer.register_forward_hook(tap(i))

def activations(text):
    ids = tok.apply_chat_template([{"role": "user", "content": text}],
                                  add_generation_prompt=True, return_tensors="pt")
    with torch.no_grad():
        model(ids.to(model.device))      # the last position is where the answer starts
    return {i: v.clone() for i, v in capt.items()}

pos = [activations(a) for a, _ in pairs]   # pairs = [(refused, fulfilled), ...], same shape
neg = [activations(b) for _, b in pairs]
r = {i: torch.stack([p[i] for p in pos]).mean(0) - torch.stack([n[i] for n in neg]).mean(0)
     for i in capt}
r_hat = {i: v / v.norm() for i, v in r.items()}      # one candidate per layer

Three precautions make the difference. (1) Matched pairs: each refused request has a fulfilled sibling with the same format, length and template, changing only the content, so that the subtraction cancels everything that isn't refusal. (2) Normalize each \(r_\ell\) before comparing layers or projecting, because the scale of the residual changes from layer to layer, and the projection assumes \(\lVert \hat r\rVert = 1\). (3) Validate by ablation: apply the projection at runtime (as DeepSeek does) on a held-out set, measure how much refusal drops and how much the KLD rises on neutral text, and run the test against chance from the transplant section. For the second reading choice (the attention output), the hook goes on the attention module (self_attn), before hc_post.

Recovering the direction from a published edit

There is a second way to get a direction, and the recipes use it. Suppose someone published an edited checkpoint \(W_{\text{ed}}\) and you have the original \(W\). If the edit was a rank-1 projection, the difference \(\Delta W = W_{\text{ed}} - W = -\alpha\, r\,(r^\top W)\) has rank 1, and the first left singular vector of \(\Delta W\) (via SVD) is \(\pm\hat r\). The names of GLM's files tell that story: dealign-oproj-svd-L15-35-39-43-45 and blackfrost-oproj-svd are directions extracted by SVD from the o_proj matrices of published edits (reading the layers off the name is our interpretation). If the edit is not rank 1, the SVD only returns the best rank-1 approximation, and the direction may not capture what the edit does. Keep that caveat in mind for the transplant section.

In the recipes, the direction comes ready-made

direction_r1.pt is a torch pickle with the tensor broad, 4096-dimensional with norm 1, captured on the sibling model DeepSeek-V4-Flash-0731 (FP8, DSpark). refusal_direction_glm53_dealign_late.pt and refusal_direction_glm53_bf_oproj.pt carry a 4096 fp32 vector under the key directions plus metadata (the second one: alpha_ref 3.0, n_layers 43, skip_early_ref 2). the ABLIT=1 checkpoint uses a “house” projection with the axis recovered per QSA layer from the Dealign edit.

Pitfall: a direction belongs to a checkpoint

The direction is computed on a specific model and format. Using it on a sibling (another fine-tune, another quantization) is a hypothesis to be tested. DeepSeek's README calls the transfer of the direction captured on 0731 FP8 to the Vision-Exp that the recipe serves “experimental”. Fine-tuning usually preserves the residual basis, but nobody guarantees it; only measurement can tell.

Orthogonal projection: remove, reflect, exaggerate

With the unit direction \(v\) in hand, erasing the concept from a vector \(y\) means removing its shadow on \(v\). Orthogonal projection with adjustable strength is the equation that runs in DeepSeek:

\[ y' = y - \lambda\,(y\cdot v)\,v \;=\; \underbrace{\left(I - \lambda\, v v^\top\right)}_{P_\lambda}\,y, \qquad y'\cdot v = (1-\lambda)\,(y\cdot v) \]
  • \(y\): the activation about to be written into the residual (in the DeepSeek recipe, the attention output of a layer)
  • \(v\): unit direction (\(\lVert v\rVert = 1\)); the hotfix normalizes the vector from the file before using it
  • \(\lambda\): projection strength (DSV4_ABLATE_LAMBDA; in GLM the role is played by \(\alpha\) = ABLIT_ALPHA)
  • \(P_\lambda\): \(d\times d\) matrix; \(vv^\top\) is the rank-1 projector onto \(v\)

The math that matters is what happens to the coordinate: \(y'\cdot v = (1-\lambda)(y\cdot v)\). All the rest of \(y\), the part perpendicular to \(v\), passes through untouched. In terms of eigenvalues, \(P_\lambda\) is 1 on the whole hyperplane \(v^\perp\) (\(d-1\) dimensions) and \(1-\lambda\) along \(v\). Do the math by hand once. With \(v = (0.6,\ 0.8)\) and \(y = (2,\ 1)\): \(y\cdot v = 1.2 + 0.8 = 2\).

  • \(\lambda = 1\): \(y' = (2,1) - 2\,(0.6,0.8) = (0.8,\ -0.6)\), and \(y'\cdot v = 0.48 - 0.48 = 0\). The component is gone.
  • \(\lambda = 3.5\): \(y' = (2,1) - 7\,(0.6,0.8) = (-2.2,\ -4.6)\), and \(y'\cdot v = -1.32 - 3.68 = -5 = -2.5 \times 2\). The component flipped sign and became 2.5 times larger.

Notice that \(y'\) always slides along \(v\) and never leaves the line through \(y\): the projection does not invent new components, it only rescales one. With \(\lambda = 1\) the tip lands exactly on \(v^\perp\). With \(\lambda = 2\) it goes to the other side, at the same distance (it is a mirror). Beyond that, the image gets farther and farther from the original, and that is where the risk lies.

Why do the recipes exaggerate (\(\lambda > 1\))?

Removing the direction from one writer is not removing it from the residual. Other writers keep writing along \(r\): the MLPs, the layers left untouched, the embedding itself. With factor −2 (GLM) or −2.5 (DeepSeek), each edited writer starts writing the opposite of what it would have written, and that tends to cancel the contribution of the others. This reading is our interpretation, consistent with the comment in GLM's .env.example: α above 1 “over-projects: stronger, less coherent”. The price is pushing the activations away from the distribution seen in training. With too much strength the model degenerates, and DeepSeek documents the practical limit at λ ≥ 4 for long reasoning chains.

Applying twice is not applying once

Compute \(P_\alpha P_\alpha = I - (2\alpha - \alpha^2)\,vv^\top\). To be idempotent (\(P^2 = P\)) you need \(2\alpha - \alpha^2 = \alpha\), which only holds for \(\alpha \in \{0, 1\}\) (\(\alpha = 0\) is the identity). \(\alpha = 2\), on the other hand, is an involution (\(P^2 = I\)). With \(\alpha = 3\), applying the same projection twice (same \(v\), same \(\alpha\)) multiplies the component by \((1-3)^2 = 4\): the sign goes back to the original and the magnitude becomes four times larger. The moral holds for any recipe: an edit is not a switch you can flip “again” without consequences. Before editing a checkpoint, find out whether it has already been edited, and how. The real case of GLM's pre-edited preset is subtler than this math, and it comes up in the transplant section.

From activations to weights: orthogonalizing o_proj

The projection from the previous section acts on an activation, token by token. But if \(y\) is the output of a matrix, \(y = W_o z\) (with \(z\) the concatenation of the attention heads' outputs), associativity allows a trick: \(P(W_o z) = (P W_o)\,z\). Instead of projecting every token forever, we project the matrix once. This is weight orthogonalization, exactly the formula in GLM's ablit_runtime.py:

\[ W_o' = \left(I - \alpha\,\frac{r r^\top}{\lVert r\rVert^2}\right) W_o \;=\; W_o - \alpha\, \frac{r\,(r^\top W_o)}{\lVert r\rVert^2}, \qquad r^\top W_o' = (1-\alpha)\; r^\top W_o \]
  • \(W_o\): the o_proj, with shape \([d_{\text{out}} \times d_{\text{in}}]\); the rows are the residual dimensions
  • \(r\): refusal direction in \(\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{out}}}\) (it doesn't need to be unit length, because the formula divides by \(\lVert r\rVert^2\))
  • \(r^\top W_o\): a \(1 \times d_{\text{in}}\) row vector that says how much each input (each channel of each head) writes along \(r\)
  • \(\alpha\): ABLIT_ALPHA (3.0 by default; 1.0 = simple projection)

The last equality is the property we care about. With \(\alpha = 1\), the matrix becomes blind to \(r\): whatever the heads compute, the layer can no longer write along \(r\). With \(\alpha = 3\), it writes −2 times what it would have written. The real shapes are large, but the edit is cheap: one GEMV and one outer product per matrix, done once, in fp32, with the result converted back to BF16.

Follow the math cell by cell on a toy matrix: 4 residual dimensions (rows) by 6 inputs (columns), with \(r = (0.6,\ 0,\ 0.8,\ 0)\). Change \(\alpha\) and step through. The last step shows the same computation split across two ranks.

Note how the correction \(\Delta W = \alpha\, r \otimes (r^\top W)\) is an outer product: each row \(i\) of \(W\) receives \(\alpha\, r_i\) times the same row \(r^\top W\). Whatever lives outside \(r\) (rows 1 and 3) feels nothing. That is why GLM's log can print a single control number per layer, residual_max = \(\max\lvert r^\top W'\rvert\).

Why tensor parallelism doesn't get in the way

With tensor parallelism, o_proj is a RowParallelLinear: the input dimension (the heads) is sliced across the ranks, and each rank holds \(W_k\) with all 4096 output rows and a slice of the columns. Each rank computes a partial result \(W_k z_k\), and an all-reduce sums the partials. Since \(P\) multiplies from the left,

\[ P\Big(\sum_k W_k z_k\Big) = \sum_k (P W_k)\, z_k \]
  • \(W_k\): the column slice on rank \(k\) (in GLM with TP=2, [4096 × 4096] in the KDA layers and [4096 × 8192] in the DSA layers)
  • \(z_k\): the heads that live on rank \(k\)
  • \(P\): the same projector on every rank, because \(r\) is replicated

Each rank edits its own slice with the same formula, with no communication at all, and the all-reduce that already existed sums partials that are already edited. That is what GLM's docstring says: “no collective is needed”. TP=3 has a wrinkle (TP=3 and divisibility): the 64 heads are zero-padded to 66 (22 per rank), and the transplant mirrors that pad, appending zeroed columns at the end of the donor tensor's input dimension before slicing.

When to edit: before quantizing, before capturing

In GLM, the hook runs at the end of Glm5NextModel.load_weights (layers 0–44) and of Glm5NextMTP.load_weights (block 45). At that point o_proj is still the native BF16 parameter. Next comes process_weights_after_loading. With GLM53_DENSE_FP8 on (the .env.example uses all), that is where the dense projections are quantized per output channel to FP8 e4m3 and repacked for Marlin, and the kda and mla groups include o_proj. Last comes the capture of the CUDA graphs. The order guarantees three things: the edit happens in high precision, quantization happens only once, on the edited weight, and the graphs capture the final model. If o_proj is already quantized to EXL3 (pack non_routed_exl3), there is no BF16 weight to edit. The runtime then fails loudly (AblitError), and the launcher requires ABLIT=0 with GLM53_DENSE_EXL3=1.

Classic abliteration vs. the recipes' version

Classic orthogonalization edits all the writers (embedding, every o_proj, every down_proj), so that no component can write \(r\). The recipes edit only the attention writers, in chosen layers, and compensate with \(\alpha > 1\). That is cheaper and less invasive, and it leaves the MoE experts intact, which account for ~96–97% of the parameters and are quantized to EXL3/NVFP4 (the three models). Editing the experts would require dequantizing, editing and requantizing thousands of matrices, adding quantization error on top of the edit's effect.

Where to cut: target layers, anchors and drafters

Choosing which layers to edit is half the work. The three recipes make different choices, and all of them follow the same logic. The first layers build basic features (token identity, local syntax), and the refusal direction forms in the middle of the stack. Touching things too early risks coherence, so the initial layers stay untouched as anchor layers. The drafters used for speculative decoding call for a separate decision.

DeepSeek: hook on 10–42, outside DSpark

DSV4_ABLATE_LAYERS=10-42 applies the projection after attention in 33 of the 43 trunk layers. Our interpretation: layers 0–9 play the role of anchors (the name of the repository the direction comes from, “…-Anchored-Tensors”, suggests as much). The launcher rejects any range that goes past 42, because indices 43–45 are the three stages of the DSpark drafter. But DSpark reads the mean of the 4 streams from layers 40–42, which are inside the edited range. The drafter was trained on the original features and now receives edited features. scripts/EVAL.md sums it up by saying that abliteration “drains” acceptance (0.585 versus 0.718), but look closely at what those numbers are: 0.585 was measured with the abliterated Keys checkpoint (Stage-C lane, C1) and 0.718 comes from a different probe (NVFP4 1M, p256/g256), with a different configuration. It is not a controlled A/B of the runtime hook on Vision-Exp with taps 40–42. The causal link is our interpretation; when you turn on ABLITERATED=1, measure again with spec-acceptance.py.

Why the drafter enters the picture

With rejection-sampling verification, the final distribution is the target's, so a stock drafter cannot “reintroduce” refusals into an edited target under exact verification. The cost shows up in speed: proposals that the edited target rejects are wasted work. That is why GLM edits the MTP along with the rest and DeepSeek measures acceptance. In a recipe of your own, any edit to the target calls for a fresh acceptance measurement (MTP and EAGLE, DFlash2 and DSpark).

Transplant and the random-vector test

Now for an episode of real science, recorded in GLM's repository. The recipe shipped two published directions and the proj method ready to go. Before trusting them, someone asked the right question: do these directions have anything to do with what the stock o_proj writes? The statistic chosen was \(\max_j \lvert r^\top W_{:,j}\rvert\), the largest weight with which any input of o_proj writes along \(r\). The result: 0.084 for the published directions and 0.084 for random unit vectors, with identical means. Statistically, the directions were indistinguishable from chance. Consistent with that, the author's own table shows the projection variants reaching at most 9/32 on the 32-request test (refusal32), while the byte-for-byte copy of the edited weights reaches 32/32.

The test: compare against a null distribution

The reasoning is that of any hypothesis test. The null hypothesis says that \(r\) is nothing special, just an arbitrary unit vector. Draw \(N\) random unit vectors \(u_1, \dots, u_N\), compute the same statistic \(s(u_i)\) for each of them and obtain the Monte Carlo null distribution. Then compare \(s(r)\) against it:

\[ p \approx \frac{1 + \#\{\,i : s(u_i) \ge s(r)\,\}}{1 + N}, \qquad z = \frac{s(r) - \text{mean}\,(s(u))}{\text{sd}\,(s(u))} \]
  • \(s(\cdot)\): the statistic (here \(\max_j \lvert u^\top W_{:,j}\rvert\); it could also be \(\lVert u^\top W\rVert\) or the cosine with a reference direction)
  • \(u_i\): normalized Gaussian vectors, uniform on the sphere
  • \(p\): fraction of random vectors at least as extreme as \(r\); a large \(p\) means “cannot be told apart from chance”
  • \(z\): how many standard deviations \(r\) sits above the null cloud

The figure builds this test on a toy \(64 \times 96\) matrix in which six columns (“heads”) write strongly along a planted direction \(r^\star\). Compare \(r^\star\) with a candidate whose cosine with \(r^\star\) you control, and with a purely random vector. In “cosine” mode, raise the dimension to 4096 and watch the null cloud shrink to \(\pm 1/\sqrt d\).

Play with the candidate's cosine and compare the two modes. In the toy (\(d = 64\), statistic \(\max_j\lvert u^\top W_{:,j}\rvert\)), the candidate carries only \(c\) times the planted signal, while the noise in the statistic (the maximum, over 96 columns, of random projections) stays the same size. That is why it only separates from the cloud at \(c \gtrsim 0.4\) (the exact threshold varies with the draw; press ↻). With a small \(c\), it can even fall below the null mean: at \(d = 64\), a random vector already has a typical \(\lvert\cos\rvert\) of \(\approx 1/\sqrt{64} \approx 0.12\) with \(r^\star\), more than 0.05. In cosine mode with \(d = 4096\), the cloud shrinks to \(\pm 1/\sqrt d \approx 0.016\), and even \(\cos = 0.05\) becomes \(z \approx 4\). In high dimension, pointing slightly in the right direction is statistically detectable.

But passing the test is not enough, because what matters is the size of the effect. Projecting along the candidate \(u = c\,r^\star + \sqrt{1-c^2}\,q\) with strength \(\alpha\) leaves, in the direction that matters,

\[ r^\star\cdot y' = \left(1 - \alpha c^2\right)(r^\star\cdot y) \;-\; \alpha\, c\sqrt{1-c^2}\,(q\cdot y) \]
  • \(c\): cosine between the candidate and the true direction \(r^\star\)
  • \(q\): the part of the candidate perpendicular to \(r^\star\), unrelated to the concept
  • with \(c = 0.05\) and \(\alpha = 3\), the component along \(r^\star\) drops by only 0.75%, and almost the whole edit (factor −2) lands on \(q\)

A weakly aligned candidate is therefore almost all damage: it pushes hard on a direction unrelated to refusal and barely touches the one that matters. (This is our reading, consistent with the README's warning that the projection fallback “garbles sampled output”.) The test against chance is a necessary filter, not a certificate of usefulness, and after it comes the behavioral measurement, like GLM's 9/32. Why did GLM's published directions fail? These are our hypotheses, not a diagnosis from the repository: a vector extracted by SVD from an edit that isn't rank 1, or an average of directions that vary per layer, can end up somewhere meaningless for the stock o_proj. The methodological lesson holds for any recipe: before applying a third-party direction, test it against chance and then measure its effect.

Transplant: copy instead of compute

If the direction doesn't explain the edit, GLM's solution is to stop depending on it. Weight transplant copies, byte for byte, the already-edited o_proj tensors from a donor checkpoint, dealignai/GLM-5.3-Flash-UNCENSORED-NVFP4 (ABLIT_DONOR). The copy works because o_proj is native BF16 in both NVFP4 and EXL3 (quant_ignore_includes_o_proj: true in the map), and the stock o_proj is byte-identical between the NVFP4 body and the EXL3 body. The only difference between the donor and our checkpoint, in those tensors, is the edit itself. Swapping just those 31 tensors in the EXL3 body reproduces the published edit exactly: the “dealign-oproj-transplant” method. ABLIT_METHOD=auto picks the transplant whenever ablit/transplant/ (inside ABLIT_DIR=/opt/glm53/ablit) has the MANIFEST.json and the tensors. Only when they are missing does the method fall back to proj.

And how do you fetch 31 tensors without downloading the whole checkpoint? The safetensors format helps: each shard starts with 8 little-endian bytes giving the size \(N\) of a JSON header, and the header lists every tensor with its dtype, shape and data_offsets. With three HTTP range requests per tensor, fetch_transplant.py reads the 8 bytes, reads the header and downloads only the byte range of the o_proj.

The byte math: 22 KDA × 64 MiB + 9 DSA × 128 MiB = 2,560 MiB ≈ 2.68 GB. The README says “~2.7 GiB”, which matches the math if read as GB. That is far less than the donor's ~164 GiB. The script writes each tensor to L{NN}.bin through a temporary file and an atomic replace, records the SHA-256 in MANIFEST.json and, when resuming, skips whatever already checks out. At load time, the runtime checks the byte count, the BF16 dtype and shape compatibility with TP. It then logs, per layer, the relative change \(\mathrm{rel}_{\ell_2} = \lVert W_{\text{donor}} - W\rVert / \lVert W\rVert\). It works as a fingerprint: the measured mean is ≈ 0.126, equal to the published “Mean Δrel 0.126”, and layer 44 gives 0.737 against 0.74 published.

Deep dive: what the fingerprint suggests

A back-of-the-envelope estimate (our interpretation). A rank-1 projection with \(\alpha = 3\) changes \(W\) by \(\lVert\Delta W\rVert_F/\lVert W\rVert_F = \alpha\lVert \hat r^\top W\rVert/\lVert W\rVert_F\). For a typical (random) direction, \(\lVert \hat r^\top W\rVert \approx \lVert W\rVert_F/\sqrt d\), and the ratio comes out at \(\approx \alpha/\sqrt{d} = 3/64 \approx 0.047\). The published directions measured the same as chance, so projecting them would change \(W\) by only about 0.047. The donor changes it by 0.126 on average (0.74 at layer 44): its edit was made along something that o_proj actually writes, and none of the published vectors reproduces it. This does not prove the edit isn't rank 1. Along a direction that o_proj writes strongly (close to the first singular vector of \(W\)), the ratio reaches \(\alpha\,\sigma_{\max}/\lVert W\rVert_F\), which can exceed 0.74. To settle it, take the SVD of \(\Delta W = W_{\text{donor}} - W\) using the transplant's own tensors: the fraction \(\sigma_1^2/\sum_i \sigma_i^2\) tells you how much of the edit fits in rank 1.

The pre-edited checkpoint (the abliterated preset)

GLM's third route is to download a checkpoint that comes already edited. start-abliterated.sh just exports GLM53_MODEL_PRESET=abliterated and calls start.sh. The preset pins bullerwins/GLM-5.3-Flash-exl3-4bpw-ablit@14858211ed81d7fa773f8a0db02f38f36d230252, 120 shards (175,642,157,752 bytes), and points the fallback at the same repository. That way, an incomplete download fails instead of silently bringing up the original model (fail-closed). The preset also forces ABLIT=0, overriding any value from the caller (start.sh): these weights already contain the transplant in layers 15–43 and in MTP 45, while layer 44 came from the EXL3 parent, and reapplying the runtime edit “would produce a different model”, in the README's words. It is worth understanding why, and the reason is not the \(P_\alpha^2\) math from the projection section: this checkpoint was made by transplant, not by projection along \(r\). With ABLIT_METHOD=auto and the tensors downloaded, the actual path would be the transplant again. In layers 15–43 and 45 it would, in principle, copy the same bytes, but it would replace layer 44, which the preset left as it came from the parent, with the donor's tensor (precisely the \(\mathrm{rel}_{\ell_2}\) outlier at 0.737). With proj, it would stack a second edit, different from the first: factor −2 along \(r\) on top of weights that were already transplanted. Either way, you get a different model.

Pitfall: the silent fallback

With ABLIT=1 and ABLIT_METHOD=auto, if ablit/transplant/ is empty the runtime falls back to projection with the published directions, and the README warns that this “garbles sampled output”. Check the log for the line ABLIT_METHOD=auto -> transplant. In a recipe of your own, prefer an explicit ABLIT_METHOD=transplant when the transplant is what you want, because then missing tensors become an error.

Runtime ablation: the DeepSeek hook

DeepSeek chose the path that touches no weights at all. With ABLITERATED=1, the recipe keeps serving the official checkpoint (DSPARK_MODEL is forced to deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V4-Flash-Vision-Exp) and applies directional ablation in the forward pass, using the 18 KiB direction. The advantages are clear: no need to download the 157 GiB Keys checkpoint, full reversibility (ABLITERATED=0 and the containers recreated) and no requantization. In DeepSeek-V4 the attention output projections (wo_a, wo_b) are in FP8, and editing them would require dequantizing and requantizing; that motivation is our interpretation. By associativity, and as long as nothing nonlinear comes after wo_b inside attention, projecting the output is in principle equivalent to orthogonalizing the rows of wo_b, the last linear matrix before the write.

What the hotfix injects

patches/hotfix-dsv4-runtime-ablation.py always runs. With ABLATE=0 it only checks that model.py is stock. If it finds the patch marker, it refuses and tells you to recreate the container, because a restart keeps already-patched bytes in the writable layer. With ABLATE=1, it validates the direction (4096 dimensions in broad or in directions[0], finite values, norm greater than zero) and performs a narrow, fail-closed code transformation: exact anchors, a number of hook points in {1, 2, 3} (1 in the Anemll lane, up to 3 in Stage-C), compile() of the result and an atomic os.replace. The heart of it is this method, called right after each x = self.attn(positions, x, None), before the hc_post that spreads the output across the 4 mHC streams:

def _ablate_refusal_direction(self, x):
    """Apply y <- y - lambda * (y dot v) * v in fp32."""
    direction = self._refusal_dir
    projection = (x.to(torch.float32) @ direction).unsqueeze(-1)
    return x - (self._ablate_lambda * projection * direction).to(x.dtype)

Three engineering details deserve attention. (1) Every decoder layer gets the _refusal_dir buffer (fp32, 4096, non-persistent), even outside the range. In the layers outside it, the buffer stays zeroed with \(\lambda = 0\), so that all layers share a single torch.compile/AOT signature. (2) The math is done in fp32 and converted back, so the coordinate isn't lost to BF16 rounding. (3) Since the attention output has already gone through the all-reduce of wo_b, every rank has the same \(x\) and applies the same projection, as long as it has the same \(v\).

The per-token cost is negligible. Per layer, it is \(2\cdot 4096\) FLOPs for the dot product and another \(2\cdot 4096\) for the subtraction; across the 33 layers, \(\approx 0.54\) MFLOP per token. That is roughly \(2\times 10^{-5}\) of the ~26 GFLOP of a token with ~13.2 B active parameters (our math). The real price lies elsewhere: in the compilation cache and in the integrity of the vector.

The AOT cache stamp

The hook changes the model's code, and \(\lambda\), the layers and the direction become part of what gets compiled. A torch_compile_cache generated with a different configuration would be wrongly reused. That is why the hotfix keeps an AOT stamp at VLLM_CACHE_ROOT/.dsv4_ablate_stamp, a string with the version, enabled, \(\lambda\), the layers, the direction's SHA-256 and the patch's own SHA-256. If the stamp changes, the cache is deleted and recompiled. In GLM this isn't needed: the edit changes data (the weights) before capture, and the compiled code is the same with or without ABLIT.

The launcher's gates

The launcher treats ablation as a dangerous operation, with gates in sequence. ABLITERATED is the product switch, and ABLATE is internal: ABLATE=1 on its own aborts with exit 2, and so does any value other than 0/1. The range must match ^N-M$, with \(N \le M \le 42\). \(\lambda\) must be finite and \(\ge 0\). RESPONSIBLE_USE.md and the direction must be in the cache (prepare-dspark-model-cache.sh --abliterated downloads them from gated repositories, using HF_TOKEN), and the file's SHA-256 must be 6e4d8a8f…. Only then does staging copy the direction to the head, the worker and, with TP=3, worker2, always via a temporary file, a SHA-256 check and an atomic mv. Finally, the entrypoint exports DSV4_ABLATE_FILE=/cache/huggingface/dspark-ablation/direction_r1.pt for the hotfix.

Why so much ceremony over 18 KiB? Because the failure is silent. If the worker had a truncated or different copy of the direction, each rank would project \(x\) with a different \(v\). The ranks' residual streams would diverge, and the next TP operations would mix incompatible states. The result would be a model that answers fluently and wrongly, without a single error in the log. Checking the SHA-256 on every node turns that risk into a loud exit 1. Reading the file with torch.load(..., weights_only=True) prevents a malicious pickle from executing code (checkpoints and integrity).

The price: measure, document and take responsibility

Abliterating means altering what the model has learned, and every edit has two effects: the intended one (the refusal goes away) and the collateral one (the rest of the behavior shifts a little, or a lot). A serious researcher measures both. The main tool for the collateral effect is the Kullback-Leibler divergence (KLD) between the next-token distributions of the original and the edited model, computed on neutral text. The chapter measuring the damage goes deeper; here is a preview:

\[ \mathrm{KL}(p\,\Vert\,q) = \sum_{t \in \mathcal{V}} p(t)\,\log\frac{p(t)}{q(t)}, \qquad \overline{\mathrm{KL}} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{i=1}^{T} \mathrm{KL}\big(p_i \,\Vert\, q_i\big) \]
  • \(p_i\): next-token distribution of the original model at position \(i\) (the reference)
  • \(q_i\): the same distribution for the edited model
  • \(\mathcal{V}\): the vocabulary; \(T\): number of positions evaluated in the neutral corpus
  • KL = 0 when the distributions match; it grows when \(q\) takes probability away from where \(p\) puts it

The figure is a toy balance scale. On the left pan, how much of the probability mass on refusal tokens was removed for a sensitive request (the intended effect). On the right pan, the KLD on a neutral text (the collateral). Move \(\lambda\) and watch both pans change together.

Notice the shape. Refusal removal saturates near \(\lambda \approx 1\text{–}1.5\), while the KLD on neutral text keeps rising. Past a certain point, each extra unit of \(\lambda\) buys almost no intended effect and a lot of damage. The shape is qualitative, but it is exactly the trade-off that the recipes' values of 3.0 and 3.5 choose with open eyes, and the reason DeepSeek warns about λ ≥ 4.

What the recipes measured, and what they didn't

  • The KLD panel in the README was measured without ablit, and the KLD with ablit was not measured. The warning is explicit: “expect behavioral drift”; rerun the decode benchmark, because DFlash2's acceptance may change. For the pre-edited checkpoint, the model card reports a KLD against the teacher that is higher than the original's, and the README concludes that it is “an alternative checkpoint, not a quality-equivalent replacement”. The same README records a historical test by the author: 32/32 with no refusal, 0 refusals and 0 corrupted outputs in a greedy run, without reasoning.
  • EVAL.md records that abliteration drains the drafter's acceptance (0.585 with the abliterated Keys checkpoint versus 0.718 from a different probe, with no controlled A/B of the runtime hook). The audit's C1 decode came in at 73.1 tok/s with the abliterated Keys checkpoint versus 75.5 with the official one. \(\lambda \ge 4\) risks making long reasoning chains degenerate, and the 0731 → Vision-Exp transfer is experimental.
  • The checkpoint is treated as a derivative: it is only valid in this recipe's NVFP4 dual-Spark layout (not MXFP8 single-Spark, not official FP8, not FP8_DENSE). And its config.json labels the MTP experts as FP8_PB_WO, a label that patch_checkpoint_config.py normalizes.

Responsibility: the safeguards pass to you

A model without refusals doesn't become “free”: the responsibility for what it does changes hands. Qwen's README says so bluntly: the safeguards pass to the operator, and filtering, human review and access control are up to you. The risk is greater because start.sh exposes the server on 0.0.0.0, and anything that can reach the port reaches an unfiltered model (API authentication). The gated repositories make this formal. Requesting access means accepting terms: as summarized in Qwen's README, being 18 or older, being liable for any harm caused by what is generated, and respecting a list of prohibited uses and the upstream licenses (NVIDIA Open Model License, Qwen Community License). In DeepSeek, the boot doesn't even start without RESPONSIBLE_USE.md in the cache. In GLM, the donor's weights come with their own redistribution terms.

Why a researcher studies this

Abliteration is, first and foremost, a scientific instrument. It shows that an entire safety behavior can hinge on a single direction, in other words, that safety tuning can be shallow. That motivates more robust training, adversarial evaluations (red teaming) and defenses that don't rely on a single direction. It is also a way to study interpretability, by checking whether what the model represents matches what it does. Treating the technique as an experiment demands what any experiment demands: a hypothesis, measurement, a control and a written record.

In practice, document every edit in an edit record that travels with the checkpoint or the recipe:

edit:
  method: transplant | proj | runtime | derived-checkpoint
  target: o_proj of layers 15-45 (incl. MTP)    # or: attention output, 10-42
  direction: {source: ..., file: ..., sha256: ..., dim: 4096, norm: 1}
  strength: {alpha: 3.0}                        # or lambda: 3.5
  anchors: 0-14
  drafter: {mtp: edited, dflash2: untouched, acceptance_before: ..., after: ...}
measurements:
  kld_neutral_text: ...                         # against the original model
  refusal_rate: {before: ..., after: ..., set: ...}
  long_cot_degeneration: ...
  date: ..., image: ..., checkpoint_revision: ...
responsible_use: {terms: ..., access: authenticated, exposure: internal-network}

The DSPARK_MODEL_ABLITERATED id, in turn, must not point to the abliterated 0731 dump, which loses image_url (DeepSeek README). HF_TOKEN authenticates the gated repositories: ABLIT=1 ./download.sh in Qwen fails right away without it, and a 403 means the terms have not been accepted yet. In Qwen, ABLIT and HF_TOKEN are the only two keys where the environment wins over .env, and the checkpoint selection order is FP8_DENSE=true > OVERRIDE_MODEL_ID > ABLIT=1 > MODEL_ID.

ParameterABLIT_METHOD=auto

This default is a scientific decision baked into a setting. Projection is the “textbook” technique, but the available directions failed the test against chance, and projection stalled at 9/32. The transplant reproduces the measured edit (32/32 on the donor's test and a rel_l2 that matches the published one) at the cost of a ~2.7 GB download. auto prefers the transplant when the tensors exist and keeps proj for anyone who extracts their own directions. If you extract one, test it against chance before trusting it.

ParameterABLIT=1

Here the edit was made by a third party and arrives as a whole checkpoint (~133 GB, ≈124 GiB), side by side with the stock one in the cache. Switching is just a matter of flipping the flag, and start.sh syncs the chosen checkpoint to the worker (or shares it over NFS). Since only 12 tensors differ (~360 MiB), the download cost is almost entirely redundant. That is the price of needing no hook at all in vLLM: the engine serves an ordinary checkpoint.

ParameterDSV4_ABLATE_LAMBDA=3.5

With a unit \(v\), the component becomes \(1 - 3.5 = -2.5\) times the original, which the self-test selftest-runtime-ablation.py checks numerically. The value sits below the practical limit of 4, above which long reasoning degenerates. Changing λ requires no download and no rebuild, but it does require recreating both ranks, and the stamp wipes the compilation cache. It is cheap to experiment with and expensive to forget to measure.

  1. Map the writers. List, per layer, the matrices that write into the residual (o_proj, out_proj, down_proj), with their shape, dtype and whether they are quantized. That is your LAYER_MAP.json.
  2. Get the direction and test it. Capture \(h_\ell\) with a forward hook at the last template token (in a 4-stream residual, the mean of the streams or the attention output before hc_post), take the difference of means between matched pairs and normalize. Or take a published direction. Either way, test it against random vectors and validate it by ablation before using it.
  3. Choose where to apply it. BF16 weights at load time (no per-token cost, no cache invalidation), a derived checkpoint (no code in the engine) or a runtime hook (reversible, but it touches the compiled graph).
  4. Choose the layers and the strength. Leave anchors at the start, decide what to do with the MTP/drafter and start with a small α or λ.
  5. Guarantee integrity. SHA-256 of the vector on every node, weights_only=True, fail loudly if anything is missing, and no silent fallback.
  6. Measure and document. KLD on neutral text, refusal rate, degeneration on long CoT, drafter acceptance and tok/s, all written down in the edit record.

One detail from this section opens the next part of the trail. GLM's edit happens in BF16, before o_proj is quantized to FP8, and DeepSeek preferred not to touch the projections that are already in FP8. Why quantize these weights, and why is editing a quantized weight so delicate? That is the subject of the next chapter, why and how to quantize.
