In 2022, human evaluators preferred the outputs of a 1.3-billion-parameter model over those of the 175-billion-parameter GPT-3, a model 100× larger. The difference was not size but three post-training stages that became known as RLHF: SFT, a reward model and, at the end, a reinforcement learning algorithm called PPO. And it was cheap: PPO-ptx for the 175B model cost 60 petaflop/s-days, versus 3,640 for GPT-3's pretraining. This chapter opens PPO up from the inside (the probability ratio, the clip, the critic, the entropy, the epochs) and then builds the system around it: the reward held in check by a KL leash, the four models in memory, the inference engine generating responses inside the training loop and the tricks that separate a stable run from a collapse. By the end, you will be able to read and write the config of a PPO-RLHF run, estimate whether it fits on your machine and diagnose what went wrong when the reward curve lies.

Why PPO exists: reuse the batch without falling off the cliff

Start with what you already have from chapter 54. An LLM generating a response is a policy \(\pi_\theta\). The state is the prompt plus the prefix generated so far, the action is the next token, and the reward arrives almost entirely at the end. The policy gradient says how to move the weights to increase the expected reward:

\[ \begin{gathered} \nabla_\theta J = \mathbb E_{x\sim\mathcal D,\ y\sim\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)}\Big[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\hat A_t \\ \cdot\,\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_t\mid x, y_{<t})\Big] \end{gathered} \]
  • \(x\): prompt drawn from the set \(\mathcal D\); \(y = (y_1,\dots,y_T)\): response sampled from the policy itself
  • \(\hat A_t\): estimated advantage of token \(t\), that is, how much better (positive) or worse (negative) than expected it was at that point
  • \(\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_t\mid\cdot)\): the same log-probability gradient as in SFT (chapter 52)

A useful reading (it is an intuition, not a theorem): the formula is SFT with weights. Tokens with positive advantage become more likely; tokens with negative advantage, less likely. The detail that will change everything sits in the subscript of the expectation: \(y\sim\pi_\theta\). The gradient only holds for responses sampled from the current policy. And generating responses is the expensive part of RLHF (the section on the inference engine measures how expensive). Throwing away a batch of 512 responses after a single gradient step would be an enormous waste. We want to take several steps with the same batch.

The catch is that, after the first step, the weights are no longer the ones that generated the batch. Call \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\) the policy that generated the responses. To use samples from one distribution as if they came from another, there is importance sampling, and the weight of each token is the probability ratio:

\[ \begin{aligned} \rho_t(\theta) &= \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t\mid s_t)} \\ L^{CPI}(\theta) &= \hat{\mathbb E}_t\big[\rho_t(\theta)\,\hat A_t\big] \end{aligned} \]
  • \(s_t = (x, y_{<t})\) and \(a_t = y_t\): state and action of token \(t\)
  • \(\rho_t\): how much the current policy likes the sampled token, relative to the policy that sampled it; it is exactly 1 before the first step
  • \(L^{CPI}\): the surrogate objective inherited from TRPO (conservative policy iteration); \(\hat{\mathbb E}_t\) is the average over the tokens of the batch

At \(\theta = \theta_{\text{old}}\), the gradient of \(L^{CPI}\) is exactly the policy gradient, because \(\nabla\rho = \rho\,\nabla\log\pi\) and \(\rho = 1\). Far from there, the approximation rots. The advantages were measured under \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\), and the per-token ratio ignores that the new policy would also visit other prefixes. The PPO paper sums it up: without a constraint, maximizing \(L^{CPI}\) leads to excessively large updates. And why is the ratio per token rather than per sequence? Because the product of hundreds of ratios explodes. With four tokens whose ratios are 1.20, 0.96, 5.00 and 0.99, the product is already 5.70, dominated by the single rare token on which the two policies disagree (illustrative example).

TRPO (Schulman and colleagues, 2015) solved this with a trust region: maximize \(L^{CPI}\) subject to \(\hat{\mathbb E}_t[D_{KL}(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\,\|\,\pi_\theta)]\le\delta\), with \(\delta = 0.01\) in every experiment in the paper. It works, but each update requires products with the Fisher matrix, a conjugate gradient and a line search, over billions of parameters, and the method combines poorly with minibatches, dropout and shared parameters. PPO (Schulman, Wolski, Dhariwal, Radford and Klimov, 2017) wants the same effect with an ordinary first-order optimizer, such as Adam. The figure shows the problem and the solution on a three-token policy.

Run both modes with the positive advantage. Without the clip, ten steps take the ratio of the sampled token from 1 to nearly 2 (with the default step) and mass keeps migrating to it, all based on a single batch and a single noisy advantage estimate. With the clip, the steps continue until \(\rho\) goes past \(1+\varepsilon\); from then on, that token's gradient is zero and the bars freeze. Notice that they freeze slightly above \(1+\varepsilon\): the last step started inside the band and ended outside it. The clip is not a wall, it is an incentive cut. In a real network the weights are still shared, and the gradient from other tokens keeps moving the probability of a token that is already clipped. That is why, in practice, people still monitor the KL between the policies and take few steps per batch.

Analogy: the speed limiter

\(L^{CPI}\) is a car without a speed limiter: the gradient says “accelerate”, and it accelerates until it leaves the road, which here is the region where the batch still describes the policy. TRPO is an engineer who, at every curve, computes the maximum safe speed by solving a linear system (the conjugate gradient) and then also tests the brakes (the line search). PPO is the electronic limiter: once past \(1+\varepsilon\), the pedal simply stops responding. It is crude, but it is cheap and it works with any optimizer.

The clipped objective, case by case

Here is the objective that gave the algorithm its name (eq. 7 of the PPO paper):

\[ \begin{aligned} &L^{CLIP}(\theta) = \hat{\mathbb E}_t\Big[\min\big(\rho_t(\theta)\,\hat A_t, \\ &\qquad\operatorname{clip}(\rho_t(\theta),\,1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon)\,\hat A_t\big)\Big] \end{aligned} \]
  • \(\operatorname{clip}(\rho, 1-\varepsilon, 1+\varepsilon)\): clamps \(\rho\) inside the interval \([1-\varepsilon,\,1+\varepsilon]\)
  • \(\varepsilon\): clip radius; the paper suggests “say, 0.2”, which is the value used by InstructGPT, Bai and Llama 2
  • \(\min\): picks the smaller of the two terms, which makes the clipped objective a pessimistic lower bound on \(L^{CPI}\); near \(\rho = 1\) the two coincide to first order

The \(\min\) looks like a detail, but it is what decides when the gradient dies. Do a case analysis, looking at the derivative with respect to \(\rho\):

  1. Good action (\(\hat A > 0\)), ratio rising. The objective grows with \(\rho\) up to \(1+\varepsilon\) and then goes flat. There is no incentive to make the good action even more likely in this batch.
  2. Good action, ratio falling below \(1-\varepsilon\). The unclipped term is the smaller one, so the gradient keeps going: PPO corrects a good action that became unlikely.
  3. Bad action (\(\hat A < 0\)), ratio falling. Below \(1-\varepsilon\), flat: the bad action has already been demoted enough.
  4. Bad action, ratio rising above \(1+\varepsilon\). The \(\min\) picks the unclipped term and the penalty keeps going: the bad action became more likely, and PPO does not let that slide.

The rule, in the authors' words: the change in the ratio is ignored only when it would improve the objective, and it is included when it makes the objective worse. The table does the math with \(\varepsilon = 0.2\) for a good action (\(\hat A = +2\)) and a bad one (\(\hat A = -1\)).

Play with the sign of the advantage. With \(\hat A > 0\), the ball climbs to \(1+\varepsilon\) and stops. With \(\hat A < 0\), it descends to \(1-\varepsilon\) and stops. Now put the ball to the right of \(1+\varepsilon\) with a negative advantage: it gets pushed back, because there the \(\min\) picked the unclipped term. That is the asymmetry of item 4 in the list.

Does it work? On MuJoCo, the paper compares variants on a normalized score (7 environments, 21 runs per variant). Without clip or penalty, the score is −0.39: the plain surrogate diverges. After one iteration on Hopper, the KL of the updated policy sits around 0.02, the point where \(L^{CLIP}\) peaks (Fig. 2 of the paper).

It did worse than the clip on these benchmarks, and the clip became the standard.

Two KLs that are not the same

Two KL divergences with different roles show up in this chapter. PPO's, \(D_{KL}(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\|\pi_\theta)\), measures how far the policy moved within one iteration; it is the trust region, and it amounts to hundredths of a nat (about 0.02 on Hopper). RLHF's, \(D_{KL}(\pi_\theta\|\pi_{\text{ref}})\), measures how far the policy drifted from the SFT model over the whole training run; it is the leash, and it reaches several nats (14 to 18 nats in Stiennon's final models; 19 in one of the runs of the \(\beta\) sweep). Mixing the two up is a common mistake when reading configs: clip_range and kl_coef control different things.

The full loss: critic, entropy, epochs and minibatches

The clip takes care of the policy. But PPO is an actor-critic method: it needs a critic \(V\) to estimate the advantages. The paper combines everything into a single objective (eq. 9):

\[ \begin{aligned} &L^{CLIP+VF+S}_t(\theta) = \hat{\mathbb E}_t\Big[L^{CLIP}_t(\theta) \\ &\qquad - c_1\,L^{VF}_t(\theta) + c_2\,S[\pi_\theta](s_t)\Big] \\ &L^{VF}_t = \big(V_\theta(s_t) - V^{\text{targ}}_t\big)^2 \end{aligned} \]
  • \(L^{VF}\): value loss, a regression of the critic toward the target \(V^{\text{targ}}\)
  • \(c_1\): weight of the value loss, needed when policy and critic share parameters (on Atari, \(c_1 = 1\))
  • \(S[\pi_\theta](s_t) = -\sum_a \pi_\theta(a\mid s_t)\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s_t)\): the policy's entropy, added as an entropy bonus with weight \(c_2\) (0.01 on Atari) to keep exploration going

The critic of an LLM

In RLHF the critic is a whole LM with a scalar head that outputs a value at every position: \(V(s_t)\) answers “what final score do I expect if I keep sampling from this prefix?”. That is different from the reward model, which is only read at the final token. Stiennon and colleagues (2020) used a Transformer with parameters “completely separate” from the policy, because this keeps the value-function updates from partially destroying the pretrained policy early in training. And they initialized the critic with the RM weights, a practice InstructGPT repeated. With separate parameters, \(c_1\) loses its role of arbitrating shared weights. When the two networks live in the same optimizer (as in the N+ reproduction, \(c_1 = 0.1\)), it only rescales the critic's gradient. With Adam, which normalizes each parameter, that matters little, except through the global norm clip, which is shared by both.

The critic's target comes from the GAE you saw in chapter 54: \(\hat A_t = \sum_l(\gamma\lambda)^l\delta_{t+l}\), with residual \(\delta_t = r_t + \gamma V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t)\), and target \(\hat R_t = \hat A_t + V_{\text{old}}(s_t)\), which is the λ-return. In RLHF almost everyone uses \(\gamma = 1\): Stiennon and the “N+ implementation details” reproduction (Huang and colleagues, 2024) use \(\lambda = 0.95\); InstructGPT only says it used no discount. Long responses come at a price. With the reward only at the last token, its direct contribution to \(\hat A_t\) is weighted by \(\lambda^{T-t}\): with \(\lambda = 0.95\), \(0.95^{50}\approx 0.077\) and \(0.95^{500}\approx 7\times 10^{-12}\). In reasoning chains of thousands of tokens, the first tokens depend almost entirely on the critic's guess. VC-PPO (2025) attacked exactly this, according to the paper's abstract: it pretrains the critic and decouples GAE (\(\lambda = 1\) in the critic, \(0.95\) in the actor). VAPO, also according to its abstract, took Qwen 32B base to 60.4 on AIME 2024 in 5,000 steps with PPO and a critic.

Value clipping

Just like the policy, the critic can also take too big a jump on a single batch. The implementation detail inherited from OpenAI Baselines, and adopted in RLHF, is to clip the value:

\[ \begin{aligned} V^{\text{clip}}_t &= V_{\text{old}} + \operatorname{clip}\big(V_\psi - V_{\text{old}},\,-\hat\varepsilon,\,\hat\varepsilon\big) \\ L^{VF,\text{clip}}_t &= \max\Big(\big(V_\psi - \hat R_t\big)^2,\ \big(V^{\text{clip}}_t - \hat R_t\big)^2\Big) \end{aligned} \]
  • \(V_\psi\): current critic; \(V_{\text{old}}\): the value it predicted when the batch was generated (both evaluated at \(s_t\))
  • \(V^{\text{clip}}_t\): the new prediction clamped to the band \(V_{\text{old}}\pm\hat\varepsilon\)
  • \(\hat R_t\): target (λ-return); \(\hat\varepsilon\): radius of the value clipping (0.2 in N+)
  • \(\max\): the pessimistic version of a loss (for the policy, PPO took the \(\min\) of an objective to be maximized)

The logic mirrors \(L^{CLIP}\). If the new value has already left the band \(V_{\text{old}}\pm\hat\varepsilon\) in the direction that reduces the error, the clipped term, now constant, becomes the larger one, the \(\max\) picks it and the gradient goes to zero. If it left in the wrong direction, the unclipped term dominates and pulls it back. The figure shows three cases (illustrative example, exact math).

Entropy: the bonus RLHF does without

In A3C (Mnih and colleagues, 2016), the entropy bonus with weight 0.01 discouraged premature convergence to deterministic policies. In LLM RLHF it is almost always switched off: Bai and colleagues (2022) use no entropy bonus, and the KL penalty to the reference model already plays that role, as the section on the KL leash shows with a two-line identity.

Epochs and minibatches

The paper's algorithm is simple: at each iteration, \(N\) actors collect \(T\) steps with \(\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}\); the advantages are computed; the surrogate is optimized for \(K\) epochs on minibatches of size \(M\le NT\) with Adam; and \(\theta_{\text{old}}\leftarrow\theta\). On MuJoCo, \(K = 10\) epochs, a minibatch of 64 and a horizon of 2,048; on Atari, \(K = 3\). In LLMs the numbers drop: InstructGPT uses a batch of 512 and a minibatch of 64 (8 minibatches) with 1 epoch; Stiennon and N+ use 4 epochs; Bai tries 1, 2 and 4 and observes that a larger \(K\) typically gives more stable results; Llama 2 takes one gradient step per minibatch. Interpretation: each epoch pushes the policy further from the one that generated the batch, and in a model with billions of parameters a few steps already move the distribution a lot.

The RLHF pipeline: SFT → reward model → PPO

PPO is only the third piece. The pipeline around it was assembled over five years, paper by paper:

🦿

Christiano et al. 2017

RL from human comparisons of 1–2 s clips, on Atari (A2C) and MuJoCo (TRPO), with the environment reward hidden. With 900 queries, in under an hour, Hopper learned to do a backflip.

✍️

Ziegler et al. 2019

The first RLHF on an LM (GPT-2 774M). Brought the KL-shaped reward to LMs and introduced the adaptive \(\beta\) controller. The summarizers turned into “smart copiers” that copied whole sentences from the text.

📰

Stiennon et al. 2020

The canonical SFT → RM → PPO pipeline on summarization. \(\beta = 0.05\), a separate critic initialized from the RM, 1M episodes, about 320 GPU-days for the RL of the 6.7B.

🧭

InstructGPT 2022

The same pipeline applied to general instructions, with PPO-ptx to avoid losing capabilities. It became the industry standard.

🤝

Bai et al. 2022

The KL leash: shaped reward, adaptive control and PPO-ptx

If PPO maximized only the RM score, it would find the responses that fool the RM: the reward hacking from chapter 53. The classic defense is to tie the policy to the SFT model with a KL penalty built into the reward itself:

\[ \begin{aligned} R(x,y) &= r_\phi(x,y) - \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} \\ \mathbb E_{y\sim\pi_\theta}[R] &= \mathbb E[r_\phi] - \beta\,D_{KL}\big(\pi_\theta\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}\big) \end{aligned} \]
  • \(r_\phi\): the RM score, already normalized; \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\): the reference policy, a frozen copy of the SFT
  • \(\beta\): KL coefficient (0.05 in Stiennon, 0.02 in InstructGPT)
  • \(\log\pi(y\mid x) = \sum_t\log\pi(y_t\mid s_t)\); the second line holds because \(\mathbb E_{y\sim\pi}[\log\pi/\pi_{\text{ref}}]\) is the definition of KL, with both distributions conditioned on the same prompt: \(D_{KL}\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(\cdot\mid x)\big)\)

In PPO, this reward is spread across the tokens. The RM only speaks at the last one; every token receives its share of the KL:

\[ \begin{aligned} r_t = &-\beta\big(\log\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_t\mid s_t) - \log\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_t\mid s_t)\big) \\ &+ \mathbb 1[t = T]\;r_\phi(x,y) \end{aligned} \]
  • \(\mathbb 1[t=T]\): equals 1 only at the last token (the EOS), where the RM score is added
  • with \(\gamma = 1\), \(\sum_t r_t = R(x,y)\); N+ shows a real example: 4.5 at the EOS, and the other numbers are the per-token KL penalty

Why this leash? The sources give three readings. First, it is an entropy bonus in disguise: \(D_{KL}(\pi\|\pi_{\text{ref}}) = -H(\pi) - \mathbb E_\pi[\log\pi_{\text{ref}}]\), so \(-\beta D_{KL} = \beta H(\pi) + \beta\,\mathbb E_\pi[\log\pi_{\text{ref}}]\), and the policy is rewarded for keeping its entropy. That is why RLHF does without \(c_2\). Second, it keeps the RM valid: it stops the policy from producing outputs very different from the ones the RM saw in training. Third, in style tasks it is part of the very definition of the task (Ziegler): coherence and topic come from the SFT. There are counterpoints: Gao and colleagues (2022) found the KL equivalent to early stopping in their setup, and Bai used an almost-zero KL.

The optimal policy: the SFT reweighted by the reward

The KL objective has a closed-form solution. It is the foundation of DPO (chapter 57) and the best way to understand what \(\beta\) does:

\[ \begin{aligned} \pi^*(y\mid x) &= \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\big(r(x,y)/\beta\big) \\ Z(x) &= \sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,e^{r(x,y)/\beta} \end{aligned} \]
  • \(\pi^*\): the policy that maximizes \(\mathbb E_\pi[r] - \beta D_{KL}(\pi\|\pi_{\text{ref}})\)
  • \(Z(x)\): the normalization constant (the partition function)
  • \(\beta\): the temperature; as \(\beta\to0\) only the reward matters, as \(\beta\to\infty\) the policy stays at \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\)
Deep dive: the derivation in two lines

Rewrite the objective as \(\mathbb E_{y\sim\pi}\big[r - \beta\log\pi + \beta\log\pi_{\text{ref}}\big] = -\beta\,\mathbb E_{y\sim\pi}\big[\log\pi - \log(\pi_{\text{ref}}e^{r/\beta})\big]\). Add and subtract \(\log Z\): the objective becomes \(-\beta\,D_{KL}\big(\pi\,\|\,\tfrac1Z\pi_{\text{ref}}e^{r/\beta}\big) + \beta\log Z\). The second term does not depend on \(\pi\), and the KL is minimal (zero) when the two distributions are equal. Therefore \(\pi^* = \tfrac1Z\pi_{\text{ref}}e^{r/\beta}\). RLHF with KL does not invent new responses: it reweights what the SFT already did, exponentially by the reward.

The figure shows this reweighting on a prompt with six possible responses. One of them is an “exploit”: the RM gives it the highest score, but its real quality is terrible, and the SFT would almost never write it. Lower \(\beta\) and watch the mass drain toward it.

With \(\beta = 1\), the optimal policy still looks like the SFT, and the helpful, correct response is the most likely one. With \(\beta = 0.3\), the exploit already takes 68% of the mass: the RM score climbs to 2.0, but the mean real quality plunges to −0.8. This is the portrait of the over-optimization (Goodhart) that chapter 53 measured, now seen as a choice of \(\beta\).

Measuring the KL with samples

The exact sequence KL would require summing over every possible response. Per token, you could sum over the whole vocabulary, but that requires keeping the full logits of \(\pi\) and \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\) (\(B\times T\times 10^5\) numbers per iteration). Standard practice is an estimator that uses only the sampled token. With \(y_t\sim\pi_\theta\) and \(r = \pi_{\text{ref}}(y_t)/\pi_\theta(y_t)\), Schulman's blog (2020) compares three:

\[ \begin{aligned} k_1 &= -\log r \\ k_2 &= \tfrac12(\log r)^2 \\ k_3 &= (r-1) - \log r \end{aligned} \]
  • \(k_1\): unbiased, high variance, can be negative; it is the per-token reward term above
  • \(k_2\): biased, low variance; matches the KL up to second order
  • \(k_3\): unbiased and always ≥ 0; it is what GRPO uses inside the loss (chapter 56)

Interpretation: putting the KL in the reward (InstructGPT) and putting it in the loss (GRPO) do not give the same gradient. In the reward, it goes through the advantage, the critic and GAE; in the loss, it is differentiated directly. In expectation, both pull toward \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\). Adaptive control: aim at a KL, not at a β

Ziegler and colleagues noticed a practical problem: runs with the same \(\beta\) and different seeds ended up with very different KLs, which were hard to compare. The solution was a proportional adaptive controller in log space that aims at a target KL:

\[ \begin{aligned} e_t &= \operatorname{clip}\Big(\frac{\text{KL}_t - \text{KL}_{\text{target}}}{\text{KL}_{\text{target}}},\ -0.2,\ 0.2\Big) \\ \beta_{t+1} &= \beta_t\,(1 + K_\beta\,e_t), \quad K_\beta = 0.1 \end{aligned} \]
  • \(e_t\): relative error of the measured KL \(\text{KL}_t = \text{KL}(\pi_t,\pi_{\text{ref}})\), clipped at ±20%
  • \(K_\beta\): controller gain; at 0.1, \(\beta\) changes by at most ±2% per step
  • \(\text{KL}_{\text{target}}\): targets used in the paper, such as 6, 8 and 10 nats

With a target of 6 nats and \(\beta_0 = 0.1\), follow a sequence of measurements: a KL of 12 gives \(e = +0.2\) and \(\beta\) goes to 0.1020; the next one, 10, takes it to 0.10404; a 6 leaves it unchanged; a 3 brings it to 0.10196. The controller is slow on purpose. The PPO paper's rule, which doubles or halves \(\beta\), is much more aggressive. The figure simulates three seeds that “pull” the KL with different strengths.

PPO-ptx: the third force

Even with the leash, InstructGPT's RLHF caused regressions on public benchmarks (SQuADv2, DROP, translation), which the authors called the alignment tax. The fix was to mix pretraining gradients into PPO (eq. 2 of the paper):

\[ \begin{aligned} &\text{objective}(\phi) = \mathbb E_{(x,y)\sim D_{\pi^{RL}_\phi}}\Big[r_\theta(x,y) \\ &\qquad - \beta\log\frac{\pi^{RL}_\phi(y\mid x)}{\pi^{SFT}(y\mid x)}\Big] \\ &\qquad + \gamma\,\mathbb E_{x\sim D_{\text{pretrain}}}\big[\log\pi^{RL}_\phi(x)\big] \end{aligned} \]
  • \(\pi^{RL}_\phi\): the policy (the paper calls its parameters \(\phi\)); \(r_\theta\): the RM
  • \(\gamma\): pretraining coefficient (not the discount!), equal to 27.8; with \(\gamma = 0\) the model is “PPO”, and “InstructGPT” is PPO-ptx
  • the second term is the log-likelihood of pretraining text, the same loss as in pretraining

In the implementation, each minibatch computes the PPO gradient and the pretraining gradient in consecutive steps, accumulated in the same buffer, the second one multiplied by \(\gamma\), with 8× more pretraining examples than RL episodes. This doubles training time compared with plain PPO. \(\gamma\ge20\) reversed the regressions on the 1.3B, and 27.8 worked from 1.3B to 175B. And the most instructive detail: raising only the KL's \(\beta\) does not solve it. Even \(\beta = 2.0\), 100× the default, did not fix the regressions and also dragged down the validation reward. Interpretation: the leash keeps the policy close to the SFT on the distribution of RL prompts; ptx keeps it close to pretraining on the distribution of general text.

The inference engine inside the loop

This is where Module 1 comes back in full. Generating 512 responses is autoregressive decode, the memory-bound phase from chapter 10. And a training engine is terrible at it. Llama 2 reports that generating with FSDP was about 20× slower, even with a large batch and a KV cache; the mitigation was to consolidate the weights on each node before generation. The OpenRLHF paper says the inference phase often accounts for more than 90% of the total time of RLHF or RLVR. That is why every modern framework generates the rollouts with vLLM or SGLang, with PagedAttention, continuous batching and CUDA graphs (chapter 30), and computes gradients with another engine (DeepSpeed ZeRO, FSDP, Megatron). The vLLM docs list, among the frameworks that use it for rollouts, OpenRLHF, verl, TRL, NeMo-RL, Open Instruct, SkyRL and Unsloth.

A FLOP count helps to see the paradox. With the rule of thumb of ~\(2N\) FLOPs per token for a forward and ~\(6N\) for a forward+backward (Kaplan and colleagues, 2020), four 7B models, 512 prompts of 256 tokens and responses of 256 tokens (262,144 tokens per iteration) and \(K = 1\):

PhaseFLOPsMath
generation (prefill + decode)3.67 PFLOP\(2N\times262{,}144\)
scoring forwards (\(\pi_{\text{old}}\), \(\pi_{\text{ref}}\), RM, \(V_{\text{old}}\))14.68 PFLOP\(4\times2N\times262{,}144\)
policy + critic training (1 epoch)22.02 PFLOP\(2\times6N\times262{,}144\)
total40.37 PFLOPillustrative example, attention excluded

In FLOPs, generation is the smallest share. In time, it weighs much more than the FLOPs suggest, because every decode step reads all of the weights. On the GB10, the 14 GB of bf16 weights of a 7B at 273 GB/s give at least 51 ms per step, or 13 s for 256 steps, and that only if all 512 rollouts fit in a single KV batch (calculation). The compute limits, on the same machine, give the other half of the story: at ~125 TFLOP/s of dense bf16, the 22.02 PFLOP of training take ≥ 176 s, the scoring forwards ≥ 117 s and generation itself ≥ 29 s, that is, ≥ 323 s in total at 100% utilization, versus the ≥ 13 s of bandwidth-bound decode (calculation). Interpretation: in this short-response example, the lower bounds still point to training; the dominance of generation that OpenRLHF reports shows up with long responses (reasoning that runs to thousands of tokens) and with the tail, where the longest response in the batch holds everyone up.

Colocated or disaggregated

There are two ways to put the two engines together:

Colocated (hybrid engine)Disaggregated
GPUsthe same GPUs alternate between training and generationseparate training and rollout pools
weight syncin memory: shared tensors or CUDA IPC handlesnetwork: NCCL broadcast from training to rollout, or a checkpoint on disk
memorymust free the inference engine's KV and weights during training, and the optimizer during generationno contention; each side is sized on its own

examplesDeepSpeed-Chat; verl 3D-HybridEngine; OpenRLHF hybrid modeOpenRLHF (Ray); slime disaggregated; Noukhovitch et al.

verl (the HybridFlow paper) reshards the actor between the training parallelism and the generation parallelism “with zero memory redundancy”, offloading the generation weights to the CPU during training, and reports 1.53× to 20.57× the throughput of the baselines it compares against. OpenRLHF uses Ray to schedule the roles of rollout engines (vLLM) and actor engines (DeepSpeed ZeRO), supports a hybrid mode and an async flow, and reports speedups of 1.22× to 1.68×.

The engines grew their own APIs for this. In vLLM (latest docs as of 2026-10), sleep mode (--enable-sleep-mode) has two levels: llm.sleep(level=1) offloads the weights to CPU RAM and discards the KV cache; level=2 discards weights and KV, which is useful when the weights are about to be replaced anyway. llm.wake_up(tags=["weights"]) and then tags=["kv_cache"] wake up in stages to reduce peak memory. Weight synchronization uses WeightTransferConfig(backend=...), with the backends "nccl", "ipc", "sparse_nccl", "sharded_rdt" and "nccl_m2n", in a four-phase protocol: init_weight_transfer_engine → start_weight_update → update_weights (repeatable, in chunks) → finish_weight_update. On the HTTP server, these routes (and /sleep, /wake_up) only exist with VLLM_SERVER_DEV_MODE=1. In SGLang, the equivalents are update_weights_from_tensor (colocated), update_weights_from_distributed (NCCL/IB), release_memory_occupation/resume_memory_occupation and pause_generation in the abort, retract or in_place modes.

How much does moving the weights cost? The 14 GB of a 7B in bf16, over the GB10's ConnectX-7 (≈ 15.7 GB/s per PCIe function, ≈ 25 GB/s with both), take ≈ 0.9 s or ≈ 0.56 s, without protocol overhead (calculation with the numbers from chapter 39). In unified memory, colocated mode can reduce the sync to a handle exchange (interpretation). Watch out for the same fact in reverse: on the GB10, “offloading to CPU RAM” (sleep level 1) does not free physical memory, because the CPU and the GPU share the same LPDDR5x (chapter 27); only level 2, which discards, frees it. This is our interpretation, by the same argument as for Adam offload; it has not been tested on the hardware.

Asynchronous RL

In synchronous disaggregated mode, the training pool waits for generation and vice versa. Noukhovitch and colleagues (ICLR 2025) generate batch \(i+1\) while training on batch \(i\): training becomes 1-step off-policy. They ask how much off-policyness can be tolerated. Performance generally degrades with more lag, and among the losses tested Online DPO was the most robust, with robustness growing with the size of the policy. The measured gains: the same performance as synchronous training about 25% faster on a 2.8B, about 40% faster on a LLaMA 3.1 8B chatbot and about 70% faster on Rho 1B on GSM8k. It is the A3C idea (actors generating experience in parallel, a central learner consuming it) with vLLM instances playing the actors.

Move the fraction of GPUs in the rollout pool. In synchronous disaggregated mode, any choice leaves someone idle. In async mode, there is a balance point (near 70% in the figure's default) where both pools work all the time, at the price of one iteration of lag. Colocated mode has no idle time, but it pays for the context switch at every iteration and serializes the phases. Notice that, in this toy model, well-balanced async only ties with colocated. Interpretation: the real advantage of disaggregation comes from what the model does not capture. Decode does not scale linearly with the number of GPUs, the longest response in the batch holds up the generation phase, and each pool can use whatever parallelism and memory suit it best, without sharing the GPU with the optimizer.

Same weights, different probabilities

There is an even sneakier source of off-policyness. With generation in vLLM and training in FSDP, \(\pi_{\text{vllm}}(a;\theta)\ne\pi_{\text{fsdp}}(a;\theta)\), despite the same parameters: different kernels, reduction order, precision and batching (Yao and colleagues, 2025). This implicitly turns on-policy RL into off-policy RL, and it gets worse with rollouts quantized to INT8 or FP8. The fixes have names: TIS multiplies the gradient by \(\min(\pi_{\text{fsdp}}/\pi_{\text{vllm}}, C)\); MiniMax-M1 raised the LM head to FP32 and took the correlation between training and inference probabilities from ~0.9 to ~0.99; in MoE, R3 records the router's choices at inference and replays them in training, because ~94% of tokens pick different experts in at least one layer between the two phases. Chapter 56 goes deeper into this topic; here, the bridge to the recipes is enough.

The recipes have already solved half the problem

The Qwen recipe has a determinism overlay (VLLM_QSA_DET_TOPK=1, VLLM_MOE_DET_FINALIZE=1 with VLLM_FLASHINFER_MOE_FUSED_FINALIZE=0) that took the prompt logprob spread from 0.25 / 6.07 nats to 0 / 0, at a cost of ~4% in decode: that is exactly the class of fix that a “truly on-policy” setup requires. The recipe ships logprob_capture.py, which compares the KL and the argmax of logprobs between two servers, the same metric you monitor between sampler and trainer. And GLM's patch_cache_reset.py overlay exposes only the cache reset routes, without /sleep or the RPC routes that VLLM_SERVER_DEV_MODE=1 would mount. Interpretation: a serving recipe deliberately closes the doors that an RL system needs to open. And since the three models of Module 1 are MoE with quantized weights, using them as rollout generators for a policy trained in BF16 would be the worst case of mismatch: quantization and routing at the same time.

Instabilities, tricks and cost

PPO has a reputation for instability, and the reputation is deserved: Engstrom and colleagues (2020) and the post “The 37 Implementation Details of PPO” showed that normalizations, initializations and clippings explain a good part of the performance. For RLHF, the equivalent lists are “N+ implementation details” (Huang and colleagues, 2024) and “Secrets of RLHF” (Zheng and colleagues, 2023). The tricks that show up most often:

Whitening deserves one more sentence. The advantage multiplies the gradient, so its scale is, in practice, a hidden learning rate. A reward that ranges from −5 to +5 in one batch and from −0.1 to +0.1 in the next would make the effective step vary 50× (illustrative example). Subtracting the mean and dividing by the standard deviation leaves only the relative signal: in this batch, which tokens were better than the others. It is the same idea as the REINFORCE baseline, applied once more after GAE.

The typical symptoms, as read from the sources (interpretation): the reward rises while human quality falls (over-optimization); length explodes (length bias); KL shoots up (\(\beta\) too small or LR too high); the mean ratio is already far from 1 in the 1st epoch (dropout on, or sampler–trainer mismatch); huge value loss at the start (critic not initialized from the RM). The dashboard below acts out each one.

Finally, the cost. The real numbers give the scale: PPO-ptx for InstructGPT 175B cost 60 petaflop/s-days (GPT-3's pretraining, 3,640); Stiennon's RL for the 6.7B, about 320 GPU-days; one PPO iteration on Llama 2 70B, about 330 s. And PPO is expensive for four reasons that add up: two trainable models (twice the optimizer memory), four forwards per response, \(K\) epochs and a serial generation bottleneck. Hence the three escape routes the next chapters explore: DPO eliminates RL, RLOO and GRPO eliminate the critic, and async RL eliminates the bubbles.

In practice: does it fit on your machine? Tools in 2026

The first question for anyone who wants to run PPO is not about the algorithm, it is about memory. A model trained with Adam in mixed precision costs about 16 bytes per parameter: 2 for the bf16 weights, 2 for the gradients and 12 for the FP32 master copy, the first moment and the second moment (4 + 4 + 4), in the ZeRO paper's accounting (chapter 51). A frozen model in bf16 costs 2 bytes per parameter. Policy and critic with \(N\) parameters, plus a reference and an RM of the same size:

\[ M_{\text{PPO}} \approx 2\times16N + 2\times2N = 36N\ \text{bytes} \]
  • \(16N\): each trainable model (policy and critic)
  • \(2N\): each frozen model (reference and RM)
  • left out of the math: activations, communication buffers, fragmentation, the inference engine's copy of the weights and the rollouts' KV cache

That gives 54 GB (50.3 GiB) for 1.5B, 108 GB for 3B, 252 GB (234.7 GiB) for 7B and 288 GB for 8B. A DGX Spark exposes about 121.69 GiB of unified memory to CUDA; two of them add up to about 243 GiB. In other words: PPO with full fine-tuning of a 7B policy and critic (≈ 235 GiB of states alone) no longer fits with activations and KV, not even on two nodes; 3B (≈ 101 GiB) fits on a single node for the states alone. Do the math in the calculator.

The usual ways out show up in the calculator. Critic and RM smaller than the policy: InstructGPT used a 6B RM and critic for a 175B policy. LoRA: the reference becomes “the same network with the adapter turned off”, with no extra copy. Offloading Adam to the CPU: helps on discrete GPUs, but on the GB10 it does not free physical memory, for the same reason as sleep mode. And dropping the critic, which is the path of RLOO and GRPO. In MoE, as with the three models of Module 1, memory scales with the total parameters, while FLOPs scale with the active ones.

The tools in 2026

The ecosystem has moved. TRL, which for years was the easiest way to run PPO, removed PPOTrainer in version 1.13.0, according to the release notes; the current version is 1.14.1, which keeps RewardTrainer stable for the RM stage. For PPO with a critic, the path today is dedicated RL frameworks:

OpenRLHF 0.11.2

Ray + vLLM + DeepSpeed. Entry point python3 -m openrlhf.cli.train_ppo_ray; the default advantage estimator (--algo.advantage.estimator) is PPO with a critic, and the same script runs group_norm (GRPO), reinforce_baseline and rloo. Namespaced flags: --vllm.num_engines, --vllm.tensor_parallel_size, --ds.lora.rank, and --reward.remote_url to serve the RM over HTTP.

verl 0.9.1

Hydra config, training on FSDP/FSDP2/Megatron and rollout on vLLM or SGLang (HybridFlow paper). The official PPO example on GSM8K turns on a separate critic with critic.model.path and puts the KL in algorithm.kl_ctrl.kl_coef=0.001.

On the Mac: mlx-lm-lora

mlx-lm-lora 3.1.3 accepts --train-mode ppo (and rlhf_reinforce) with a reward model as the judge. The reference is a second full, frozen copy, loaded from --reference-model-path: on the 24 GB Mac, budget for that.

The verl example, as it appears in the GSM8K docs (8 GPUs on one node):

python3 -m verl.trainer.main_ppo \
  data.train_files=... data.train_batch_size=1024 \
  data.max_prompt_length=512 data.max_response_length=512 \
  actor_rollout_ref.model.path=deepseek-ai/deepseek-llm-7b-chat \
  actor_rollout_ref.actor.optim.lr=1e-6 \
  actor_rollout_ref.rollout.name=vllm \
  actor_rollout_ref.rollout.gpu_memory_utilization=0.5 \

Read each line with what you have learned. rollout.name=vllm is the engine in the loop; gpu_memory_utilization=0.5 is the slice of memory vLLM may reserve, because the training weights live on the same GPUs (colocated mode, interpretation); critic.model.path is the fourth model; kl_coef=0.001 is \(\beta\), at the same level as Bai's \(\lambda_{KL}\). On the infrastructure side, slime (SGLang + Megatron) states in its README that it is the RL framework behind GLM versions 4.5 to 5.3, and vime (June 2026) brings slime's design to the vLLM ecosystem, with colocated and disaggregated modes.

So where does PPO-RLHF stand today? For general chat alignment, a good part of the open community moved to DPO and its variants, with no explicit RM and no RL (chapter 57), and to REINFORCE, RLOO and GRPO, with no critic. HelpSteer2-Preference reached 85.0 on Arena Hard (1st place on October 1, 2024) with the RM and REINFORCE. For reasoning with verifiable rewards, DeepSeek-R1's GRPO became the standard, but PPO with a critic is competitive again when the critic is handled well (VC-PPO, VAPO). And the bottleneck moved from the algorithm to the system: efficient generation, weight sync, asynchrony and numerical consistency between sampler and trainer. This is where Module 1 becomes a prerequisite for Module 2.

Roadmap for your first run

Start small, with the critic the same size as the RM. Turn off dropout, make sure EOS ≠ PAD, normalize the RM with the demonstrations, whiten the advantages and turn on value clipping. Use \(\varepsilon = 0.2\), few epochs (1 to 4), an LR on the order of 10⁻⁶ and a \(\beta\) in the range of the leash table, or a KL target. Before looking at the reward, look at the ratio in the 1st epoch (it must start at exactly 1), the KL to the SFT and the mean length. If the memory math does not work out, swap full FT for LoRA or drop the critic, which is the subject of the next chapter.

The next step is precisely to rip out the most expensive piece of this chapter. Chapter 56 replaces the critic with group statistics (GRPO, RLOO), replaces the RM with math and code verifiers (RLVR), and tackles head-on the training–inference mismatch that only appeared in passing here.
