Open the byte budget of a single Qwen3.8-Flash-Next decode step and look for the biggest slice. It isn't the 512 experts, and it isn't attention: it's a single matrix, the 248,320-row lm_head, read four times per step, once by the model and three times by the drafter. This chapter is about cheap drafters: the MTP that already ships inside the checkpoint, the EAGLE-3 “taps” on the target's residual stream, and the tricks the recipes use to cut the cost of each draft without changing the output distribution (the target still decides every token; under greedy, only numerical nondeterminism, which the recipe measures separately, can move the output): a reduced vocabulary, a pairwise distributed argmax, reuse of the sparse indexer's top-k, and the prefix-cache block that EAGLE used to throw away. By the end, you'll be able to assemble the three recipes' --speculative-config parameter by parameter and justify every value.

MTP: the drafter that comes factory-installed

A regular LLM is trained on a single task: given the text up to position \(t\), predict token \(t+1\) (chapter 1). Multi-Token Prediction adds an extra question to training: and the token after that? A small module, hanging off the top of the stack, receives the model's final hidden state at position \(t\) and the embedding of the true token \(t+1\), and tries to get \(t+2\) right. In training, this densifies the signal (each position yields two losses instead of one) and forces the representations to “plan” a little ahead. At inference, the module becomes a drafter that was trained together with the target, uses the same vocabulary and ships in the same checkpoint. The architecture was introduced in chapter 16. What matters here is what happens when it becomes the engine of speculative decoding (chapter 35).

Analogy

Think of the student who, besides answering the current question, takes a stab at the next one. They studied with the same teacher and read the same prompt, so on the easy guesses (“close the parenthesis”, “semicolon”) they're almost always right. The teacher, who here is the target model, grades all the guesses at once, and every correct guess is an answer the teacher didn't have to write.

The math is short. In GLM's form, which follows DeepSeek-V3's design, with \(d\) being the model width:

\[ \begin{aligned} z_t &= W_{eh}\,\big[\,\mathrm{RMSNorm}_e(E\,x_{t+1})\;;\;\mathrm{RMSNorm}_h(h_t)\,\big] \\ u_t &= \mathrm{Block}_{\mathrm{MTP}}(z_t) \\ \hat{x}_{t+2} &= \arg\max_{v}\;\big[W_{\mathrm{head}}\,\mathrm{RMSNorm}(u_t)\big]_v \end{aligned} \]
  • \(h_t\): the target's final hidden state at position \(t\) (dimension \(d\))
  • \(E\,x_{t+1}\): embedding of token \(t+1\), looked up in the target's own input table
  • \([\,\cdot\,;\,\cdot\,]\): concatenation, which yields a vector of size \(2d\)
  • \(W_{eh}\): a \(d \times 2d\) projection (eh_proj [4096 × 8192] on GLM)
  • \(\mathrm{Block}_{\mathrm{MTP}}\): a full decoder layer (attention + MoE), with its own KV cache
  • \(W_{\mathrm{head}}\): the lm_head [\(V \times d\)], which turns the vector into \(V\) logits
  • \(\hat x_{t+2}\): the draft, which the target still has to verify

In the figure, follow the path of one guess. Each vector is a column of cells, each matrix is a grid, and the active step glows. Switch the model to see how the same pieces change size on Qwen.

Notice two details that come back throughout the chapter. First, the input is shifted: the state at position \(t\) travels together with token \(t+1\). That shift explains the prefix cache's “dropped block” in section 8. Second, the last matrix is huge, with \(V\) rows against \(d\) columns: 154,880 rows on GLM and 248,320 on Qwen. The decoder layer in the middle looks like the main piece, but it's the lm_head that dominates the final bill (section 4).

Why this matters

A separate drafter has to be trained for that particular target, downloaded, loaded and kept in sync with the target's version. The factory MTP spares you all of that: it was trained alongside, it's already in the checkpoint and it learned from the target's own representations. That's why Qwen uses MTP as its main method and GLM keeps it as the rollback for DFlash2.

From training to drafting: the same layer, k times

In training, MTP answers a single question (\(t+2\)). To draft \(k\) tokens, vLLM treats it as an “EAGLE-style” proposer and applies it recursively. Each engine step has three beats:

  1. Verify. The target processes \(k+1\) positions in a single forward: the last accepted token and the \(k\) drafts from the previous round. Rejection sampling decides the accepted prefix, and the target always adds one token of its own: the correction at the first rejected position or, if every draft passes, the bonus token at position \(k+1\) (chapter 35).
  2. Extend. In draft-extend, the MTP runs over the freshly accepted positions using the hidden states the target has just computed, and proposes the 1st draft.
  3. Repeat. For \(i = 2, \dots, k\), the same layer receives its own output state and its own previous draft, in place of the target's state and the true token, and proposes the next one.

On Qwen, the engine inventory describes this as “1 draft forward on the padded batch + 2 more single-token forwards per step” for \(k=3\). Every one of those forwards ends in a multiplication by the entire lm_head. Keep that detail in mind for section 4.

Why does acceptance drop with position? There are two reasons, and they add up. Guess \(i\) only counts if all the previous ones passed, because acceptance is by prefix. And, from the 2nd step on, the module reads its own output instead of the target's state, an input it saw less of in training. vLLM warns about this in the log (“running multiple times of forward on same MTP layer, which may result in lower acceptance rate”). Qwen measured, at batch 1 greedy, 89% / 74.5% / 60% per-position acceptance, averaging 72.8% (823 of 1,131 drafts). The decreasing curve is also the recipe's sanity check: a wrong FP8 scale in the MTP's experts would show up as near-random acceptance, not as a crash.

Going deeper: the module reuse rule

A checkpoint can have \(n_{\text{predict}}\) stacked MTP layers, each trained for one distance. vLLM then requires \(k \le n_{\text{predict}}\) or \(k \bmod n_{\text{predict}} = 0\), because the recursion reapplies the set of layers in whole cycles (“Ensure divisibility for MTP module reuse”). GLM and Qwen have \(n_{\text{predict}}=1\), so any \(k\) works. DeepSeek Vision-Exp declares 3, and the Anemll lane applies the rule to DSpark as well; with the trained block's minimum of 5, that leaves \(k=6\). On 0731, with \(n_{\text{predict}}=1\), it ran \(k=5\): that's the “MTP-5” of the old lanes, which under the hood was already DSpark. Chapter 37 shows the block-k hotfix, which exempts DSpark from the rule and unlocks \(k=5\).

Choosing \(k\) is a cost-benefit calculation. Each extra draft adds its acceptance to the tokens per step, but costs one drafter forward and one more row in verification:

\[ \tau(k) = 1 + \sum_{i=1}^{k} a_i, \qquad \text{tok/s}(k) = \frac{\tau(k)}{t_{\mathrm{verif}}(k) + k\,t_{\mathrm{draft}}}, \qquad \text{position } k{+}1 \text{ pays off} \iff a_{k+1} > \tau(k)\,\frac{\Delta t}{t(k)} \]
  • \(\tau(k)\): tokens emitted per step. The “1 +” is the token the target itself always emits: the correction at the first rejection or, when all \(k\) pass, the bonus
  • \(a_i\): acceptance at position \(i\) (fraction of steps in which draft \(i\) and all the previous ones passed)
  • \(t_{\mathrm{verif}}(k)\): target forward with \(k+1\) rows, almost the cost of 1 row, because decode is memory-bound
  • \(t_{\mathrm{draft}}\): one drafter forward (MTP layer + lm_head)
  • \(t(k)\): the whole step; \(\Delta t\): how much more it costs with one more position (\(t_{\mathrm{draft}}\) + one verification row)

The last inequality comes from comparing \(\tfrac{\tau+a_{k+1}}{t+\Delta t}\) with \(\tfrac{\tau}{t}\). Let's plug in Qwen numbers (our math, calibrated on the measurements): without MTP, the step takes ~40.8 ms (24.5 tok/s); with \(k=3\) and the full vocabulary, ~60 ms, so each of the three drafts costs ~6.4 ms. On the measured prose profile (0.81 / 0.64 / 0.51), \(\tau(3) = 2.96\), very close to the ~2.9 tokens per step recorded. The threshold for the 3rd position comes out to \(2.45 \times 6.9/54.6 \approx 0.31\), consistent with the inventory's rule of thumb: if position 3 accepts less than ~35%, \(k=2\) is faster.

The figure does this math live. The timeline shows one engine step: verification (lavender), MTP layer (mint) and the draft's lm_head (amber), repeated \(k\) times. The little characters that drop into the tray are the tokens delivered at each step.

Try the prose profile: going from \(k=2\) to \(k=3\) the model gains ~7%, and \(k=4\) would add only ~2% more, and that with an extrapolated acceptance nobody measured. On synthetic code, where everything is accepted, each extra position pays off handsomely. GLM's DFlash2 shows the same thing: acceptance on structured text goes from 0.98 to 0.83 across positions, while on prose it falls from 0.75 to 0.06. The ideal \(k\) depends on the content, and that's why GLM got an adaptive k, which picks how many positions to verify from recent acceptance, before verifying the current step (chapter 37).

EAGLE and EAGLE-3: taps on the residual stream

Look at the MTP's input again: hidden state at \(t\) and token \(t+1\). That's exactly the idea behind EAGLE, a drafter trained after the fact, for models that don't have MTP. Instead of a small model that only reads tokens, EAGLE is a lightweight head (one decoder layer) that works at the feature level: it receives the target's top hidden state and the embedding of the next token, and uses the target's own lm_head to get to the token. The one-token shift settles the question a blind drafter would have to guess: which of the plausible continuations the target actually chose. In that sense, MTP is a factory-installed EAGLE, and that's why vLLM handles both through the same interface.

EAGLE-3 changed two things. It stopped trying to predict the top feature and started predicting the token directly. And, above all, it started reading several depths of the target: states from low, middle and high layers are concatenated and fused by a projection. Shallow layers hold syntax and local form; deep ones, semantics and planning. A drafter that sees all three gets more right. In vLLM, these states are called auxiliary hidden states, and the contract has three pieces:

SupportsEagle3
Interface that the target model class declares to say it knows how to deliver auxiliary states.
EagleModelMixin + aux_hidden_state_layers
The tuple of layers to tap; the proposer fills it in from the drafter's config.
forward → (hidden, aux_hidden_states)
When layers are marked, the target's forward also returns the list of captured states.

What cost GLM a patch was the shape of its residual stream. The 45 trunk layers use mHC with 4 streams (chapter 15). In the middle of the stack, the state is a [tokens, 4, 4096] tensor, and on top of that hc_post is deferred, because vLLM fuses one layer's write with the next layer's read. A naive hook would capture something that isn't a 4096-wide hidden. overlay/patch_glm_eagle3.py solves it like this:

\[ \tilde h^{(\ell)} = \mathrm{hc\_contract}\Big(\mathrm{hc\_post}\big(x^{(\ell)}, R^{(\ell)}, \mathrm{post}^{(\ell)}, \mathrm{comb}^{(\ell)}\big)\Big) = \frac{1}{4}\sum_{s=1}^{4} H^{(\ell)}_{s}, \qquad c_p = \mathrm{RMSNorm}\big(W_{fc}\,[\tilde h^{(5)}_p;\tilde h^{(14)}_p;\tilde h^{(24)}_p;\tilde h^{(33)}_p;\tilde h^{(42)}_p]\big) \]
  • \(x^{(\ell)}, R^{(\ell)}, \mathrm{post}, \mathrm{comb}\): what the mHC layer returns (output, residual, write weights and mixing matrix)
  • \(H^{(\ell)}_s\): stream \(s\) of the residual after materializing hc_post
  • \(\tilde h^{(\ell)}\): the “contracted” hidden (mean of the 4 streams), \([\text{tokens}, 4096]\)
  • \(W_{fc}\): the DFlash2 projection, [4096 × 20480], from 5 × 4096 down to 4096
  • \(c_p\): the context the drafter injects as K/V (chapter 37)

In the figure, choose who taps what. Each bar on the left is a layer, and the probes mark the taps. Turn off the contraction to see what the hook would deliver without the patch.

Three practical consequences follow. First, the hook changes the target's graph, which now has extra outputs. That's why the compute_hash of DeepSeek's SpeculativeConfig includes the auxiliary states: a compilation cache generated without them can't be reused. Second, under sequence parallel each rank holds only a slice of the tokens, and GLM's patch does an sp_all_gather before handing over the state. An all-gather is the collective in which each rank sends its piece and everyone receives the concatenation; here, every rank ends up with the taps for all tokens. Third, and most treacherous: to vLLM, mtp, eagle, eagle3, dflash and dspark all count as use_eagle(). Everything the engine does “as an EAGLE precaution” applies to all of them, including the block drop of section 8.

In the recipe

patch_glm_eagle3.py is applied only at image build time (Dockerfile), not at runtime. It gives EagleModelMixin to Glm5NextModel and SupportsEagle3 to the …ForCausalLM and …ForConditionalGeneration classes. After each layer whose idx+1 is in aux_hidden_state_layers, it materializes hc_post, contracts to [tokens, 4096] and returns (hidden, aux). Every substitution is source-exact, that is, it only applies on top of an exactly known source text: if the target snippet doesn't appear exactly once in the file, the build stops instead of “guessing” where to fit the code (chapter 41). The consumer of these taps is DFlash2 (target_layer_ids = [5, 14, 24, 33, 42]), not MTP.

The elephant in the room: the drafter's head

Back to the opening question. The drafter exists to be cheap, but every one of its forwards ends in a matrix × vector multiplication by the lm_head: \(V\) rows of \(d\) numbers. In decode, with a small batch, that's nothing but memory reads (chapter 10). Time is \(\approx \text{bytes} / \text{bandwidth}\), and the bytes are \(V \cdot d \cdot 2\) in BF16, divided by the TP degree.

Qwen is the extreme case: it has the largest vocabulary and the smallest hidden size. With tie_word_embeddings: false, the embedding and the lm_head are separate matrices of 1.18 GiB each. The embedding costs next to nothing, because it only looks up rows (chapter 5); the lm_head is read in full on every forward. In the inventory's byte model (batch 1, MTP=3, per GPU, ≈9.2 GiB per step), the 4 lm_head reads add up to 2.36 GiB, 26% of the step, more than the routed experts (~2.1 GiB). Three of those four reads belong to the drafter. The MTP layer itself, running 3 times, reads only ~0.5 GiB.

With the full vocabulary, ~78% of the drafter's bytes are the lm_head (1.77 of 2.27 GiB). With 47k ids, the whole step shrinks ~12% in bytes (from 9.2 to ~8.1 GiB). The measured step time fell in almost the same proportion: from 61.2 to 54.0 ms on code and from 59.9 to 53.3 ms on prose, between −11 and −12%. Linking the two numbers is our own reading, but it's what you'd expect from a bandwidth-bound step.

Going deeper: block drafting dilutes the head

An autoregressive drafter pays for the lm_head \(k\) times, because it runs \(k\) forwards of one row each. A block drafter (DSpark, DFlash2) produces every position in a single forward and reads the head once per block; the extra rows cost almost nothing in a bandwidth-bound GEMM. That's one of the reasons DeepSeek, with \(k=6\), spends only ≈0.9 GB on the drafter and ≈1.06 GB on the head (target + draft), out of a total of ≈16.5–17 GB per rank per step. Chapter 37 covers this family.

Reduced vocabulary: draft only over what actually gets said

Token frequency roughly follows Zipf's law: a few thousand tokens (spaces, punctuation, common words, pieces of identifiers) cover most of any text, while a huge tail of rare kanji, words from other languages and proper names almost never shows up in a given kind of traffic. Qwen's 248,320-token vocabulary was built for the whole world, but an English code server uses a fraction of it. FR-Spec (frequency-ranked speculative sampling) exploits this: the drafter computes logits only over a subset \(S\) of the most frequent ids.

This only works because Qwen's drafter needs an argmax and nothing else. The draft is greedy (vLLM only builds the draft's full logits in the probabilistic method), so the question is “which token is most likely?”, not “what is the whole distribution?”. The argmax restricted to \(S\) only misses when the true winner is outside \(S\), and that wrong draft is rejected by the target at verification, like any other bad draft. The server keeps emitting what the target decides, with the same output distribution. The cost shows up in acceptance, never in correctness.

Analogy: today's specials

A rushed waiter who only offers the 20 most-ordered dishes gets most tables' orders right before the customer even opens their mouth. When someone wants the rare dish at the bottom of the menu, the chef, who is the target, simply corrects the order. Nobody eats the wrong dish; all that's lost is the time spent on the guess. That's exactly the reduced-vocabulary contract: the guess gets cheaper, and a miss costs just one rejected draft.

“Same distribution” is not “same bytes”

The guarantee is about the distribution. With temperature > 0, the literal sample can change, because the acceptance paths change. Under greedy, the sequence would only change through the numerical nondeterminism of TP2+EP, which the recipe measured separately: 5 identical requests gave 5 different outputs without VLLM_QSA_DET_TOPK=1 / VLLM_MOE_DET_FINALIZE=1, and a single output with them. Changing \(k\) or the vocabulary changes what goes into each verification batch and, with it, can change the order of floating-point sums (our reading).

\[ \hat x^{S} = \arg\max_{v \in S}\, \ell_v, \qquad \alpha_S \;\ge\; \alpha_{\text{full}} - \big(1 - \mathrm{cov}(S)\big), \qquad \mathrm{cov}(S) = \Pr\big[\,y \in S\,\big] \]
  • \(\ell_v\): the drafter's logit for token \(v\)
  • \(\hat x^{S}\): the draft restricted to the subset \(S\)
  • \(y\): the token the target picks at that position
  • \(\alpha_S, \alpha_{\text{full}}\): acceptance (greedy verification) with and without the restriction
  • \(\mathrm{cov}(S)\): the coverage, the fraction of the target's choices that fall in \(S\)

The inequality has a one-line proof. If the full draft was right (\(\arg\max \ell = y\)) and \(y \in S\), the restricted argmax also gives \(y\), because the global maximum lies inside \(S\). The only hits lost are those where \(y \notin S\), at most \(1-\mathrm{cov}(S)\) of the cases. Sometimes you even gain a hit, when the full draft's mistake was outside \(S\). When the target samples (temperature > 0), the output guarantee comes from rejection sampling: any draft distribution \(q\) preserves the target's distribution. Changing \(S\) changes \(q\), not the output.

In Qwen's implementation, the drafter's reduced vocabulary lives in files/patch_mtp_draft_vocab.py, which rewrites the image's nvidia/mtp.py, and the cut is literal: selecting rows of a matrix.

  1. _attach_draft_vocab runs after load_weights. It reads the file pointed to by VLLM_MTP_DRAFT_VOCAB (one id per line), discards invalid ids, does an index_select of the lm_head rows and keeps two buffers: _draft_lm_head_weight, the smaller matrix, and _draft_id_to_target_id, the map from local row to global id. It refuses to engage if the logit scale isn't positive.
  2. get_top_tokens multiplies by the smaller matrix, takes the argmax and translates the local index through the map.
  3. compute_logits stays untouched, with the full vocabulary. The rest of the system (target sampling, logprobs) never sees the cut.

Pitfalls

(1) The default 47k file was tuned on English and code; in other languages it covers only 30–69% of the tokens, and drafts get accepted less. The recipe already ships seven 65,536-id files, one per language (es, zh, ja, de, pt, fr, ru), that you switch in through MTP_DRAFT_VOCAB; the default is still the 47k one. For another language or another kind of traffic, build your own (section 7). (2) The trick requires greedy drafting: with draft_sample_method: probabilistic, the drafter needs the whole distribution. (3) MTP_DRAFT_VOCAB and FP8_DENSE are incompatible. Each one mounts, on top of the image's file, its own version of the same nvidia/mtp.py, and start.sh refuses the combination with “pick one”. The box below explains what FP8-dense does in that file.

Another lever: cut bits instead of rows

The reduced vocabulary reads fewer rows of the head. The other way to read fewer bytes is to spend fewer bits per weight. With FP8_DENSE=true, the recipe serves the MiaAI-Lab/Qwen3.8-Flash-Next-NVFP4-FP8dense checkpoint, in which the dense projections that used to be BF16, lm_head.weight included, become FP8 E4M3 with one scale per output channel on the weights and a dynamic per-token scale on the activations (FP8_PER_CHANNEL_PER_TOKEN; the QUANT_PATTERNS list in make_fp8_dense_checkpoint.py; format in chapter 21). On the head, that means one scale per vocabulary row and 1 byte per weight instead of 2: each read drops from 0.59 to ~0.30 GiB per GPU (our math). The catch is that the drafter builds its own ParallelLMHead and its own mixer without a quantization config, and overlay/mtp.diff exists to pass quant_config to those two modules. The inventory's estimate for the whole FP8-dense setup is ≈9.2 → 5.7 GiB per step, never measured. Since both paths rewrite the same file, today you choose between cutting rows (vocabulary) and cutting bits (FP8). In principle, nothing prevents a patch that does both (our interpretation).

Two GPUs, one argmax: the TP-aware version

The original implementation (Mia's single-Spark repo) only worked on a single GPU and refused to engage under TP. The reason: with tensor parallel, the lm_head is vocab-parallel, and each rank holds a contiguous range of ids (chapter 38). On Qwen TP2, rank 0 holds ids 0–124,159 and rank 1, ids 124,160–248,319: 124,160 rows each, 0.59 GiB per GPU. Each GPU therefore computes logits only for its half of the vocabulary. The TP-aware version has each rank cut its own piece, keeping only the ids of \(S\) that fall in its range, [org_vocab_start_index, org_vocab_end_index).

This is where a tokenization detail comes in. Byte-level BPE vocabularies are built in merge order, and the most frequent merges get low ids (chapter 5). A draft vocabulary of frequent ids is therefore dense at the start of the table: of the 47,149 ids, 45,734 land on rank 0 and only 1,415 on rank 1, about 97% on a single rank. Rank 0 reads 0.22 GiB per draft; rank 1, ~0.01 GiB. But the step only ends when the slowest rank finishes, because the two meet in a collective. That's why the gain on the two-node kit (+8 to +10%) is smaller than on single-Spark (+13.1%): there, the single GPU went from 1.18 to 0.22 GiB; here, the critical rank goes from 0.59 to 0.22 GiB.

Analogy: two librarians, one dictionary torn in half

Picture a dictionary split between two librarians: one keeps pages A through M, the other N through Z. The question “what's the most likely word?” is only answered when both have replied. Now give each of them a list of the words “that tend to come up”. If almost all the common words start with A through M, the first librarian is still flipping through a pile of pages, while the second finishes in seconds and sits there with arms crossed, waiting. Service is never faster than the busiest librarian.

What's left is to combine the winners from the two ranks. The default path does an all-gather of the logits of both halves (each rank sends its half and everyone receives the whole vector): 248,320 values per token. The patch's get_top_tokens does the same thing as vLLM's own LogitsProcessor.get_top_tokens, a pairwise distributed argmax:

\[ (m_r, j_r) = \Big(\max_{i \in S_r} \ell_i,\; \mathrm{id}\big(\arg\max_{i\in S_r} \ell_i\big)\Big), \qquad r^\star = \arg\max_r m_r, \qquad \hat x = j_{r^\star} \]
  • \(S_r\): the draft vocabulary ids that live on rank \(r\)
  • \(m_r\): the largest local logit; \(j_r\): the corresponding global id (via _draft_id_to_target_id)
  • a rank with no ids sends \(m_r = -\infty\), a “losing bid”
  • communication: \(2 \cdot \text{tp}\) values per token, versus \(V\) in the logits all-gather

Two subtleties make the result exact. The logit scale and the tanh soft-cap are monotonically increasing functions, applied identically on every rank, so they can be skipped without changing the local argmax or the comparison between ranks. Hence the refusal when the scale isn't positive: a negative scale would flip the order. And the global id travels as float32 in the same tensor as the value; floats represent integers exactly up to \(2^{24} \approx 16.8\) million, plenty of headroom for 248,320 (our note).

Analogy: a sealed-bid auction

The logits all-gather is like asking each librarian to read aloud the score of every one of their words to the other. The pairwise argmax is a sealed-bid auction: each one writes down only their best bid (the score) and the item's name (the global id), and the highest envelope wins. Whoever has no item on the list sends an envelope with \(-\infty\), a bid that always loses. For the auction to be fair, both must bid in the same currency: that's why the scale and the soft-cap can be skipped (they're the same for everyone and don't change the order), but a negative scale, which would flip the order, is refused.

The “balanced” scenario has the smallest read of all, 0.16 GiB per rank, and it was indeed the fastest on prose, code and entropy: 42.5 / 44.5 / 45.4 tok/s, versus 40.0 / 43.2 / 40.1 with the full vocabulary. But acceptance fell from 56.5% to 47.8%, and the copy task ended up below the full vocabulary (56.5 versus 59.1 tok/s). The recipe logged this as a real trade-off, not a free win, and left that file off by default. Bandwidth and acceptance pull in opposite directions, and the next section shows where the acceptance loss most likely came from.

Parameter"use_local_argmax_reduction": true

start.sh adds this key to the --speculative-config whenever MTP_DRAFT_VOCAB isn't empty. It makes the speculator call get_top_tokens instead of compute_logits + argmax, and it's the only path that reaches the reduced head. As a bonus, it cuts the draft's collective from \(O(V)\) to \(O(2\cdot\text{tp})\) values per token. The upstream default is False, and the key only applies to greedy, non-tree drafting.

And DeepSeek?

DSpark has an equivalent, VLLM_DSPARK_LOCAL_ARGMAX: a local vocab-parallel argmax with an exchange of (value, index) pairs, instead of the all-gather of the draft's logits. In the Stage-C lane the override turns the variable on (1); in the default Anemll image it isn't even registered. It's flagged as experimental because the Markov head's sequential sampling may require a per-position synchronization.

Workshop: build the vocabulary by coverage, not by size

The recipe ships a ready-made file, but the goal here is for you to know how to make your own. The golden rule is in the builder's docstring: “coverage, not size, is the number to tune on”. And the ideal corpus is the model's own output, because that's what the drafter tries to predict; prompts or a generic corpus are only approximations of that distribution. (Curiously, the 47k file the recipe uses came from one of those approximations and works well on code; the end of the section tells that story.)

  1. Generate the corpus. bench/gen_draft_corpus.py calls the live endpoint with varied prompts (prose on 16 topics, modules in 7 languages, math, JSON, email, one prompt in Chinese) and writes each response as {"text": …} to a .jsonl. Defaults: --per-prompt 2, --max-tokens 1200, --temperature 0.8, top_p 0.95, --concurrency 4.
  2. Count. files/build_draft_vocab.py tokenizes the corpus with the served model's tokenizer (--model, whose default DRAFT_VOCAB_MODEL is nvidia/Qwen3.8-Flash-Next-NVFP4; it must be the same 248,320-token vocabulary) and counts the occurrences of each id. The corpus.jsonl:N suffix repeats the file N times, so that a small corpus faithful to the traffic weighs as much as a large generic one.
  3. Protect the specials. Every special or added token always goes in, whatever its frequency. They're only a few hundred rows, and losing them would cost acceptance precisely at structural boundaries (end of turn, tool tags), where drafting is easiest.
  4. Rank and cut. The most frequent ids are kept, up to --size (default 32,768; the README suggests 65,536).
  5. Fill and balance (optional). --fill-to-size tops up with the lowest ids not yet seen, betting on BPE merge order (“a proxy, not a measurement”). --balance-shards (use the TP value) spreads that filler evenly across each rank's id range, without ever dropping ids that came from the corpus.
  6. Read the report. The script prints the corpus coverage at 8k, 16k, 32k, 65k and 131k ids, and how many occurrences are left out (“those become rejected drafts, never wrong output”). With --report-only, it stops there without writing anything: pick the size before generating the file.

The figure is the workbench. Pick the corpus, slide the size and turn on the filler. The curve is the frequency distribution on a log-log scale. Whatever lies left of the cut is real coverage; whatever goes past the end of the corpus is guesswork filler.

The story of the 65,536-id vocabulary is the workbench's lesson. The corpus used had 85 documents and only 8,305 distinct ids, and everything above that was filler by id order. Without balancing, filling with the lowest ids put 65,392 of the 65,536 on rank 0 and saved bandwidth on one rank only (acceptance 53.2%). With --balance-shards 2, rank 1's filler became a range of ids the corpus never justified: the step got faster on three of the four tasks, but acceptance fell to 47.8%. The CHANGELOG itself points to guesswork filler as the most likely explanation for the loss (“a proxy, not a measurement”), not balancing per se: ~57k of the 65,536 ids never appeared in the corpus.

The file the recipe adopted afterwards, the 47k one, came from another corpus: 30 MiB of host code and documentation, which isn't model output, vendored unchanged from the single-Spark repo (where it measured +13.1%). It has 100% coverage of its own corpus and 99.58% on a separate corpus, and with it tokens per step stayed practically the same as with the full head (3.80 → 3.85 on code). The CHANGELOG lists as untested a larger corpus for the 65k vocabulary and the corpus-only vocabulary of ~8.3k ids, which would cut the draft head to ~0.02 GiB per rank. A larger corpus generated by the model itself with gen_draft_corpus.py, as the golden rule asks, is the natural experiment for anyone building their own.

A second path: extend the floor with a language

Building from scratch isn't the only way out. On 2026-09-29 (commit 288369d) the recipe gained the “extend” method, ported from the single-Spark repo (files/build_draft_vocab_extend.py): instead of throwing the 47k file away, it gets extended with a language. The docstring tells why the script exists: rebuilding from scratch with a dictionary as the corpus “dropped 90 byte-fallback ids and destroyed Spanish”. Three rules came out of that failure:

  1. The floor goes in whole. All 47,149 ids of the 47k file stay, so no id that already works is lost, and English and code drafting doesn't change.
  2. Byte-fallback and specials, always. Every id below 400 (--byte-fallback-max) and every special token goes in unconditionally, whatever the frequencies say. The byte-fallback ids assemble every multi-byte UTF-8 character (accented Latin, Cyrillic, CJK, emoji): without them, the drafter can't propose any character outside the main vocabulary.
  3. Ids are only added by frequency, over real text. The new ids come from counting over 668 MiB of that language's Wikipedia, up to 65,536 rows. Never a dictionary, where every inflected form weighs as much as “que”.

The arithmetic checks out on the files: 65,536 = 47,149 from the floor + 18,387 new ids. Of those, 24 are byte-fallback ids the floor didn't have (it carried 376 of the 400) and the rest came in by frequency (our count on the seven files). To switch, set MTP_DRAFT_VOCAB=files/draft_vocab_<lang>_en_code_65k.txt in .env; correctness doesn't change, because the target still verifies everything.

Beyond the DGX Spark

Nothing here depends on the GB10. Any server with greedy drafting and a vocab-parallel head (vLLM or SGLang on an H200 node with TP=8, for example) can cut the drafter's head with a per-language vocabulary, and the “extend” rules hold as they are: whole floor, pinned byte-fallback, frequency over real text from your traffic. With a larger TP, the concentration of frequent ids matters more: each rank holds a narrower range, and rank 0's range sits almost entirely inside the frequent vocabulary. With the 47k file, rank 0 would still read 37% of its shard at TP=2, 58% at TP=4 and 72% at TP=8 (our arithmetic on the file). And the measurement method, with a control in the same boot and ABBA when boot-to-boot noise is as large as the effect, works for any server A/B.

The block EAGLE used to throw away

Now for a cost that doesn't show up in decode, but in TTFT. Prefix caching reuses KV blocks from an already-seen prefix (chapter 33). With an EAGLE-style drafter, though, vLLM plays it safe: it drops the last matched block and recomputes it. The reason is the shift from section 1. The drafter's input at position \(p\) is \((h_p, x_{p+1})\), so what it stored for the last position of a block depends on the first token of the next block, which may not be the same for every request sharing that prefix. On top of that, to resume drafting after a hit, the drafter needs the target's hidden states at the boundary, and a hit is precisely what skips that computation. Recomputing one block solves both problems at once.

Analogy: rereading the last page just in case

You pick up a book at the bookmark but, just to be safe, reread the whole last page before continuing, because its final sentence ran over onto the next page and you don't remember how it went. In a paperback, rereading a page takes seconds. In a hybrid model, the “page” (the block) is 1,664 tokens; and, with bug #62, the absent-minded reader went all the way back to the start of the book.

The price depends on the block size, and in a hybrid model it's large. vLLM makes the attention page match the state page of the GDN layers, and Qwen's block reaches 1,664 tokens with MTP 3. That's the value quoted in the patch; the README says 1,600, and the exact number depends on KV dtype × SSM dtype × TP. Worse: the day-0 image had bug #62, and a session's second turn lost the entire cache. In a hybrid model, a hit only counts where a checkpoint of the recurrent state exists, and backing off one block landed on a point with no saved state (our reading, tied to the aligned-hits mechanism of chapter 33).

An order-of-magnitude check (our math): 3.25 s is almost exactly the time it takes to redo ~9.5k tokens at the prefill rate measured on this lane (~2.9k tok/s at 8k). In other words, the second turn was paying for the whole prompt all over again.

The KV connector files don't apply when there's no connector, and the PR's sliding-window file was left out because QSA has no SWA. If the image already knows the option, the patch mounts nothing. Result (medians of 3 sessions of 9.5k; the CHANGELOG counts turns from 0, so its “turn 1” is the session's 2nd turn): 2nd turn, 3.25 → 0.63 s; 3rd turn, 1.12 → 0.59 s; turn after 4k of tool output, 2.55 → 1.98 s. Decode, prefill and NLL didn't change. The log shows EAGLE trailing prefix-cache block dropping is disabled. .env.sample ships 1; if the key is missing from your .env, start.sh's fallback is 0. In vLLM 0.30, the option is native and #62 was fixed upstream (vllm#54713, vllm#53945).

What can go wrong

Turning off the drop doesn't change the output distribution, because the target verifies everything. What can change is acceptance right after a hit, since the drafter starts from a boundary context that wasn't recomputed (“the drafter still runs; the change can move acceptance only”). On GLM, the problem looked different. DFlash2 counts as use_eagle(), and the coordinator marked all KV groups as EAGLE; patch_hybrid_prefix_hit.py changed it to mark only the drafter's exact SlidingWindowSpec groups (eagle_group_ids; chapter 33).

IndexShare: one indexer for all three drafts

Qwen's MTP layer isn't just any layer: it uses QSA attention, which is sparse. A quick recap (chapter 13): a cheap indexer, with 4 query heads, 1 shared key and dimension 128, scores micro-blocks of 4 keys compressed by averaging, picks the 512 best blocks (2,048 tokens) and appends the tail of up to 3 tokens from the still-incomplete block. Only then does the real attention run, over those ~2,051 positions.

\[ I_{i,b} = \sum_{h=1}^{4} \mathrm{ReLU}\big(\langle q^{h}_i,\, \bar k_b \rangle\big), \qquad T_i = \mathrm{TopK}_{512}\,\{\, b : p_b + r - 1 \le i \,\} \;\cup\; \mathrm{tail}(i) \]
  • \(q^h_i\): query of indexer head \(h\) at position \(i\)
  • \(\bar k_b\): compressed key (mean of \(r=4\) tokens) of block \(b\), which starts at position \(p_b\)
  • \(T_i\): the blocks that position \(i\)'s attention will read, chosen by the largest \(I_{i,b}\)
  • only complete blocks compete; the up to 3 most recent tokens always go in

With \(k=3\), the MTP layer runs three times per step, and each time the indexer would sweep the whole context: the scoring kernel (_qsa_mqa_paged_kernel), persistent_topk and the index expansion. But between the 1st and the 3rd draft, the query moved at most two positions. IndexShare (index_share_for_mtp_iteration) computes \(T\) once, in draft-extend, and reuses that set in the following steps: \(T_{i+j} \approx T_i\) for \(j \le k-1\). The possible error is small and localized: a block that completed in the meantime (with \(r=4\) and \(j \le 2\), at most one) and the score drift of a selection whose indices are “one position stale”.

The saving is in kernel time, not bandwidth: the indexer reads compressed keys, far fewer bytes than attention. That's why the performance report's prediction was modest, +1–3%. The measured gain was larger on code at high concurrency: +6.1 / +4.6 / +21.3 / +26.6% with S = 1/2/4/8, and prose within noise (+0.3 / +5.3 / −0.1 / −1.8%). Quality doesn't budge, by construction, because the target verifies everything: NLL of 1.328 versus 1.334 (and NLL doesn't even see the drafter) and HumanEval 156/164 on the IndexShare arm. Qwen's tech report compared MTP with full attention (mean accepted length of 4.06) and with QSA plus index reuse (4.07), with 4 speculative steps: sparsity and reuse, together, cost no acceptance. It isn't an isolated comparison of reuse versus no reuse, but it's the closest published data.

Analogy: checking the map once for three short steps

Someone walking three steps down a familiar street doesn't open the GPS at every step: they check the map once and keep going. The risk is small and localized: if, during those three steps, a new street opens right beside you (a micro-block that just completed), you'll only see it at the next check. And what just happened, the recent tail, you see anyway, because it always goes in.

On GLM

The four levers of a cheap draft

Fewer bytes per draft (reduced vocabulary and pairwise argmax), less work per draft (IndexShare), less prefill per turn (keeping the prefix cache block) and more accepted tokens per step (choosing \(k\) by per-position acceptance and building the vocabulary by coverage, preferably over the model's real output). All of them touch only the drafter; the output distribution remains the target's. That's what makes these tweaks safe to experiment with, as long as you measure each one.

Checklist: the MTP of a new checkpoint

The goal is for you to put together a recipe like these on your own. Here's the chapter's roadmap, condensed and applied to MiMo-V2.6-Flash-MOPD, the model that chapter 46 takes from the model card to start.sh. The values come from the config; the math is ours, and none of this was measured on the GB10.

  1. How many MTP layers are there? Read num_nextn_predict_layers in config.json and look for the mtp.* tensors in the weight index. Apply the reuse rule: \(k \le n_{\text{predict}}\) or \(k\) a multiple of \(n_{\text{predict}}\). On MiMo: num_nextn_predict_layers: 3 (model.mtp.layers.0-2, 1.19 GB), and the tutorial works with \(k\) from 1 to 3 in the mtp method.
  2. How much does the head cost? Compute \(V \cdot d \cdot 2 / \text{TP}\) bytes per read, multiply by \(k\) (autoregressive drafting) and compare with the rest of the step. On MiMo: \(152{,}576 \times 4096 \times 2\) B ≈ 1.25 GB; on TP2, ≈0.58 GiB per rank per read, or ≈1.75 GiB per step with \(k=3\), if each draft reads the whole head, as on Qwen. If that slice weighs close to the rest of the step, a reduced vocabulary and an FP8 head go on the test list.
  3. Is the head its own matrix? tie_word_embeddings tells you whether the lm_head is a matrix separate from the embedding (on MiMo, false: 1.25 GB in BF16). Also check, in vLLM's MTP model file, whether the drafter reuses the target's head or builds its own (on Qwen, it builds its own ParallelLMHead). That's the module a vocabulary or FP8 patch needs to touch.
  4. Where do the frequent ids land? Generate the corpus from the model's own output, run build_draft_vocab.py --report-only and count how many ids fall in each TP range. On MiMo with TP2, the boundary is at id 76,288. If ~97% land on a single rank, as on Qwen, the bandwidth gain is capped by the slowest rank.
  5. Measure per position, always as a delta. Take a snapshot of spec_decode_num_accepted_tokens_per_pos_total in /metrics before and after each test (Qwen's bench/mtp_accept.py does this), because the cumulative counter mixes every request the server has ever handled. A decreasing curve is expected; near-random acceptance gives away wrong weights or scales in the MTP. Choose \(k\) with the rule \(a_{k+1} > \tau\,\Delta t / t\).
  6. Check the engine's precautions. Every use_eagle() method inherits the drop of the last matched block. In a hybrid model, with large blocks, measure the 2nd-turn TTFT with and without disable_eagle_block_drop.

And don't forget that the right method depends on the content. On SGLang, MiMo's MTP beat DFlash on prose (35.0 versus 25.8 tok/s) and lost on code (46.0 versus 69.8), as measured by MiaAI-Lab; on vLLM, this model's mtp was only tested with \(k=1\). It's the same message as section 2, now on a model you have yet to serve.

MTP and EAGLE pay for the draft token by token: \(k\) forwards, \(k\) head reads. The next chapter changes the question: what if the drafter filled the whole block at once? That's the idea behind DFlash2 and DSpark, which also inherit from this chapter the EAGLE-3 taps and use_eagle() (chapter 37).
