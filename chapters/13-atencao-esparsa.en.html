Imagine pasting a whole repository, an 800-page contract or months of conversation into a single prompt: a million tokens. In “textbook” attention, every new token compares its query against all the previous keys. The bill grows with the square of the context and the KV cache turns into an ocean. In this chapter you'll meet the three ideas that make that context possible in the recipes: windows that forget on purpose, cheap indexers that pick where the expensive attention will look, and compression of the KV itself. By the end you'll be able to read every parameter of these ideas (sliding_window, index_topk, index_kpool, compress_ratios…) and understand every patch the recipes had to apply around them.

The quadratic wall

Recall the mechanism from the attention chapter. For the query \(\mathbf q_i\) of token \(i\), the model computes a score against each key \(\mathbf k_j\) at position \(j \le i\) (the causal mask forbids looking at the future), passes those scores through the softmax and uses the weights to sum the values \(\mathbf v_j\). None of these operations is expensive on its own. The problem is how many times they happen.

\[ \text{prefill:}\ \ \underbrace{\frac{n(n+1)}{2}}_{\text{pairs }(i,\,j)} \times H \times L \] \[ \text{decode:}\ \ \underbrace{n \cdot L \cdot b_{\mathrm{KV}}}_{\text{bytes read}} \ \ \text{per new token} \]
  • prefill: total number of \(\mathbf q \cdot \mathbf k\) dot products needed to read the prompt
  • decode: bytes of KV read for every generated token
  • \(n\): tokens in the context
  • \(H\): attention heads per layer
  • \(L\): layers that do softmax attention
  • \(b_{\mathrm{KV}}\): KV bytes stored per token in each layer

Plug in some numbers (our own math, just to get a feel for the scale). With \(n = 1{,}048{,}576\) tokens, the causal triangle has about \(5.5 \times 10^{11}\) pairs per head and per layer. A hypothetically dense DeepSeek-V4-Flash, with 64 heads and 43 layers, would do some \(1.5 \times 10^{15}\) 512-dimensional dot products just to read the prompt. Decode is no better: with the model's 584 B cache row, every new token would have to read \(43 \times 584 \times 1{,}048{,}576 \approx 26.3\) GB of KV. At around 230–250 GB/s (the effective bandwidth the recipes estimate for the GB10's LPDDR5x memory), that's more than 0.1 s per token just to read the cache, a ceiling of under 10 tokens/s before touching a single weight.

The first step toward escaping is to notice that the attention matrix, after the softmax, is almost empty. Each query concentrates its probability mass on a few keys: the recent neighborhood, a handful of “anchor” tokens and the passages that answer the question at hand. If we know in advance which pairs matter, we can skip the rest. That is sparse attention. The hard question is how to know in advance, and cheaply. Notice that the masks answer different questions. The window uses a fixed rule (“only the last \(W\)”) and doesn't need to think. Top-k decides by content, so it needs someone to score the keys first. Pools and compression change the unit of reading: instead of tokens, blocks of 4 or 128 tokens summarized into a single entry. The recipes combine these pieces, and the next sections take each one apart.

Analogy: the library of a million books

Dense attention is the reader who opens every book in the library for every question. The sliding window reads only the new-arrivals shelf. The indexer is a librarian who flips through the catalog cards (cheap summaries) and brings the 2,048 most promising books to the table. Compression swaps each box of 4 (or 128) books for a set of reading notes, and the reader starts consulting the notes instead.

Before getting into the mechanism, it's worth seeing how the per-query cost of each strategy grows with the context. The formulas are simple: dense attention reads \(n\) entries; the window reads \(\min(n, W)\); GLM's DSA and Qwen's QSA read at most ~2,048 tokens; a DeepSeek C4A layer reads \(\min(n,128) + \min(\lfloor n/4 \rfloor, 512)\) entries and a C128A layer reads \(\min(n,128) + \lfloor n/128 \rfloor\).

Two lessons jump out of the figure. The first is that the sparse attention curves go flat: past the budget, reading a 1M context costs the same as reading an 8K one. That's why DeepSeek's decode stays nearly constant (≈ 62–83 tok/s with one sequence, from 256 to 128K tokens). The second is the dashed curve: the indexer keeps growing with \(n\). Sparsity didn't abolish the quadratic term of prefill; it only swapped a gigantic constant for a small one. Keep that in mind, because this curve will explain half the patches in this chapter.

Why this matters

There are three escape routes from the quadratic wall, and all three models use more than one: read less (windows and top-k, this chapter), store less (the latent KV of MLA, this chapter's C4/C128 compression and quantized KV) and trade memory for a fixed-size state (linear attention, the KDA and GDN layers that fill 3 out of every 4 layers of GLM and Qwen). Knowing which route each layer takes is what lets you do the memory math for a recipe.

Sliding window: forgetting on purpose

The oldest strategy is also the simplest. In Sliding Window Attention (SWA), token \(i\) only sees the last \(W\) tokens, itself included. In terms of an additive mask (the one added to the scores before the softmax):

\[ M^{\mathrm{SWA}}_{ij} = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } 0 \le i - j < W \\ -\infty & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \] \[ \mathrm{slot}(j) = j \bmod W \]
  • \(W\): window size (sliding_window)
  • \(-\infty\): the pair vanishes from the softmax (\(e^{-\infty} = 0\))
  • \(\mathrm{slot}(j)\): where token \(j\)'s KV lives in the ring of \(W\) positions

Two properties change everything for whoever serves the model. The cost per token becomes \(O(W)\), and the KV per sequence becomes constant: \(W\) rows per layer are enough, reused as a ring, because token \(j\) overwrites the slot of token \(j - W\), which nobody will ever read again. The DeepSeek-V4 reference implementation does exactly that in decode: self.kv_cache[:bsz, start_pos % win] = kv, with win = 128.

Notice the hidden cost: the ring really does forget. Stacking layers gives a theoretical receptive field of \(L\,(W-1)+1\) tokens, but distant information arrives secondhand, like in a game of telephone. For a windows-only DeepSeek-V4, that reach would be \(43 \times 127 + 1 = 5{,}462\) tokens (our math), a far cry from 1M. That's why none of the models uses SWA alone in every layer: it is a component, combined with a global mechanism.

Where the windows show up in the recipes

Parametersliding_window: 128

In DeepSeek-V4, every layer has a window of 128 raw tokens. Layers 0 and 1 are window only (compress_ratio = 0, which vLLM raises to 1). The other 41 add a compressed, global branch to the window, which you'll see later on, and the 3 stages of the DSpark drafter also use a 128-token window. The window plays a subtle role, too: compressed entries only exist for blocks that are already closed, so the block still being formed is seen precisely through the window. Cost: 46 layers × 128 rows × 584 B ≈ 3.4 MB per sequence, constant, replicated on both ranks.

DrafterDFlash2: 5× sliding_attention, window 2048

The GLM target has no SWA, but the DFlash2 drafter does: 5 layers with a 2,048-token window and BF16 KV (2,048 B/token/layer at TP2), about 20 MiB with the window full. Because it's a window, it becomes its own KV group (SlidingWindowSpec) in vLLM's hybrid manager. In the version of the recipe, the engine keeps the drafter's context in a ring of 2,176 rows (the window, one block and one tile), about 21 MiB per rank however long the conversation gets. The drafter details are in the DFlash2 and DSpark chapter.

Pitfall: windows and prefix caching

Because vLLM frees the blocks that leave the window, a long prefix may no longer have its window KV at the point where you want to reuse it. In DeepSeek this zeroed out the prefix caching hit rate (issue #26). The v2 fix lets the window shrink the hit and keeps window “tails” every VLLM_PREFIX_CACHE_RETENTION_INTERVAL=4096 tokens. The DSpark drafter, in turn, has to recompute the last 128 tokens on every hit (DSPARK_ENABLE_DSPARK_SWA_PREFIX). And there's a position detail: according to the reference, YaRN ×16 only applies to the layers with a compressor, and the opt-in DSPARK_ENABLE_ROPE_SWA_FIX gives plain RoPE back to the window-only layers (RoPE chapter).

The lightning indexer: a cheap scout

Windows don't know what's important, only what's recent. To find the needle in a haystack 900 thousand tokens away, you have to decide by content. The idea behind DeepSeek Sparse Attention (DSA), which debuted in DeepSeek-V3.2, is to split attention into two stages: a very cheap lightning indexer scores all the keys and makes a top-k selection, keeping the best \(k\). Then the expensive attention, the real one, runs only over the chosen ones.

\[ I_{t,s} = \sum_{h=1}^{H_I} w_{t,h}\,\mathrm{ReLU}\!\left(\mathbf q^{I}_{t,h}\cdot \mathbf k^{I}_{s}\right) \] \[ S_t = \operatorname*{top-k}_{s \le t}\, I_{t,s} \] \[ \mathbf o_t = \sum_{s \in S_t} \operatorname{softmax}_{s \in S_t}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf q_t \cdot \mathbf k_s}{\sqrt d}\right) \mathbf v_s \]
  • \(I_{t,s}\): the indexer's score for key \(s\) as seen by token \(t\)
  • \(H_I\): indexer heads (few of them)
  • \(\mathbf q^{I}_{t,h}\): indexer query, head \(h\), with a small dimension (128)
  • \(\mathbf k^{I}_{s}\): one indexer key per token, shared by all heads
  • \(w_{t,h}\): weight of each head, computed from token \(t\) itself
  • \(S_t\): set of the \(k\) chosen keys (index_topk)
  • \(\mathbf q_t, \mathbf k_s, \mathbf v_s\): query, key and value of the main attention

Every detail of the formula is there to make the scout cheaper:

  1. Few heads and a small dimension. The indexer uses 128 dimensions, versus 512–576 for MLA attention, and between 4 and 64 heads.
  2. One key per token. The key \(\mathbf k^I_s\) is shared by all the indexer heads (the MQA scheme), so the indexer cache stores only 128 numbers per token (or per block, as we'll see).
  3. Low precision. GLM and DeepSeek-V3.2 score in FP8, and DeepSeek-V4 in FP4. Before quantizing, queries and keys go through a Hadamard rotation \(\mathbf H\). Since \(\mathbf H\) is orthogonal, \(\langle \mathbf H\mathbf q, \mathbf H\mathbf k\rangle = \langle \mathbf q, \mathbf k\rangle\): the rotation doesn't change the exact score, it only spreads the outliers across all channels before FP8/FP4, and the rounding error shrinks. The catch is that you must rotate both sides. In GLM the compressed keys are stored already rotated (\(\mathbf H\mathbf k\)), and without also rotating the query, the needle's pool dropped out of the top-k.
  4. ReLU instead of softmax. The indexer doesn't need probabilities, only an order. With ReLU each head merely “votes for” the keys it likes (negative evidence becomes zero), and there's no need to normalize over all the keys.
  5. One selection per token, shared. The sum over \(h\) produces one set \(S_t\) per token and per layer, used by every head of the main attention. With a single KV head (MLA/MQA), the kernel does one gather of 2,048 rows and every head consumes them. That's why DSA and MLA go together so well.

All told, per query it costs \(1/32\) (DeepSeek), \(1/68\) (GLM) and \(1/96\) (Qwen) of a dense attention. It's still \(O(n)\) per token, just with a tiny constant. When the context is smaller than the budget (\(n \le k\)), top-k picks everything and sparse attention is dense attention: that's what GLM's docs/improve-prefill.md says for contexts of up to 2,048.

What about storage?

The indexer reduces what attention reads, not what it stores. The main KV for every position stays in the cache, because tomorrow another query may pick a key nobody wanted today. In GLM and Qwen, therefore, the KV of the sparse layers grows linearly with the context, just like in dense attention. Only DeepSeek-V4 also compresses what it stores, and that's two sections away.

Deep dive: DSA, NSA and relatives

NSA (Native Sparse Attention), proposed by DeepSeek before DSA, organizes sparse attention into three branches summed by learned gates: a coarse attention over block summaries, a fine attention over the selected blocks and a sliding window, all at block granularity to match the hardware. The name lives on in FlashInfer: the geometry of the sparse MLA kernel GLM uses is called GLM_NSA. Our interpretation: DeepSeek-V4 is a close cousin of NSA, with a window, a compressed branch and a selected branch. The difference is that it distributes the branches across layers (C4A and C128A) instead of summing them within the same layer.

How the scout learns: KL distillation

There's a training problem hidden in the formula: top-k isn't differentiable. The language loss has no way to tell the indexer “you should have picked key 812,337”, because a key that wasn't chosen simply doesn't appear in the computation. The solution in the public reports (DeepSeek-V3.2 for DSA, Qwen's tech report for QSA) is to treat the indexer as a student of the model's own dense attention, in a distillation:

\[ \mathcal L_I = \sum_t D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(p_{t,\cdot}\ \big\|\ \operatorname{softmax}(I_{t,\cdot})\right) \] \[ p_{t,s} = \frac{\sum_h a^{(h)}_{t,s}}{\sum_{s'} \sum_h a^{(h)}_{t,s'}} \] \[ D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p\|q) = \sum_s p_s \log\frac{p_s}{q_s} \]
  • \(a^{(h)}_{t,s}\): dense attention weight of head \(h\) (the teacher)
  • \(p_{t,\cdot}\): target distribution, summed over the heads and normalized
  • \(\operatorname{softmax}(I_{t,\cdot})\): the indexer's scores turned into a distribution (the student)
  • \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}\): KL divergence, zero when student = teacher

Training has two phases. In the dense warmup, the model is frozen, attention stays dense and only the indexer learns to imitate \(p\). In Qwen's QSA this took 1,000 steps, LR \(10^{-3}\), batches of 8 × 256K tokens, with the target aggregated by max-pooling over blocks of 4 (because QSA picks blocks, not tokens). In the joint sparse training phase, top-k is switched on for real and the whole model adapts to seeing only what the indexer picked. In Qwen this took 8,000 steps, LR \(2.5 \times 10^{-5}\), batches of 96 × 256K ≈ 200 billion tokens.

The figure shows why selection costs almost no quality: attention is concentrated. If, in a head \(h\), the top-k captures a fraction \(1 - \varepsilon_h\) of that head's attention mass, its sparse output (with the softmax renormalized over \(S_t\)) differs from the dense one by at most

\[ \big\lVert \mathbf o^{(h),\text{dense}}_t - \mathbf o^{(h),\text{sparse}}_t \big\rVert \ \le\ 2\,\varepsilon_h \max_s \lVert \mathbf v_s \rVert \] \[ \varepsilon_h = 1 - \sum_{s \in S_t} a^{(h)}_{t,s} \]
  • \(\varepsilon_h\): attention mass of head \(h\) that was left out
  • one \(\varepsilon_h\) comes from the ignored keys, the other from renormalizing the chosen ones
  • the bound holds for each head \(h\); with \(p\), the average over the heads, \(\varepsilon = 1 - \sum_{s\in S_t} p_{t,s}\) is the average of the \(\varepsilon_h\) and the bound holds on average over the heads

With 2,048 chosen out of a million, the indexer only has to get the head of the distribution right. And the second phase helps close the loop, because the model learns to concentrate its attention where the indexer knows how to look. Qwen's numbers confirm it: on RULER, QSA ties full attention up to 128K (99.89 vs. 99.84) and beats it from 512K to 1M (93.00 vs. 90.08), with the same pattern on 8-needle MRCR (40.53 vs. 30.66 at 512K). Interpretation: in huge contexts, cutting off the noisy tail distracts the model less.

Pitfall: quantizing the keys changes the choice

The indexer is a ranking, and rankings are fragile near the cutoff. Quantizing K (or the indexer cache) shifts the scores and can change which blocks get picked, not just the output. In the Qwen recipe, FP8 KV gives 1.70× more tokens and passes 12/12 on reasoning_check, but the accepted tokens per step drop in all 8 measured cells. Upstream reported a long-reasoning test falling from 6/6 to 2/6 with FP8 KV, not reproduced in the recipe. In DeepSeek, the MXFP4 indexer cache (--attention-config {"use_fp4_indexer_cache":true}, opt-in) reads half the bytes, at the risk of the selection drifting in deep context. Details in the quantized KV chapter.

Compressing the keys: pools of 4 in GLM and QSA

The dashed curve from the first section grows because the indexer scores one key per token. The next trick is to score one key per block: \(r = 4\) neighboring keys become one, the indexer cache shrinks 4× and so does the scoring. The selection becomes a selection of blocks (512 blocks × 4 = 2,048 tokens), which on top of that are contiguous in memory and cheaper to fetch. GLM and Qwen do this in different ways, and GLM has a history of patches worth telling.

QSA: Qwen Sparse Attention

In Qwen3.8's 12 QSA layers (indices 3, 7, …, 47, one out of every four, plus the MTP layer), the indexer has indexer_n_heads=4 query heads with indexer_head_dim=128 and a single key head (indexer_kv_heads=1). For each micro-block of indexer_compress_ratio=4 tokens, the keys are summarized by a mean in FP32, normalized (RMSNorm) and only then rotated with RoPE at the block's starting position, so as not to mix different rotation phases. The score of a block \(b\) for token \(i\) is

\[ s_{i,b} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{128}} \sum_{h=1}^{4} \mathrm{ReLU}\!\left(\langle \mathbf q^{h}_i,\ \bar{\mathbf k}_b \rangle\right) \] \[ \text{only for complete blocks: } p_b + r - 1 \le i \]
  • \(\bar{\mathbf k}_b\): compressed key of block \(b\) (mean of 4 keys)
  • \(p_b\): position of the block's first token
  • \(r = 4\): tokens per micro-block

The best 512 blocks give 2,048 positions (indexer_budget=2048). On top of those come the up to 3 tokens of the still-incomplete block at the end of the context, the tail, which has no compressed key and is always included: at most 2,051 positions. Then a regular GQA runs (24 query heads, 2 KV heads, head_dim 256, with an output gate) over the original K/V of those positions. In the day-0 image the pipeline is made entirely of custom Triton kernels: fused pre-indexer (norm + RoPE + key write) → _qsa_mqa_paged_kernel (scores) → persistent_topk → index expansion → sparse GQA in split-K. In a kernel microbenchmark at 1M (batch 4, next_n 4), the tech report measures QSA at 7.6× faster than dense GQA in prefill and 4.9× in decode, and the indexer with \(r = 4\) at 3.8× (prefill) and 4.4× (decode) faster than with \(r = 1\). These are gains in the attention kernel, not in the end-to-end model, which also spends time on MoE, GDN and communication.

A detail that only shows up when you want reproducibility: top-k isn't deterministic for free. The top-k kernel returns the right set, but the order of the indices within each row can vary from one run to the next. Since floating-point addition isn't associative, the sparse GQA that walks the indices in that order gives results that differ in the last bits, and in greedy decoding one bit can flip a token somewhere down the line. The Qwen recipe's opt-in VLLM_QSA_DET_TOPK=1 sorts each row's indices (the −1 padding goes to the end). Together with VLLM_MOE_DET_FINALIZE=1, which swaps MoE's fused finalize for a fixed-order one, the logprob difference between identical runs drops from 0.25/6.07 nats to 0/0 and the greedy output becomes bit-identical. It costs about 4% in decode and prefill, which is why it ships turned off.

GLM: the kpool indexer

In GLM-5.3-Flash's 11 MLA layers (3, 7, …, 43, plus the MTP), the kpool indexer has index_n_heads=32 heads of index_head_dim=128, and the keys are also grouped into pools of index_kpool=4. Here the pool isn't a mean: it's a learned pooling (index_kpool_compress: true). A gate gives each of the 4 tokens a per-channel score, adds a per-slot positional bias (index_kpool_compress_ape [4×128]) and takes a softmax over the 4. Then come Hadamard-128 and FP8, and each pool becomes a 132 B entry (128 B FP8 + 4 B scale), 33 B per token per layer. Since GLM's MLA layers are NoPE, the indexer has no RoPE either, and the pool's small positional bias is one of the rare explicit sources of order.

So far, it all looks like QSA. The trouble is at the edges. The budget is index_topk=2048 tokens, so select_k = 2048/4 = 512 pools, and index_kpool_always_select_tail: true adds the tail of up to 3 tokens (stored in its own KV group, KpoolTail). Result: up to 2,051 candidates, which the stock code stores in a buffer rounded up to 2,176 (a multiple of 128). But in vLLM, the only sparse MLA kernel for the GB10 takes the top-k as a template parameter: prefill rejects any value other than 2,048 in the GLM_NSA geometry. The recipe solves this in the Dockerfile, and the figure shows how.

The fix has three parts. buffer_width = topk_tokens removes the “+ (kpool − 1)” from the buffer width. The expansion now uses select_k − 1 = 511 pools (2,044 tokens) plus the tail (≤ 3), at most 2,047 candidates, within the 2,048. And the backend now treats top-k as a capacity, with valid counts per row (return_valid_counts=True): rows without candidates point to slot 0 with length 1 and get their output zeroed. The cost is giving up 4 candidates out of 2,048 (0.2%), precisely the lowest-ranked ones, while preserving the recent tail.

select_k stays at 512 because the Dockerfile wants to preserve the indexer's fast fused top-k path, which the code only takes when select_k ∈ {512, 1024, 2048}. There's a tension here that the sources don't resolve. The overlay patch_glm_video_placeholders.py replaces the first occurrence of that very condition, if current_platform.is_cuda() and select_k in (512, 1024, 2048):, with if False and …, and describes it as the decode persistent_topk, which overflows the GB10's shared memory (see the pitfall further on). Interpretation: if it's the same gate, the fused path survives only where the condition still appears intact (prefill), and in decode the recipe uses top_k_per_row_decode. The file sparse_attn_indexer_kpool.py isn't vendored in the repository, so it couldn't be checked line by line.

Why sacrifice the worst pool and not the tail

Truncating the 2,051-entry row to 2,048 would be the obvious solution, and it would throw away exactly the tail, the 1–3 most recent tokens. In a causal model they tend to carry the highest attention weight, and they can't be recovered through the pools, because they don't have a compressed entry yet. The 512th pool, on the other hand, is by construction the least relevant of the chosen ones. It's a recall decision: lose the smallest possible mass.

The pool also imposes rules on paging (PagedAttention chapter). The indexer cache is paged like the KV: a manager block of \(B\) tokens holds \(B/4\) pool entries, and DeepGEMM's paged logits kernel reads those entries in pages of block_kv rows. On sm_10x (datacenter Blackwell), DeepGEMM accepts a block_kv of 32, 64 or 128. On the SM12x family (sm_120 and the GB10's sm_121), only 64. So \(B/4\) has to be a multiple of 64, and the recipe changes the block alignment from index_kpool × 32 = 128 to index_kpool × 64 = 256 tokens. The Dockerfile's own example: a hybrid block of 1,664 tokens would give 416 entries, which isn't a multiple of 64 and trips an assert; once aligned, it becomes 1,792 tokens (448 = 7 × 64 entries). In the served configuration (TP2, DFlash with \(k = 7\)) the block is 3,584 tokens = 14 × 256, and that's where the indexer page the tail depends on lives.

The circular tail and two memory bugs

KpoolTail stores, per request, one circular block of block_size = 4 (= index_kpool): a ring for the keys and gates of the tokens that haven't closed a pool yet. Two overlays fix its addressing. patch_kpool_tail_slotmap.py exists because the generic slot mapping did block_table[req, pos // 4] with no bound: for \(pos \ge 4\) it read garbage, and with ~2k generated tokens there was a crash or silent corruption of another layer's indexer. The fix is block_indices = min(block_indices, block_table_stride − 1), which gives slot = block 0 · 4 + \(pos \bmod 4\). patch_kpool_tail_seed_stride.py (a backport of vLLM #57477) fixes the tail seed in prefill, which assumed a dense stride of 2,048 B per block. But the tail is an as_strided view of the indexer page: it reinterprets that page's memory with hand-picked strides, without copying, and the step between blocks is the size of the whole page, not the tail's. That page is 118,272 B in the recipe's 3,584-token block (896 entries × 132 B; stride(0) = 59136 BF16 elements), versus 38,016 B in upstream's 1,152-token block. Every prefill left its own tail unseeded and wrote key and gate into blocks 0–9 of another request. Finally, in prefix caching, the rule [glm53-kpool-replay-floor-v1] leaves at least 4 fresh tokens to rebuild the ring.

The same indexer on an engine with its own kernels

Everything we've seen in GLM so far is a negotiation between the model and an off-the-shelf kernel. The version of the recipe serves the same checkpoint on TensorFold, an engine that writes its own Triton and CUDA kernels for the GLM family (exact engine chapter). With no template to respect, the token list is as wide as the model asks: TOKENS = 512 × 4 + 3 = 2,051, all 512 pools plus the incomplete pool. No candidate is dropped. The same number marks where sparsity begins, SPARSE_FROM = (512 + 1) × 4 − 1 = 2,051. A row at position \(q\) sees \(\lfloor (q+1)/4 \rfloor\) complete pools and only starts selecting once there are more than 512 of them, that is, from \(q = 2{,}051\) on. Before that, it does dense attention over the latent of every position, which is exactly what the top-k would pick, and in prefill the dense pass runs only on those early rows. It's the dense route the vLLM recipe doesn't have (see the edge cases at the end of the chapter). Without the recipe's patches, TensorFold starts GLM with a default window of 2,051 tokens, where attention is still fully dense, and only a larger --context turns on the sparse path.

The price is maintaining those kernels. The engine only runs the families someone wrote them for, and every new TensorFold release means reapplying and adjusting the recipe's patches (53 in v1.3.2; 123 in v1.10+). vLLM offers the opposite path: a ready-made kernel, maintained by others, at the cost of 4 candidates out of 2,048.

Picking 512 pools out of hundreds of thousands is also the job of a kernel of its own. TensorFold uses a radix select: instead of sorting the scores, it finds the 512th best one byte by byte. Each FP32 score becomes an order-preserving 32-bit integer key (a larger integer means a larger float), and the kernel makes 4 passes of 8 bits.

  1. Histogram of the top byte. Count how many keys fall into each of the 256 possible values of the most significant byte.
  2. Walk down from the top. Adding the counts from bin 255 downward, the bin where the sum reaches 512 is the top byte of the 512th best score. The pools in the bins above it are certainly picked, and the ones in its bin are still contested.
  3. Repeat on the next byte. Now only among the contested keys, looking for however many are still missing. After 4 passes, all 32 bits of the threshold \(\tau\), the 512th best score, are known exactly.
  4. Write the picks. One final pass, in pool order, writes the pools whose key is above \(\tau\) and, among the ties at \(\tau\), the lowest-index ones until there are 512. The output comes out already in ascending order.

A pocket example, in decimal and illustrative: the 3rd largest of {47, 52, 58, 31, 55, 50}. The tens digit 5 has 4 numbers, and 4 ≥ 3, so the threshold starts with 5 and the numbers with a smaller tens digit are out. Among 52, 58, 55 and 50, the units from the top down are 8, 5 and 2: the 3rd largest is 52, and the picks are {52, 55, 58}. There was no sorting, only integer counts. That's why the selection is deterministic by construction, in both the set and the order. Compare with QSA, whose top-k returns the right set in a varying order: the Qwen recipe has to sort the indices in a separate step (VLLM_QSA_DET_TOPK), and that opt-in, together with the deterministic MoE finalize, costs ~4%. Here the ascending order comes for free, and the sparse attention always walks the tokens in the same order.

The tail becomes a per-stream ring

Each token's keys and gates are only needed until its pool of 4 closes. After that, the indexer reads only the pooled key. In vLLM, this observation became KpoolTail, a 4-slot ring per request, with the two addressing bugs from the box above. TensorFold uses the same idea in a BF16 ring per stream, of \(\min(\text{capacity}, \lceil (\text{rows}+3)/64 \rceil \cdot 64)\) rows: it holds the rows of the widest forward plus the 3 tokens before it, with token \(t\) at row \(t \bmod \text{ring}\). A kept prompt state carries the ring's tail along, the \(n \bmod 4\) tokens of the incomplete pool. With KV=fp8, each pooled key becomes an FP8 e4m3 row with a power-of-two scale: 144 B per pool (128 codes, 4 B of scale and 12 B of alignment), or 36 B per token and layer, versus vLLM's 132 B per pool. The format is in the quantized KV chapter.

DeepSeek-V4: compressing the KV itself (CSA and HCA)

GLM and Qwen compress only the indexer keys. DeepSeek-V4 takes the next step and compresses the KV of the main attention. Each layer has a compression ratio, given by the compress_ratios list in config.json, with three possible values:

▭

0 · window only

Layers 0–1 and the 3 DSpark stages (43–45): pure 128-token SWA. vLLM raises 0 to 1 (max(1, ·)).

▦

4 · CSA / C4A

21 layers (2, 4, …, 42): a 128-token window + the top-512 4:1 compressed entries, chosen by a lightning indexer. About 2,048 tokens of coverage.

▬

128 · HCA / C128A

20 layers (3, 5, …, 41): a 128-token window + all the 128:1 compressed entries. Dense attention, but over a sequence 128× shorter: 8,192 entries at 1M.

The technical names are Compressed Sparse Attention (CSA) and Heavily Compressed Attention (HCA), and vLLM calls them C4A and C128A. At the heart of both is a compressor: a learned, gated pooling, similar to GLM's kpool, but applied to the 512-dimensional vector that serves as key and value.

\[ \mathbf c_i = \sum_{j \in B_{i-1}} \mathbf s^{a}_{j} \odot \mathbf C^{a}_{j} \;+\; \sum_{j \in B_i} \mathbf s^{b}_{j} \odot \mathbf C^{b}_{j} \] \[ \mathbf z = \big[\mathbf Z^{a}_{B_{i-1}} + \mathbf B^{a};\ \mathbf Z^{b}_{B_i} + \mathbf B^{b}\big] \] \[ \big[\mathbf s^{a};\, \mathbf s^{b}\big] = \operatorname{softmax}_{\text{8 positions}}(\mathbf z) \]
  • \(B_i\): the 4 tokens of block \(i\) (C4)
  • \(\mathbf C = \mathbf H \mathbf W_{KV}\): what gets mixed; \(\mathbf W_{KV}\) projects each token to \(2 \times 512\), the halves \(\mathbf C^a\) and \(\mathbf C^b\)
  • \(\mathbf Z = \mathbf H \mathbf W_{Z}\): the gate scores, per token and per channel (also \(2 \times 512\))
  • \(\mathbf z\): the 8 scores of each channel, 4 from the previous block and 4 from the current one
  • softmax: one per channel, over the 8 positions (column by column)
  • \(\mathbf B\): ape, positional bias within the block
  • \(\odot\): element-wise product (each channel has its own softmax)

In C4 there's overlap: entry \(i\) mixes 8 positions. \(\mathbf W_{KV}\) and \(\mathbf W_Z\) project each token to \(2 \times 512\) numbers. Half \(a\) is the version of the token used when it sits in the previous block, and half \(b\) is the version used when it sits in the current block. Each of the compressed entry's 512 channels then takes a softmax over 8 candidates: the 4 tokens of the previous block (seen through half \(a\)) and the 4 of the current block (seen through half \(b\)). Every token takes part in two entries, once as “current” and once as “previous”. That way a fact that falls right on the boundary between two blocks doesn't get split in half. In C128 there's no overlap, and each channel takes a softmax over the block's 128 tokens. After the pooling come RMSNorm, RoPE on the last 64 dimensions at the position of the block's first token, and FP8 quantization of the 448 position-free dimensions. The result is a 584 B row, the same size as a window entry.

Incremental decode explains one of the DS recipe's patches. The compressor keeps partial states (kv_state, score_state) and only emits an entry when the block closes. In a C128 layer, that happens once every 128 steps of each request, but stock ran compress → RMSNorm → RoPE → quant → write on every step, ≈ 127 out of 128 of them for nothing. The backport #48957 (hotfix-dsv4-skip-empty-c128-48957.sh) works out on the CPU whether any request crosses a 128 boundary in the step and, after writing the partial state, returns early. Upstream measures ~2× on that kernel.

To know when that shortcut actually applies, you need to know vLLM's two CUDA graph modes (CUDA graphs chapter). In PIECEWISE, vLLM records into the graph only the stretches between attention calls. Attention, and with it this layer's compressor, runs eagerly, launched by the CPU on every step, so an if on CPU metadata is re-evaluated every time. In FULL, the whole step is a single graph: what runs was decided at capture time, and on replay the Python if simply isn't executed. That's why the #48957 gate requires cudagraph_runtime_mode != FULL.

Our interpretation: the DS recipe boots in FULL_AND_PIECEWISE, vLLM's default when piecewise compilation is on. In this mode uniform decode batches (every request with the same number of tokens in the step, 1 + 6 with the drafter) run in a FULL graph, and mixed batches with prefill run piecewise. The boot shows Capturing CUDA graphs (FULL) 6/6 and the CHANGELOG records FULL_AND_PIECEWISE on the live server (the hotfix's own header assumes piecewise as the default, which doesn't match those records). So the decode and verification steps captured in FULL, which are the regime of a long generation, still run the whole compressor. The shortcut only fires on piecewise or eager steps: batches with prefill and batches larger than the largest captured size. The “~2×” is an upstream kernel measurement, not a gain the recipe reaps in steady-state decode.

Who reads what, in each layer

In the C4A layers, a lightning indexer with index_n_heads=64 heads of index_head_dim=128 (with its own ratio-4 compressor, Hadamard and FP4 simulation) picks the best index_topk=512 among the \(\lfloor (t+1)/4 \rfloor\) entries already closed. That choice lives in a topk_indices_buffer of shape [max_num_batched_tokens, 512] (int32), shared by all 21 C4A layers. This is the V3.2 lineage, which picked 2,048 raw tokens and now picks 512 4:1 entries, with the same coverage and FP4 scoring. In the C128A layers there's no indexer: at 1M there are only 8,192 entries, and reading all of them is cheap. The maximum size of that list is ceil(ceil(max_model_len/128)/128) × 128 (_C128A_TOPK_ALIGNMENT = 128), exactly 8,192 at 1M.

At serving time, window and compressed branch become a single list. combine_topk_swa_indices concatenates the compressed top-k (min((pos+1)//ratio, topk) valid indices) and the window (min(pos+1, 128)), with the width rounded up to a multiple of 128. The SM120 kernel (FlashInfer BatchSparseMLAPagedAttentionWrapper) receives two caches, the window one as kv_cache and the compressed one as extra_kv_cache, and runs a single online softmax (with the attention sink) over both sources. Window and summary compete for the same probability mass, and the model learns to balance the two. The C4A indices come out of the indexer buffer and are converted layer by layer into physical slots (compute_global_topk_indices_and_lens). The C128A ones are precomputed in the forward metadata, because they don't depend on the layer.

Neat detail: vision lines up with compression

In the Vision-Exp checkpoint, an image's token block always starts on a 4-token boundary (COMPRESS_PAD_TO = 4, with 0–3 pad tokens in compress_pad_tokens). Interpretation: that way the C4 entries don't mix text with image, and the image's 2×2 blocks fall entirely inside one compressed entry. An attention architecture forces even the image preprocessor to respect it.

Deep dive: “2.22×” isn't what it seems

DeepSeek's boot says Available KV cache memory: 17.04 GiB, GPU KV cache size: 2,331,430 tokens and Maximum concurrency for 1,048,576 tokens per request: 2.22x. Divide one by the other and you get ≈ 7.8 KB/token, twice the physical cost of 3.85 KB. The reason is that the hybrid allocator adds up the four KV groups (1 MLA + 3 window), all with the same 37,376 B page. It's allocator bookkeeping, not the real cost of each row, and the physical concurrency at 1M is higher than 2.22×. The page mechanics are covered in PagedAttention.

The other half of the story is still missing: time. Sparse attention left decode almost flat, but prefill still pays for the indexer. In DeepSeek, a 1,024-token chunk in a 900K context costs ≈ 80 TFLOP of indexer work alone (1,024 queries × 225K keys × 64 heads × 128 dimensions × 2 × 21 layers), and that work is duplicated on both ranks. Prefill drops from ≈ 2,563 tok/s at 2K to ≈ 875 tok/s at 900K, and the acceptance test with 899,994 tokens took 1,028.85 s of TTFT. In decode, at 1M, attention reads ≈ 7.9 MB (C4A) + 97 MB (C128A) + 0.15 MB (windows), but the indexer sweeps the 262,144 compressed keys × 132 B × 21 layers ≈ 0.73 GB per token (inventory figures). The version of the GLM recipe shows the same curve shape, on another model and another engine (compare the shape, not the values): 1,952 tok/s of prefill at 8.2 thousand tokens, 1,642 at 262 thousand and 1,015 on a 981,841-token prompt, with the needle found (sparkDash, default configuration, GPU clocks capped at 2,200 MHz). Interpretation: near 1M, each prompt row scores ~245 thousand pools. The scout has become the biggest reader in the house, and that's why it gets so much engineering.

In the recipe: the invisible cost of a high MAX_MODEL_LEN

Even with short prompts, QSA sizes structures by the maximum context: the scorer allocates an FP32 buffer [rows, page_table_width × page_size] per layer, and at 1M with ratio 4 that comes to 250K columns, 4 MB per verification step per layer. persistent_topk also runs over those 250K columns, and the block tables have 625 entries. That's why the recipe serves MAX_MODEL_LEN=262144 (the native context) and recommends that value for agentic use, with an expected gain of 2–5%. Prefill stays close to linear: ≈ 2.96k tok/s from 16K to 64K and 2,727 tok/s at 128K (−8%).

The indexer in production: replicas, split top-k and workspaces

Three engineering facts weigh on the indexer. First, it's replicated: in DeepSeek, the indexer's wq_b and weights_proj are ReplicatedLinear and the K cache has a single head, so each rank scores the 64 heads against all the keys. Second, it needs a large scratch memory in prefill. Third, top-k is a kernel with its own shared memory limits. Let's start with the first.

Sequence parallelism in the indexer

If every rank redoes the same scoring, why not split the keys? The hotfix hotfix-dsv4-sp-indexer-prefill.py (opt-in DSPARK_ENABLE_SP_INDEXER=1) applies sequence parallelism to the prefill indexer. Each rank takes a contiguous, page-aligned slice of each request's compressed keys and scores only that slice (fp8_fp4_mqa_logits). Then it runs the local top-k, exchanges (score, global id) pairs with an all-gather and merges everything with the stable merge kernel borrowed from DCP (decode context parallel, the parallelism that splits the KV tokens across ranks; stable_topk_from_gathered_candidates_cutedsl), writing straight into the topk_indices_buffer.

The merge is exact, by a one-line argument: if a key is in the global top-k, then at most \(k-1\) keys in the whole world are better than it. In particular, at most \(k-1\) keys in its own slice are better, and so it's in the local top-k of the rank that owns it. The union of the local top-ks contains the global top-k, and the merge recovers it. The decision to take the SP path is made with CPU metadata that's identical on all ranks, so the collective never gets out of sync. The traffic is small: \(\mathrm{TP} \times 512 \times 8\) B per query, 8 KiB at TP2 (our math).

ParametersDSPARK_ENABLE_SP_INDEXER=0|1 · DSPARK_SP_INDEXER_MIN_KEYS=8192

The SP path is only used on chunks with at least 8,192 compressed keys, that is, 32K tokens of context at ratio 4. Below that, the chunk uses the stock replicated path, and MIN_KEYS=0 turns SP off even with the patch applied. Measured in the 2026-09-02 A/B: TTFT at 128K of 76.0 s (cold) and 74.4 s (warm) versus 79.0 s and 81.2 s in the two baseline boots, ≈ −4 to −8% (the A/B table rounds this to −4 to −6%), with 8K and 32K unchanged, RULER-lite 8/8 and ~1 GB less MemAvailable. The A/B verdict was to turn it on, but the compose default is still 0 (opt-in). The gain fell far short of the projected 1.5–1.8×. Interpretation: only the scoring was split, while MoE, the C128A layers, the all-reduces and the gather itself are all still in the path.

The same problem inside one GPU

In decode there are few query rows and, at long context, each one has hundreds of thousands of pools to score and select. One program per row leaves the GPU nearly empty. TensorFold splits the work on two fronts. In scoring, each \((\text{row}, g)\) program loads the indexer query (32 heads × 128) and the head weights into registers once, and walks the pool blocks \(g, g+128, g+256, \dots\) in a single launch for all rows. In selection, rows with at least 8,192 pools (32K tokens) in windows of fewer than 64 rows are cut into chunks of 2,048 or 4,096 scores, one program per \((\text{row}, \text{chunk})\), in 5 launches: the 4 radix select passes and the write. In each pass, the chunks add their histogram to the row's total with integer atomics, and the write puts each chunk's pools at the offset given by the earlier chunks' counts. Integer sums are exact in any order and float sums aren't, so the result is bit for bit that of the one-program-per-row selection. DeepSeek's SP indexer splits the keys across ranks and merges candidates, and TensorFold splits one row across programs and merges histograms. They are two different exactness arguments for the same idea.

Workspaces: the memory that disappears from the KV

Every buffer vLLM reserves during the profile run comes out of the KV budget, so an oversized workspace is context thrown away. The recipes tune three of them.

ParameterGLM53_INDEXER_WORKSPACE=rightsize

Stock sizes the indexer's prefill gather workspace as max_model_len × 40 entries of 132 B: 5,036.40 MiB locked up at 1M. But the splitter is fed compressed lengths: the workspace was sized in tokens and indexed in pools. patch_indexer_workspace.py (overlay #23) computes min(max_num_seqs, MNBT) × cdiv(max_model_len + k, compress_ratio), with MNBT = max_num_batched_tokens and \(k\) = speculative tokens, capped at the stock size: 125.9 MiB with 4 sequences (503.5 MiB with 16), which hands +4.79 / +4.43 GiB back to the KV (≈ +26–28%). The builder also checks that index_kpool == compress_ratio in both directions and refuses to run if they differ, because the math depends on it. It's been the default on TP2 since 2026-09-07; the TP3/TP4 scripts default to stock, but inherit rightsize from the .env.

VariableVLLM_SPARSE_INDEXER_MAX_LOGITS_MB=256 (stock 512)

In prefill, the indexer materializes an FP32 logits tensor [M, N] (queries × compressed keys), and this variable caps its size. Our math: at 1M, \(N = 262{,}144\), and each row takes up 1 MiB. With 256 MB, 256 queries fit per launch, and a 1,024-token chunk needs 4 launches (2 with 512 MB). The recipe tested 512: a tie (−1.5% at 32K and −1.8% at 128K in TTFT, within the ±2% noise). It stayed at 256, which saves workspace at no measured cost.

The third is DeepSeek's own topk_indices_buffer, \(\mathrm{MNBT} \times 512 \times 4\) B: 16 MiB with MAX_NUM_BATCHED_TOKENS=8192 (our math). It's small, but it has to be persistent, because twenty-one layers read it and the SP indexer writes to it.

Constant and variableSELECT_ROWS = 512 · TF_GLM_SPARSE_ONEPASS=1

Scores in blocks of rows. TensorFold doesn't do a profile run: it computes memory with formulas before loading the weights, and every byte of scratch counted too many becomes one fewer KV token. In prefill, token selection used to allocate the FP32 scores of every row of the prompt chunk (2,048) against every pool. The glm-prompt-select-rows patch processes 512 rows at a time, with the same columns, and therefore the same bits per row. Order of magnitude (the research's estimate, assuming a buffer the size of the visible pools): at 1M there are 262,144 pools, and the buffer drops from 2,048 × 262,144 × 4 B = 2 GiB to 512 MiB. It's the same lever as DeepSeek's VLLM_SPARSE_INDEXER_MAX_LOGITS_MB, which caps the queries per launch.

Sparse attention without partials. With TF_GLM_SPARSE_ONEPASS=1, each (row, group of 32 heads, latent slice) program walks the up to 2,051 picked tokens in a single online softmax and writes the result in BF16, with no FP32 partials and no merge. To hide the gathers' latency, the token indices are read two tiles ahead and the latent rows one tile ahead. The glm-lean-prompt-scratch patch removed from the memory estimate the partials this kernel doesn't allocate. The recipe publishes no isolated number, only “faster prefill, less memory at 1M”.

Trade-off. Splitting the keys into chunks and merging the partials (split-K) gives parallelism when there are few rows, and it's what TensorFold's own decode windows do: a row's up to 2,051 tokens become 5 chunks of 512, merged afterwards. In prefill, hundreds of rows already fill the GPU, so one pass per row is enough and leaves no scratch behind.

Size by the work, not by the maximum: two measured cases

The indexer's prefill transient. Mia's recipe that serves DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash with SGLang on three and four Sparks measured the memory peak the indexer creates on each prompt chunk. Each low-ratio source layer scores the chunk's \(T\) rows against the whole prefix \(L\) in a single FP32 logits tensor, and around it live, at the same time, a copy for the candidate blocks and the boolean candidate masks. The measured peak is about 14 B × T × L per rank (2.97 GB with \(T\) = 2,048 and \(L\) = 100k; 2.6 GB with 1,024 and 208k; docs/chunked-prefill-memory.md). By the same arithmetic, at 1M context that's ~15 GB with 1,024-token chunks, which fit in the ~24 GiB per-rank headroom of the four-Spark profile, and ~60 GB with 4,096, which no rank has. The way out is the chunked indexer, a backport of sglang#39187 (DSV41_INDEXER_CHUNKED=1): it scores each request in row blocks bounded by a fixed logits budget (2 GiB of FP32 by default), writes the masks straight into a preallocated tensor and publishes only their tail rows. With it, 4,096-token chunks are safe at any context: the head's lowest MemAvailable stayed at 7.0–7.7 GiB during a 262k cold prefill, and a 1M needle test passed at TP=4. It's the principle of VLLM_SPARSE_INDEXER_MAX_LOGITS_MB above: the scratch size becomes a parameter, not a consequence of the context.

A pool-sized scratch, read in full. In the GLM·TensorFold recipe after v1.3.2 (v1.9, patch 0085-glm-select-split-loop; see what changed since v1.3.2), the decode's chunked selection had a last pass in which each program summed the histograms of the earlier chunks by loading the row's whole chunk table, masked. But that table came from the selection scratch, sized for the whole KV pool (5,791,744 rows with PARALLEL=4 on three Sparks, or 512 chunks per row), not for the row's context. At 226k, each launch took 5.3 ms and accounted for 92% of the growth of one request's decode round since 35k. The fix sums only the earlier chunks, 16 at a time (SPLIT_BLK). The whole selection call, at 16 rows, fell from 5.79 to 0.28 ms at 226k and from 27.87 to 1.08 ms at 1.04M, and one request's decode on three Sparks at 35k / 226k / 590k went from 182.6 / 111.2 / 66.2 to 203.0 / 191.0 / 184.4 tok/s, with byte-identical replies (they are integer sums, just in another order).

Beyond the Spark: both cases are the same sizing mistake, and they show up in any engine with sparse attention, on an H200 or a GB300. The SGLang backport, in fact, came from an upstream measurement on 4× GB300: the transient of a 300k cold prompt fell from 49.7 to 10.7 GB. A temporary buffer sized by the maximum (maximum context, whole pool) costs memory when it's allocated and bandwidth when it's read; size it and walk it by the real work.

Pitfall: top-k has hardware limits too

The persistent_topk kernel keeps state in shared memory. On the GB10, GLM's decode variant overflows SMEM on long sequences, and the overlay patch_glm_video_placeholders.py disables it (if False and …) in favor of top_k_per_row_decode. For the same reason, the GLM recipe never uses MAX_NUM_BATCHED_TOKENS=8192: an 8,192-token chunk with a long history overflowed persistent_topk's SMEM (crash at 300K). In Qwen, persistent_topk is precisely one of the costs that scale with MAX_MODEL_LEN. The SMEM and occupancy details are in the kernels chapter.

Other kernel knobs tied to sparse attention

FLASHINFER_MLA_SPARSE_SM120. FlashInfer's sparse MLA backend for SM12x. It reads the fp8_ds_mla latent (a 656 B record) only on the rows the top-k picked, in the GLM_NSA geometry, and takes the top-k as a template parameter: that's why 2,048 is an untouchable number in the GLM recipe. DeepSeek uses the model's own SM120 implementation, with the two caches (window and compressed).

_DECODE_MAX_TOKENS = 64 and opt-in #141. That kernel accepts up to 64 query rows per call; above that, FlashInfer uses the prefill orchestrator. With a drafter, a verification step has \(1 + k\) rows per request: with \(k = 6\) and 6 requests that's 42 rows, and with more requests it goes past 64. It was on the prefill path that stochastic hangs showed up at TP2, and the opt-in DSPARK_ENABLE_ISSUE141_SPARSE_MLA_CHUNK slices those calls into pieces of up to 64 rows. It's a workaround, not a fix for the root cause.

#50298, reusing the combined-index workspace. On every prefill chunk, combine_topk_swa_indices allocated a tokens × combined_topk tensor and filled it with −1, with combined_topk = round_up(top_k + window, 128) = 640. The backport reuses buffers from the shared workspace and skips the fill, because the kernel respects each row's valid length and never reads the padding. Upstream measures 1.88× on that kernel.

QSA_PROFILE. QSA's Triton kernels also do split-K in decode, with the number of partitions tuned for the GB300's 160 SMs. On the GB10, with 48 SMs, the stock profile launches about 5× more CTAs than fit in one wave and then merges 64 partials. It hasn't been measured yet, and it doesn't combine with FP8 KV or with deterministic top-k, because all three overwrite the same kernel file (ops/qsa.py).

Reusing the selection across drafter steps. An MTP drafter applies the same layer several times per step, for neighboring positions, and the top-k selection changes little from one to the next. In Qwen, MTP_INDEX_SHARE turns on index_share_for_mtp_iteration: the QSA top-k computed in the first draft step is reused in steps 2 and 3, with indices one position “stale”. In code generation the measured gain was +6.1 / +4.6 / +21.3 / +26.6% with S = 1 / 2 / 4 / 8 concurrent sequences; in prose, within the noise. In GLM, index_share_for_mtp_iteration is true in the config, but it only kicks in with the alternative SPEC_METHOD=mtp mode. In the recipes: edge cases and the parameter map

A sparse attention system has more branches than a dense one: shortcuts for short context, steps in which the compressor has no work, dense routes that may or may not exist. Each branch is a chance to speed things up and also to get them wrong, especially once CUDA graphs come in, since they freeze the path chosen during capture (CUDA graphs chapter). The figure walks through the traffic lights the recipes installed.

  1. Short-context shortcut (#49486 + guard #52492). If max_seq_len // compress_ratio ≤ topk_tokens (up to 512 × 4 = 2,048 tokens), top-k would pick every candidate anyway. hotfix-dsv4-skip-topk-49486.sh skips wq_b → RoPE → quant → logits → top-k and fills in the indices with the Triton kernel _fill_short_context_topk_indices ([0..n−1, −1, −1, …], with n = (pos+1)//ratio, the number of compressed entries already closed). The compressor keeps running, because the K cache still has to be written. Gain: 3.4% in end-to-end TTFT (upstream). Guard #52492 never takes the shortcut during torch.cuda.is_current_stream_capturing(). If it did, the captured graph would “bake in” the shortcut and replay it on long prefixes, selecting 0..511 without scoring: an intermittent corruption under prefix cache + graphs.
  2. C128 compressor with no work (#48957). Seen above: it skips the compressor when no request crosses a 128 boundary in the step (~2× on the kernel, upstream measurement), and never in a FULL graph, where the whole step was decided at capture time. Since the recipe runs FULL_AND_PIECEWISE, uniform decode lands in FULL and the shortcut only applies on piecewise or eager steps (our interpretation). There's also a documentation inconsistency: docs/vllm-027-new-patches.md says “not yet applied”, but the compose applies it, and the compose is the source of truth.
  3. Dense prefill: the route that doesn't exist. On short prefills, upstream sends MLA to a dense MHA and skips the indexer (#48407). The idea is legitimate for two reasons. When the context fits in the budget (\(n \le k \times \text{ratio}\), 2,048 tokens here), top-k would pick everything and sparse attention is identical to dense. And MLA has a non-absorbed MHA form, which reconstructs K and V and comes out cheaper in prefill, when there are thousands of queries at once (MLA chapter). The gate skips the indexer counting on that dense route to consume the prefill. This fork has no dense route for sparse-MLA prefills, so the sparse kernel would still be called, just without the indices the indexer stopped writing. That's why hotfix-dsv4-dense-prefill-indexer-48407.sh is deliberately installed dormant (dense_mha_metadata_layer_name=""): the gate never fires, because activating it would discard a valid selection and produce wrong attention. It's a staged port from upstream. GLM reaches the same conclusion by another path: the SM120 backend inherited forward_mha, which raises NotImplementedError, and the build patch declares supports_dense_mha_prefill = False, which sends prefill through the same MQA top-k kernel. Prefill keeps the model's own sparse semantics.
  4. Reused metadata (C128A prefill cache). In the C128A layers, converting local indices to global slots depends only on the forward metadata, not on the layer. The opt-in DSPARK_ENABLE_C128A_PREFILL_CACHE=1 (hotfix-vllm-c128a-prefill-cache.py) makes the first C128A layer store (indices, lens) in the metadata (c128a_prefill_global_topk), and the others reuse it. Fewer conversions, but the changelog warns: no end-to-end gain is claimed.
A shortcut that was removed

Backport #50004 packed the C128A top-k with an adaptive width. Under FULL CUDA graphs, the builder wrote the rows at the live batch's stride while the graph kept the capture stride, and rows ≥ 1 read stale slots: intermittent token corruption with MTP + prefix cache + graphs. Upstream reverted it (#51318) and the recipe removed the patch on 2026-08-22. Moral of the story: in sparse attention, shapes that vary from step to step and captured graphs are natural enemies.

Another engine: the graph freezes the bucket, not the work

The TensorFold version of the GLM recipe resolves the same conflict from the other side. It captures one CUDA graph per verification window width and, past the 2,051 dense limit, one per pool bucket: the number of visible pools rounded up to a power of 2, at least 1,024, so that the allocator reuses a few sizes (exact sizes fragmented memory at 128K). Everything that changes each step (positions, extents, which rows are sparse, how many pools each one sees) lives in device tables that the kernel reads. With visible pools (the glm-visible-pools patch), a decode row at position \(P + r\) scores and scans only \(\min(N_{PS}, \max(\lfloor (P+r+1)/4 \rfloor, 512))\) columns of the \(N_{PS}\)-column bucket. The selection doesn't change: the columns cut off are \(-\infty\) and have the highest indices, so they rank below every kept column (ties go to the lower pool), and the 512 best of the first columns are the 512 best of all of them. The recipe records “the same tokens” and publishes no speed gain. Compare with the hidden cost of MAX_MODEL_LEN in QSA, further up: there the scorer sweeps the maximum width, here the indexer's cost tracks the real context. Upstream already bounded the one-program-per-row selection this way, and the patch extends the bound to decode scoring and to the split selection.

You now have the vocabulary to read any config.json with sparse attention. Ask, layer by layer: is there a window, and how big is it? Is there an indexer, with how many heads, and does it score tokens or blocks? What's the budget, and does the kernel accept that number? Is the KV stored in full or compressed? Then do the bytes-per-token math and look for the branches (short context, incomplete blocks, dense prefill) that are going to run into CUDA graphs. One last piece of the hybrids is still missing. In the remaining 3 out of every 4 layers of GLM and Qwen there's no KV at all, only a fixed-size state that gets updated with every token. That's linear attention, the subject of the next chapter.
