In September 2026, the Qwen3.8 recipe uncovered an embarrassing bug: every “1 million token” measurement taken up to that point had run without the YaRN that was supposed to stretch the context. A chunk of JSON at the wrong level of --hf-overrides turned the context extension into a silent no-op. Around the same time, the DeepSeek recipe ported a vLLM fix because YaRN was being applied to layers where it should not exist. And GLM-5.3, the third model, simply has no RoPE in its text attention layers. To understand these three cases, and to build your own recipe without falling into them, you need to know how a Transformer figures out where each token is. This chapter goes from why position matters to the math of RoPE, partial RoPE, NoPE, mRoPE and YaRN, and ends with every config field and every server flag.

A bag of tokens doesn't know the order

In the attention chapter, each token compares its query with the keys of the others and mixes the values. There is a detail that looks small: none of these computations uses the token's index. The projections \(W_Q, W_K, W_V\) are the same matrices at every position, the dot product only looks at the two vectors, and the weighted sum doesn't care about the order of its terms.

\[ o_i = \sum_{j} \operatorname{softmax}_j\!\left(\frac{q_i \cdot k_j}{\sqrt{d}}\right) v_j \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \operatorname{Attn}(P X) = P\,\operatorname{Attn}(X) \]
  • \(q_i, k_j, v_j\): query, key and value of tokens \(i\) and \(j\) (rows of \(XW_Q\), \(XW_K\), \(XW_V\))
  • \(d\): head dimension; \(o_i\): output vector of token \(i\)
  • \(X\): the input matrix, one row per token
  • \(P\): a permutation matrix (it shuffles the rows)

The equality on the right has a name: permutation equivariance. If you shuffle the input, the output comes out shuffled the same way, and nothing else changes. “the cat chases the mouse” and “the mouse chases the cat” give, for each word, exactly the same vector, just in a different place. To pure attention, a sentence is a bag of tokens. The figure runs this computation for real on a toy model.

Note that the causal mask alone already breaks the symmetry. With it, shuffling changes the set of tokens each one can see, and the output changes. Keep this in mind, because it is the key to understanding how GLM-5.3 works without positional encoding (section 5). But the mask only says “how many came before”, not “how far away each one is”. For that, the model needs an explicit positional encoding.

Absolute or relative?

The first solution was absolute position (APE): add a vector \(p_m\) to the embedding of token \(m\). The original Transformer used fixed sinusoids, and GPT-2 and BERT used a learned table with one row per position. This has two flaws. The table has a fixed size, so token number \(N+1\) has no row at all. And the model has to learn separately that “three tokens back” is the same relation at position 10 and at position 10,000.

The second family is relative position: add to the score \(q_i\cdot k_j\) a bias that depends only on \(i-j\) (T5's “buckets”, ALiBi's linear penalty). It works, but it requires an extra term inside the attention kernel.

RoPE (Rotary Position Embedding, Su et al., 2021) brings together the best of both worlds. Each vector is rotated according to its absolute position, but the dot product between two rotated vectors depends only on the relative distance. It is absolute on write and relative on read. Almost every current LLM uses some variant of it, and the three models in this course show three different choices: RoPE on part of the head (Qwen), RoPE on part of the head with different frequencies per layer type (DeepSeek), and no RoPE at all in the text (GLM).

Analogy: the Ferris wheel

Two kids on a Ferris wheel go around together. How many turns the wheel has already made doesn't change the angle between their two seats. RoPE puts each token in a seat that has already turned \(m\) steps, and the dot product only “sees” the angle between the seats, that is, the distance between the tokens.

RoPE: rotate to remember

Take the query (or key) vector of a head and group its dimensions into pairs. Each pair is a point in a plane or, if you prefer, a complex number. RoPE rotates pair \(i\) of the token at position \(m\) by an angle \(m\,\theta_i\). In matrix form, this is a block-diagonal matrix of 2×2 rotations, one per pair:

\[ R_m = \begin{pmatrix} \cos m\theta_0 & -\sin m\theta_0 & & & \\ \sin m\theta_0 & \cos m\theta_0 & & & \\ & & \ddots & & \\ & & & \cos m\theta_{\frac{d_r}{2}-1} & -\sin m\theta_{\frac{d_r}{2}-1} \\ & & & \sin m\theta_{\frac{d_r}{2}-1} & \cos m\theta_{\frac{d_r}{2}-1} \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \tilde q_m = R_m\, q_m,\quad \tilde k_n = R_n\, k_n \]
  • \(m, n\): positions of the query and of the key in the sequence (0, 1, 2, …)
  • \(\theta_i\): frequency of pair \(i\), in radians per token (next section)
  • \(d_r\): how many dimensions are rotated (\(d_r/2\) pairs)
  • \(\tilde q, \tilde k\): the already-rotated vectors that go into the dot product

The magic is in one line of algebra. Rotations are orthogonal matrices (\(R_m^\top = R_{-m}\), chapter 3) and two rotations in the same plane add up:

\[ \tilde q_m \cdot \tilde k_n = (R_m q)^\top (R_n k) = q^\top R_m^\top R_n\, k = q^\top R_{\,n-m}\, k \]
  • \(R_m^\top R_n = R_{n-m}\): rotating by \(-m\) and then by \(+n\) is the same as rotating by \(n-m\)
  • result: the score depends on \(q\), on \(k\) and only on the difference \(n-m\)

The figure shows a single pair of dimensions. The query is at position \(m\) and the key at position \(n\). Move the controls and watch the dot product.

Three properties follow from this, and recipes exploit them all the time:

  1. It is relative. The same pair of words 5 tokens apart gets the same score at the beginning or at the end of a 1-million-token document.
  2. It preserves the norm. A rotation neither stretches nor shrinks the vector, so RoPE doesn't touch the scale of the logits or the \(1/\sqrt{d}\) of attention.
  3. It only touches Q and K. The value is not rotated in standard attention, because position only needs to influence who gets attention, not the content being mixed. DeepSeek-V4 is the interesting exception: there, K and V are the same vector, so the output has to be “un-rotated” at the end (the inverse RoPE, covered in the MLA chapter).

How the engine actually computes it

Nobody builds the matrix \(R_m\): it is almost all zeros, and applying it costs two multiplications per dimension. The engine precomputes a cosine and sine table with one row per position and one column per pair. In the forward pass, each token gets the row for its position and the kernel computes \(\tilde q = q\odot\cos + \operatorname{rot}(q)\odot\sin\), where \(\operatorname{rot}\) swaps the elements of each pair and flips one sign. In vLLM this object comes from get_rope(head_size, max_position, rope_parameters, is_neox_style, …), and the model receives, along with the tokens, a positions tensor. In decode, the position of the new token is the current length of the sequence.

There is one decisive detail for inference. RoPE is applied before the key is written to the KV cache. Each key is rotated only once, at its own position, and is stored already rotated. Future queries arrive rotated by their own positions, and the dot product does the rest. Nothing in the cache needs to be recomputed as the sequence grows, and that is why RoPE gets along so well with the KV cache (chapter 10).

In the last step, notice that \(\lVert\tilde q\rVert = \lVert q\rVert\) at any position. That is property 2 showing up in the numbers. Notice too that the cos/sin table grows with the number of positions: with 64 rotated dimensions and 262,144 positions, a BF16 table would take \(262{,}144 \times 64 \times 2\) B = 32 MiB per RoPE configuration (illustrative math). That's not a lot, but it is one of the reasons max_position_embeddings matters to the engine.

A clock with many hands: frequencies and rope_theta

If every pair spun at the same speed, RoPE could not tell apart distances that differ by a whole turn. That's why each pair has its own frequency, in a geometric progression controlled by a single base \(b\), the rope_theta:

\[ \theta_i = b^{-2i/d_r}, \quad i = 0, 1, \ldots, \tfrac{d_r}{2}-1 \qquad\qquad \lambda_i = \frac{2\pi}{\theta_i} = 2\pi\, b^{2i/d_r} \]
  • \(b\): the base (rope_theta in the config)
  • \(\theta_i\): radians per token of pair \(i\); \(\theta_0 = 1\) always
  • \(\lambda_i\): wavelength, that is, how many tokens the pair takes to make one full turn
  • \(d_r\): rotated dimensions (64 in Qwen and in DeepSeek)

Picture a clock with 32 hands. Pair 0 is the second hand: it makes a turn every ~6.3 tokens and tells neighbors apart precisely, but at a distance of 100 tokens it has already “lost count” of how many turns it made. The last pairs are the hour hand: they barely move between neighbors, but they can separate positions tens of thousands of tokens apart. The combination of all the hands is a kind of “address” for the distance, like the digits of a number written in several bases at once.

Map of the layer types mentioned from here on

The three models mix several layer types, and each type has its own chapter later on. For this chapter it is enough to know what each one is and why it has (or doesn't have) its own RoPE.

  • SWA: sliding window attention, in which each query only sees the last \(W\) tokens (128 in DeepSeek, 2,048 in GLM's drafter). Distances never exceed \(W-1\) (ch. 13).
  • C4A / C128A: DeepSeek-V4's compressed attentions. A compressor summarizes each block of 4 (or 128) tokens into a single KV entry, and the query attends to these entries, which cover the whole context, plus a window of 128 raw tokens (ch. 13).
  • indexer: a cheap “scorer”, with a few small heads, that rates blocks of keys and picks the top-k positions the expensive attention will read. It computes its own q and k, so it has its own RoPE (or none, as in GLM) (ch. 13).
  • DSA · QSA: sparse attention guided by an indexer. DSA is DeepSeek's version, which GLM-5.3 uses on top of MLA (in the config, the deepseek_sparse_attention layers). QSA is Qwen3.8's version, in 12 of its 48 layers: the indexer scores micro-blocks of 4 tokens and the attention reads 2,048 positions (ch. 13).
  • MLA: K and V for all heads come out of one compressed latent per token, and only the latent goes into the cache (ch. 12).
  • KDA · GDN: linear, or recurrent, attentions. Instead of per-token KV, they keep a fixed-size state that is updated token by token. GLM has 34 KDA layers and Qwen has 36 GDN (ch. 14).
  • MTP (Multi-Token Prediction): an extra layer, trained to predict tokens beyond the next one, which at inference time becomes a built-in drafter. Qwen's has its own QSA attention, with the same RoPE as the trunk (ch. 16).
  • drafter: a small model that proposes several tokens at once for the large model to check in a single step. This trick is speculative decoding (chs. 35–37). DSpark is DeepSeek's built-in drafter (3 window-only stages, in layers 43–45). DFlash2 is GLM's external drafter, with 1.17 B parameters. Each one has its own attention and therefore its own RoPE.

The base decides how far the slow hands go. With \(d_r = 64\), the slowest pair has \(\lambda_{31} = 2\pi\, b^{62/64}\) (in GLM's drafter, with \(d_r = 128\), it is \(2\pi\, b^{126/128}\)):

In the figure, each dial is a pair. The light sector of each one marks the angles that pair saw during training, that is, from 0 to \(L\,\theta_i\), where \(L\) is the training context. Advance the position and change the base.

The crucial point, which will explain all of YaRN in section 7, is this: the fast pairs already made thousands of turns during training, so any angle of theirs is familiar. The danger lies in the slow pairs with \(\lambda_i > L\). In Qwen (\(b = 10^7\), \(L = 262{,}144\)), these are pairs 22 through 31. For them, a position beyond training produces an angle the model has never seen, and the attention scores fall outside the distribution it learned.

Why the base goes up in long-context models

Pair 0 has \(\theta_0 = 1\) for any base, so resolution between neighbors is guaranteed. What the base does is “stretch” the fan toward the slow side: with a larger \(b\), more pairs have long wavelengths and the similarity between distant positions decays more slowly. The cost shows up in the middle pairs, which get slower and are worse at telling intermediate distances apart. That's why the base is chosen at training time and can't be swapped on the server: the weights \(W_Q\) and \(W_K\) learned to use exactly those frequencies.

Deep dive: a different θ per layer type in DeepSeek

DeepSeek-V4-Flash has two “clocks” in the same model. The sliding window layers (SWA, only the last 128 tokens) use rope_theta = 10000. The compressor layers (C4A and C128A, chapter 13), which attend to entries that summarize blocks of 4 or 128 tokens spread across the whole context, use compress_rope_theta = 160000. Each compressed entry gets the RoPE of the position of the first token of the block. Note the order of operations: the compressor first pools the block's tokens (and applies RMSNorm) and only then applies RoPE, once, at the block's starting position. Qwen's QSA indexer does the same: average of 4 keys, RMSNorm and only then RoPE at the starting position. The reason is geometric. Summing vectors that have already been rotated by different angles would mix their phases and blur the position, whereas rotating the average only once gives each block a clean position (chapter 13). Anyone porting a new model with compression has to respect this order. A reasonable reading (our interpretation, not documented by the vendor) is that distances measured in blocks need slower hands. vLLM picks the base layer by layer with compress_rope_theta if compress_ratio > 1 else rope_theta. This same fork by layer type is what section 8 fixes for YaRN.

Partial RoPE and the pair convention

Nothing forces you to rotate the whole head. With partial RoPE, only a fraction of the dimensions of Q and K get rotated, and the rest passes through untouched:

\[ d_r = \rho \cdot d_{\text{head}}, \qquad q = [\,q_{\text{rot}} \mid q_{\text{pass}}\,], \qquad \tilde q_m \cdot \tilde k_n = \underbrace{q_{\text{rot}}^\top R_{n-m}\, k_{\text{rot}}}_{\text{depends on distance}} \;+\; \underbrace{q_{\text{pass}} \cdot k_{\text{pass}}}_{\text{content only}} \]
  • \(\rho\): partial_rotary_factor (rotated fraction)
  • \(d_{\text{head}}\): head dimension
  • \(q_{\text{rot}}\): the \(d_r\) rotated dimensions; \(q_{\text{pass}}\): the others (NoPE)
  • the score becomes the sum of a positional channel and a pure-content channel
  • Qwen3.8: partial_rotary_factor = 0.25 with head_dim = 256, that is, 64 rotated dimensions (32 pairs) and 192 of pure content. The model card literally says “Rotary Position Embedding Dimension: 64”. The QSA indexer (the scorer that picks the blocks) also rotates 64 of its 128 dimensions.
  • DeepSeek-V4: qk_rope_head_dim = 64 in a 512-dim head, that is, 448 “nope” dimensions and the last 64 rotated. The same holds for the compressed entries and for the indexer.
  • GLM-5.3: qk_rope_head_dim = 0 in the MLA layers. No dimension rotates.

Why rotate only a part? There are three reasons, and the third only becomes clear later on. First, the pure-content channel does semantic search equally well 10 tokens away or 100,000 tokens away, without the “noise” of the rotations (a common interpretation in the literature). Second, it is cheaper. Third, in architectures with compressed K/V (MLA), the rotated part cannot be “absorbed” into the projection matrices, so it's best kept small and separate, the so-called decoupled RoPE (chapter 12).

NeoX or interleaved: who pairs with whom

“Grouping into pairs” hides an implementation choice. In the interleaved style (GPT-J), the pairs are neighboring dimensions \((2i, 2i{+}1)\). In the NeoX style, the rotated block is cut in half and pair \(i\) is \((i,\; i + d_r/2)\), which is the rotate_half of the HF code. The two forms are mathematically equivalent up to a fixed permutation of the dimensions. But the weights \(W_Q, W_K\) were trained with one of them, and serving with the other rotates the wrong pairs and scrambles the position without any runtime error. In vLLM, the choice is the is_neox_style argument of get_rope, and the default is True. Besides the pairs, the figure shows what the swap does to a head that learned to look at a specific token.

DeepSeek-V4 is interleaved: the reference implementation treats consecutive pairs as complex numbers (view_as_complex), and the vLLM overlay calls get_rope(…, is_neox_style=False). GLM's DFlash2 drafter uses the NeoX default, and that is verified: the qwen3_dflash architecture calls get_rope without that argument. Qwen3.8 should also be NeoX, but here it is an inference: the HF code of the Qwen3-Next lineage uses rotate_half and vLLM's get_rope has is_neox_style=True by default, but we did not check this in the recipe's overlay. If you port a new model, this is one of the first fields to check against the reference code.

Trap: “interleaved” means two things

In configs you will find the word in unrelated contexts. is_neox_style=False means pairs of neighboring dimensions. mrope_interleaved: true, in Qwen, means frequencies distributed round-robin among time, height and width (section 6). And indexer_rope_interleave: true, in GLM, refers to the indexer's pair style, but it is harmless, because the indexer inherits rope_dim = qk_rope_head_dim = 0 and the RoPE branch is skipped.

Where the positional dimensions live in the KV cache

Partial RoPE also shows up in the cache format. In DeepSeek-V4, each KV entry (raw in the window or compressed) is a 512-dimensional vector stored in a 584-byte row: 448 B for the NoPE dimensions in FP8 E4M3 (7 blocks of 64), 128 B for the 64 RoPE dimensions in BF16, and 8 B of scales: 7 UE8M0 bytes, one per block of 64 dimensions, each storing only an exponent (the scale is a power of 2), plus 1 byte of padding. The reference code explains why: the NoPE dimensions are quantized to FP8 “to match training”, because the model was trained simulating this quantization (QAT, chapter 20), and the RoPE dimensions “stay in BF16 for positional precision”. Position is encoded in an angle. Storing a rotated pair in FP8 E4M3, with 3 mantissa bits, gets that angle wrong by ~0.5° on average and by up to ~2° in the worst case, versus ~0.03° in BF16 (our math, without per-block scaling, in the figure's readout). What that is worth in tokens depends on the pair's speed. The slowest pair moves 0.0076°/token with θ = 10⁴ and 0.00052°/token with θ = 1.6·10⁵, so half a degree amounts to ≈ 65 tokens of uncertainty in the SWA layers, ≈ 960 in the compressor layers and ≈ 15,000 after YaRN ÷16 (our math).

ArchitectureRoPE dim 64 / NoPE 448 (head_dim 512)

Only 64 of the 512 dimensions of each head rotate, and they are the last ones, with interleaved pairs. That is what explains the 584 B KV row (448 B NoPE FP8 + 128 B RoPE BF16 + 8 B of scales), the same for nvfp4_ds_mla and fp8_ds_mla in this image (chapter 23). Anyone writing a cache kernel has to respect this split: quantizing the 64 RoPE dimensions together with the others would change the numerical regime the model was trained in.

In Qwen, the split doesn't change the cache size. The key is stored whole, already rotated in the first 64 dimensions of each head. That's 13 attention layers with KV (12 QSA + MTP) × 2 KV heads × 256 dimensions × (K + V), which gives 26,624 B/token in BF16 or 13,312 B/token in FP8. This math covers only the main KV: the QSA indexer cache stays in BF16, and that's why the recipe records 1.70× more tokens per GiB with FP8 KV, and not 2×. Partial RoPE changes the meaning of the dimensions, not the bytes.

NoPE: order without rotation

GLM-5.3-Flash takes the idea to the extreme. The 11 MLA layers (layers 3, 7, …, 43, marked in the config as deepseek_sparse_attention, that is, MLA with DSA sparse attention, plus the MTP layer) have qk_rope_head_dim: 0 and mla_use_nope: true. The indexer inherits rope_dim = 0, and the config.json doesn't even have rope_theta. No text attention uses any explicit positional encoding. This is NoPE (No Positional Encoding), the same choice made by Kimi Linear, which also interleaves 3 linear layers for each position-free MLA layer.

If pure attention is a bag of tokens, where does order come from? The figure walks through the four sources GLM has, and the cost this choice created in the recipe.

Putting the pieces together:

  1. The causal mask counts. A token at position \(m\) sees \(m+1\) tokens. A head that spreads its attention uniformly produces an average in which the “weight” of each term is \(1/(m+1)\), and that alone is position information. Published results show that decoders trained without any positional encoding learn position this way (Haviv et al., 2022; Kazemnejad et al., 2023).
  2. Recurrence is ordered by construction. The 34 KDA layers update a state \(S_t = f(S_{t-1}, x_t)\) token by token, with decay (chapter 14). Swapping two tokens changes the state. After the first KDA layers, the vectors in the residual stream already carry the order, and the MLA-NoPE layers read it along with the content.
  3. The short convolution sees neighbors. Each KDA passes q, k and v through a 4-tap causal convolution (short_conv_kernel_size = 4), which distinguishes “the previous token” from “two tokens back”.
  4. The indexer pool has position within the block. The indexer compresses 4 consecutive keys into one, weighting per channel with softmax(gate + ape), and index_kpool_compress_ape [4×128] is a learned bias for each of the 4 positions in the block.

The advantage is both conceptual and practical: with no frequencies, there is nothing to extrapolate. GLM declares max_position_embeddings = 1048576, but that number doesn't size any cos/sin table in the target. It only caps max_model_len, and the recipe needs no YaRN to serve 850,000 tokens. The context limit becomes whatever the recurrent layers and the training can support (interpretation).

Build patchqk_rope_head_dim = 0 → pad with 64 zeros

On SM12x, the only sparse MLA backend is FLASHINFER_MLA_SPARSE_SM120, and it has no kernel for \(d_{qk} = 512\) with top-k 2048 in the fp8_ds_mla layout. FlashInfer instantiates only three geometries: DSV3_2 and GLM_NSA, with \(d_{qk} = 576\) (512 of latent + 64 of RoPE), 656 B and top-k up to 2048, and DSV4, with 512 dimensions and 584 B, but only up to top-k 1024. GLM needs 512 with top-k 2048, which doesn't exist, and the stock image died on the first forward pass with pe_dim must be 64 for fp8_ds_mla. The overlay's way out is to use the 576 GLM_NSA geometry: the Python block in the Dockerfile does q = F.pad(q, (0, 64)) and writes a k_pe of zeros. It is exact: a block of zeros adds 0 to the product \(q\cdot k\), the softmax doesn't change and V comes from the NoPE region. The cost is memory: 656 B/token per DSA layer instead of the ~528 B actually needed (≈ +24% KV in those 11 layers). The details of MLA and of the fp8_ds_mla layout are in chapters 12 and 23.

NoPE isn't free: the Qwen counterexample

The Qwen3.8 tech report shows that full NoPE causes endless generation after post-training, and that's why the model keeps 25% RoPE in the QSA layers even though it is hybrid (36 recurrent GDN layers). NoPE is a training decision validated for a specific model. It is not a switch you flip on the server, and removing RoPE from a model trained with it destroys the model.

Two curiosities complete the GLM picture. The DFlash2 drafter has RoPE (rope_theta 10000, type default, 2,048-token sliding window), applied to the context keys at the token's position, even though the target has none. And the vision tower uses partial 2D RoPE (partial_rotary_factor 0.5), which is the only RoPE in the target model (chapter 17).

mRoPE: positions in three dimensions

Text is a single row and an image is a grid. When Qwen3.8 reads a photo, the visual tokens produced by the merger (chapter 17) have a row and a column. In a video, they also have time. mRoPE (Multimodal RoPE) replaces the scalar position \(m\) with a triple \((t, h, w)\) and splits the frequency pairs among the three axes:

\[ \text{angle of pair } i = p^{\,a(i)}\,\theta_i, \qquad a(i) \in \{T, H, W\}, \qquad \#T = 11,\;\; \#H = 11,\;\; \#W = 10 \]
  • \(p^{T}, p^{H}, p^{W}\): the token's three position coordinates
  • \(a(i)\): the axis that owns pair \(i\) (32 pairs in Qwen)
  • mrope_section = [11, 11, 10]: how many pairs each axis gets (11 + 11 + 10 = 32 pairs = 64 dims)
  • mrope_interleaved = true: the axes alternate across the frequencies (T, H, W, T, H, W, …)

For a text token, the three coordinates are equal (\(t = h = w = m\)) and every pair rotates by \(m\,\theta_i\). That's the plain RoPE from before, and it's why a multimodal model is still a normal LLM as far as text is concerned. For an image token, \(t\) stays fixed and \(h, w\) follow the patch's row and column. So two vertically adjacent patches differ only in the \(H\) pairs, and two horizontally adjacent ones differ only in the \(W\) pairs.

Two things jump out of the figure. First, after the image the text restarts at \(\max(h, w) + 1\), not at “number of patches + 1”: a 3×4-token image consumes 4 positions of the sequence, not 12. This is a consequence of the Qwen-VL family's index scheme, and it eases the position budget in prompts with many images (our reading of the scheme). In the figure, going from a patch to its right-hand neighbor, the hands change only in the \(W\) pairs, and only in the fast ones: the angle difference is \(\theta_i\), and only the pairs with a large \(\theta_i\) move visibly between neighbors. Second, in the blocked layout the T axis would get only the high frequencies and W only the lowest ones. The interleaved version (T on pairs 0, 3, 6, …; H on 1, 4, 7, …; W on 2, 5, 8, …) gives each axis fast, medium and slow pairs, which is the stated motivation for the interleaved mRoPE of the Qwen3-VL lineage.

Deep dive: 2D RoPE inside the vision towers

mRoPE is the position of the visual tokens inside the LLM. Before that, each vision encoder has its own position scheme. Qwen's ViT combines a learned 48×48 absolute position, interpolated to each grid, with 2D axial RoPE. The DeepSeek Vision-Exp ViT uses 2D RoPE with vision_rope_theta 10000, half of the frequencies for the row and half for the column. GLM's uses partial 2D RoPE (partial_rotary_factor 0.5). They are all variations of the same idea: splitting frequency pairs among axes.

There is a practical trap that comes back in section 9. mrope_section and mrope_interleaved live inside the rope_parameters dictionary, together with rope_theta and partial_rotary_factor. An override that replaces that whole dictionary has to repeat all of them.

Beyond training: interpolation, NTK and YaRN

Now the problem can be stated precisely. A model trained with context \(L\) saw, in pair \(i\), angles between 0 and \(L\theta_i\). For the fast pairs, this covers the circle thousands of times. For the slow ones, with \(\lambda_i > L\), it covers only an arc. Serving beyond \(L\) without doing anything is extrapolating: the slow pairs produce combinations of angles the model has never seen, the scores leave the distribution and, in models with RoPE on the whole head, perplexity usually explodes right after \(L\). The measure that organizes everything is how many turns each pair completed during training:

\[ r_i = \frac{L}{\lambda_i} \qquad \begin{cases} r_i > \beta_{\text{fast}} = 32 & \text{fast pair: every angle is familiar} \\ r_i < \beta_{\text{slow}} = 1 & \text{slow pair: never made a full turn} \end{cases} \]
  • \(L\): reference training context (original_max_position_embeddings)
  • \(r_i\): full turns of pair \(i\) in \(L\) tokens
  • \(\beta_{\text{fast}}, \beta_{\text{slow}}\): the YaRN thresholds (32 and 1 in DeepSeek, which are the usual values)

Three ways to stretch

Position interpolation (PI). It divides every position by \(s\), which is equivalent to \(\theta_i \to \theta_i/s\) on all pairs. Everything goes back inside the known arc, but the fast pairs lose resolution: neighbors now differ by \(\theta_0/s\), and local order gets blurred. This is position interpolation, which usually requires fine-tuning.

NTK-aware. Instead of dividing the positions, it changes the base: \(b' = b\cdot s^{d_r/(d_r-2)}\). Pair 0 stays intact and the slowest one is divided by exactly \(s\), with a smooth transition in between. This is NTK-aware scaling. It protects local resolution, but it still nudges all the intermediate pairs a little.

YaRN (Peng et al., 2023). It treats each pair according to \(r_i\) (the “NTK-by-parts” part): fast pairs stay intact, slow pairs are interpolated by \(s\) and the ones in between follow a linear ramp. On top of that, it corrects the “temperature” of attention, because stretching the context spreads the softmax mass over more tokens:

\[ \theta'_i = (1-\gamma_i)\,\frac{\theta_i}{s} + \gamma_i\,\theta_i, \qquad \gamma_i = \operatorname{clamp}\!\left(\frac{r_i - \beta_{\text{slow}}}{\beta_{\text{fast}} - \beta_{\text{slow}}},\,0,\,1\right), \qquad \sqrt{1/t} = 0.1\,\ln s + 1 \]
  • \(s\): the scale factor (factor); the target context is \(s \cdot L\)
  • \(\gamma_i\): 1 = keeps the frequency, 0 = fully interpolates (implementations run the ramp over the pair index, with rounding)
  • \(\sqrt{1/t}\): the mscale, which multiplies cos and sin; since it goes into both q and k, the logits get scaled by \(\text{mscale}^2\)

It's worth doing the mscale math, because it explains a choice made in the recipes. With \(s = 4\), \(\text{mscale} = 0.1\ln 4 + 1 \approx 1.139\) and the logits come out multiplied by \(1.139^2 \approx 1.296\). With \(s = 16\), \(\text{mscale} \approx 1.277\) and the logits come out \(\times 1.63\). Multiplying the logits by a factor greater than 1 is the same as lowering the softmax temperature: the distribution gets “sharper” and attention concentrates again on the few keys that matter, instead of being diluted across the thousands of extra keys a stretched context brings in. The formula \(0.1\ln s + 1\) doesn't come from any derivation: it is an empirical fit from the YaRN paper, measured on LLaMA-family models. So it is a reasonable guess for a model that has never seen long context, but not a law. A model that was trained with position scaling already turned on has learned its own logit calibration, and adding mscale on top would change a temperature that is already right. That is DeepSeek's case, and that's why the reference doesn't apply it and vLLM zeroes mscale and mscale_all_dim for it (our interpretation of the why; the zero is a fact). Static YaRN costs accuracy on short texts

vLLM applies YaRN with the fixed factor to every request. A 2,000-token prompt also gets the compressed slow frequencies, and the model ends up seeing the short text “outside its training regime”. The Qwen card warns that static YaRN can hurt short texts and suggests a smaller factor (2.0) if typical usage is around 512K. The recipe boils this down to one rule: “YaRN exists to extend the context beyond native; at MAX_MODEL_LEN ≤ 262144 it brings no benefit and costs accuracy”. That's why start.sh turns YaRN off on its own below native (section 9).

YaRN per layer type: the DeepSeek case

DeepSeek-V4-Flash mixes three attention types across 43 layers plus 3 draft stages: layers 0 and 1 are sliding-window only; 21 C4A layers and 20 C128A layers add a compressed branch to that same 128-token window; and the three stages of the DSpark drafter (43–45) are also window-only. DSpark is the little model that proposes the next tokens for the trunk to check (the map in section 3; details in chapters 13 and 37). In the reference implementation (inference/model.py), position follows the type:

Layerscompress_ratioAttentionRoPE in the reference
0, 10 (vLLM uses max(1, ·) = 1)Pure SWA, window of 128θ = 10000, no YaRN
2, 4, …, 42 (21)4C4A: sparse top-512 over KV/4 + windowθ = 160000 + YaRN ×16
3, 5, …, 41 (20)128C128A: dense over KV/128 + windowθ = 160000 + YaRN ×16
43, 44, 45 (DSpark)0Drafter SWA: a window over the target layers' features + the block of proposed tokensθ = 10000, no YaRN

Why can the window skip YaRN? Because RoPE is relative. In an SWA layer, the query at position 900,000 only sees the keys from 899,873 to 900,000, and what goes into the score is \(n-m \in [-127, 0]\). Those distances are within training at any point of the context, so there is no extrapolation at all to fix. YaRN there would only bend frequencies the model learned to use unmodified. The compressor layers are different: the compressed entries cover the whole context, distances reach 1M, and the slow pairs need to be interpolated.

In the window, the difference between pure RoPE and YaRN ×16 is at most ~2° at the 127-token edge (our calculation). It is small because, at short distances, the slow pairs barely move with or without scaling. The recipe's documentation says the same: “positions ≤128 sit where the YaRN ramp is nearly the identity”. In the compressor layers without YaRN, pairs 25–31 would go past the known arc, and with YaRN they come back inside it.

The bug: one dictionary for everyone

The vLLM RoPE builder pinned in the Anemll image (build_deepseek_v4_rope, in models/deepseek_v4/common/rope.py) did two problematic things. First, it promoted the checkpoint's type to deepseek_yarn whenever it wasn't "default", and it did so on every layer. The abliterated Vision-Exp checkpoint being served has a “flat” rope_scaling, {type: yarn, factor: 16, original_max_position_embeddings: 65536}, so layers 0–1 and the drafter's 3 layers got YaRN ×16 over a 128-token window. Second, it wrote each layer's rope_theta into the same shared dictionary, config.rope_parameters.

Analogy: the classroom whiteboard

Imagine 46 students copying “their” configuration from a single whiteboard, each one erasing and rewriting θ before copying. Each student copies the right value at their own moment, but anyone who looks at the board afterwards sees the θ of the last student who came by. That is the risk of mutating shared state: the result depends on the construction order, and a bug like this shows up far from where it was born.

The patch patches/hotfix-vllm-rope-swa-fix.py is a source-exact port of the upstream fix vllm#54815. Its core is this:

# simplified excerpt of the patch's REGION_NEW (port of vllm#54815)
rope_parameters = config.rope_parameters
if isinstance(rope_parameters.get("main"), dict) and isinstance(rope_parameters.get("compress"), dict):
    key = "compress" if compress_ratio > 1 else "main"      # newer checkpoints: one dict per type
    rope_parameters = dict(rope_parameters[key])
else:
    rope_parameters = dict(rope_parameters)                   # per-layer copy: doesn't mutate the config
rope_parameters["rope_theta"] = (config.compress_rope_theta if compress_ratio > 1 else config.rope_theta)
if compress_ratio > 1 and rope_parameters["rope_type"] != "default":
    rope_parameters["rope_type"] = "deepseek_yarn"            # YaRN only on the compressor layers
else:
    rope_parameters["rope_type"] = "deepseek_yarn"            # same kernel, but...
    rope_parameters["factor"] = 1.0                            # ...identity scaling = pure RoPE
    rope_parameters["original_max_position_embeddings"] = max_position_embeddings

Note the trick in the else branch. Instead of switching kernels, the window layers stay on the deepseek_yarn path, but with factor = 1.0 and original_max_position_embeddings = max_position_embeddings. With \(s = 1\), the formula for \(\theta'_i\) returns \(\theta_i\): it's pure RoPE through the same code. The compressor layers resolve to parameters byte-for-byte identical to stock (deepseek_yarn, factor 16, θ 160000). The patch is fail-closed: it only accepts the pinned vLLM version (0.25.2.dev0+g752a3a504.d20260714) and the target file with SHA-256 0074271a…, verifies the result 6452ce2e… and publishes via atomic replacement. A CPU suite (scripts/test-rope-swa-fix.py, 16 tests) checks the routing at ratios 1, 4 and 128 with the checkpoint's real values.

Opt-inDSPARK_ENABLE_ROPE_SWA_FIX=0

The default is 0 (stock builder, YaRN on every non-default layer). Only the exact value 1 applies the patch at boot, with --check first on the worker and then on the head. Changing the value requires recreating both ranks, because the overlays are reapplied on every start. Why not turn it on by default, if it is “right”? Because right with respect to the reference doesn't guarantee better with respect to the served checkpoint. The documentation points out that the abliterated checkpoint “may have adapted to the served embedding”, and the effect has not been measured. The agreed gate is a long-context (128K+) quality A/B, with the same seeds, against the control. A note for whoever maintains the recipe: the Stage-C overlay (model.py and dspark.py) has the same “YaRN for everyone” pattern and also mutates config.rope_parameters, and it has not been fixed there.

The general lesson for recipe writers

Position parameters can vary per layer: base, scaling type, rotated fraction, pair style. A “flat” config doesn't tell you that on its own, and the vendor's reference code has the final say. When you port a model, list for each layer type which θ, which scaling and which rope_dim the reference uses, and check them in your engine. A CPU test that calls the builder for each layer type and inspects the result is cheap and catches exactly this bug.

From config to server: what the recipes configure

What's left is to tie everything to the two numbers that show up in every recipe and that look like the same thing but aren't.

max_position_embeddings
A field in config.json: how far the model was designed or trained to go. Qwen 262,144; DeepSeek 1,048,576 (= 65,536 × 16, already counting the checkpoint's YaRN); GLM 1,048,576.
max_model_len
A vLLM argument (--max-model-len): the cap on tokens per request, prompt + output. Qwen 262,144 (1,000,000 with the optional YaRN); DeepSeek 1,048,576; GLM 850,000 (1,000,000 on TP3; 262,144 in the long-coding profile).

At boot, vLLM derives a cap from the config and refuses a larger --max-model-len, unless VLLM_ALLOW_LONG_MAX_MODEL_LEN=1 is in the environment. In that case it only warns that the flag “should be used with extreme caution”. The flag doesn't make the positions valid: it only disables the lock. What makes 1M positions valid in Qwen is YaRN, as the README itself says (“YaRN is what makes the 1M positions valid”).

  • VLLM_ALLOW_LONG_MAX_MODEL_LEN=1 in .env.sample (“required when MAX_MODEL_LEN > 262144”), passed to the container on both nodes. It stays on even with YaRN off, which is harmless but confusing, as the recipe's report notes.
  • VLLM_ALLOW_LONG_MAX_MODEL_LEN=1 in the .env and as the compose default. Here the config already declares 1,048,576, so asking for 1M doesn't exceed the advertised cap. Our reading is that the flag works as a safeguard against a more conservative derivation from some other config field (interpretation). There is a way to tighten that reading, which remains something to verify. In the vLLM code we know, _get_and_verify_max_len starts from max_position_embeddings and, when there is a rope_type: yarn, replaces the cap with original_max_position_embeddings × factor. If the pinned version does this, DS's derived cap is already 65,536 × 16 = 1,048,576 and the flag is redundant. In Qwen with the nested override, the cap would become 262,144 × 4 = 1,048,576 and the flag would no longer be needed to serve 1,000,000. Check in the version you use: v0.30 changed the way YaRN aliases rescale max_position_embeddings.
  • 850,000 < 1,048,576 and NoPE: neither flag nor YaRN.

“A cap, not a reservation”, but the cap sizes things

vLLM doesn't reserve max_model_len tokens of KV per request: it allocates blocks on demand, and what actually limits you is whether the sum of live tokens fits in the pool (chapter 31). Even so, max_model_len sizes per-step structures, even with short prompts. In Qwen, the QSA scorer allocates an FP32 buffer per layer whose width is proportional to the page table: at 1M, with ratio 4, that's ~250,000 columns and ~4 MB per verification per layer, and the recipe recommends 262,144 for agentic use, with an expected gain of 2–5%. In GLM, the stock indexer workspace is max_model_len × 40 entries of 132 B, about 5,036 MiB locked up at 1M, which motivated the rightsize mode. In DeepSeek, the C128A top-k capacity is ceil(ceil(max_model_len/128)/128)·128, or 8,192 entries at 1M. A bigger context “just in case” doesn't come for free.

The override that never arrived: text_config

In Qwen, YaRN is not in the checkpoint. It is injected at boot through --hf-overrides, which merges a JSON into the HF config without editing the cache (chapter 24). Qwen3.8 is multimodal: the top-level config (qwen4_exp) contains a text_config (the LLM) and a vision_config (the ViT), and the text model reads its position settings from text_config.rope_parameters. The detail that proved costly: the parent config also exposes a rope_parameters, as a plain dictionary.

The mechanism, in the recipe's own words: ModelConfig._apply_dict_overrides only recurses into keys that are nested configs. Since the parent of qwen4_exp exposes rope_parameters as a plain dictionary, the top-level override {"rope_parameters": …} was applied by setattr on the parent and never reached text_config. Verified inside the image: after the override, text_config.rope_parameters.rope_type was still "default". Every earlier “1M” run served 1M positions with unscaled RoPE. The fix generates a single nested payload:

{"text_config": {"ple_embedding_dtype": "float8_e4m3fn",
                 "rope_parameters": {"rope_type": "yarn", "factor": 4.0,
                                     "original_max_position_embeddings": 262144}}}

In practice, start.sh builds this JSON in Python and only includes rope_parameters when YARN_ENABLE=true. Since YaRN started working “for the first time”, the recipe downgraded its defaults: MAX_MODEL_LEN 1000000 → 262144, YARN_ENABLE true → false, and 1M flagged as not validated. The README table still shows true, but .env.sample is what counts. And the README's “Default runtime” block (lines 430–451) still shows the old override, --hf-overrides '{"rope_parameters":…}' at the top level: don't copy the command from there, because it is exactly the form that turns into a no-op. A curious data point, labeled as interpretation: the old measurements at 600K, taken while YaRN was a no-op, found 6/6 needles, including at 95% depth. A needle test doesn't prove general quality, but it suggests that θ = 10⁷ with 75% NoPE dimensions degrades more gracefully than extrapolation theory would lead you to fear.

To verify: the whole dictionary

The start.sh payload sends only rope_type, factor and original_max_position_embeddings. By the same logic of _apply_dict_overrides, rope_parameters is a plain dictionary inside text_config and could be replaced wholesale, losing rope_theta 1e7, partial_rotary_factor 0.25, mrope_section [11,11,10] and mrope_interleaved true. The official card passes the complete dictionary. If you recreate the recipe, use the complete dictionary and check in the boot log that text_config.rope_parameters ended up with what you asked for. Make this check a fixed step of your boot.

Three pieces of shell close the loop. The YaRN guard below native (start.sh:164–170) forces YARN_ENABLE=false and prints NOTE: MAX_MODEL_LEN=… <= native 262144 — YaRN force-disabled. when MAX_MODEL_LEN ≤ 262144. OVERRIDE_YARN_ENABLE overrides the value after .env is read, and that is how start-fp8.sh forces false on the FP8 checkpoint (chapter 43). And YARN_FACTOR=4.0 gives 262,144 × 4 = 1,048,576 ≈ 1M.
